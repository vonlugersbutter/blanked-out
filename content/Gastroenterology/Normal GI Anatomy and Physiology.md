# Overview of the GI system
- Gastrointestinal (GI) Tract (Alimentary Canal): A continuous tube extending from the mouth to the anus. Organs include the mouth, most of the pharynx, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, and large intestine
- Accessory Digestive Organs: Include the teeth, tongue, salivary glands, liver, gallbladder, and pancreas
	- Don't come into contact with food, except for teeth and tongue of course
- Ingestion, secretion, motility, digestion, absorption, defecation
# Overview of metabolism
## Mechanisms of ATP generation
- Substrate-level phosphorylation = transfer of a high-energy phosphate group from an intermediate compound (substrate) directly to ADP
	- Occurs in cytosol
- Oxidative phosphorylation = removal of electrons from organic compounds, passing them through an electron transport chain to oxygen molecules ($O_2$). Occurs in the inner mitochondrial membrane
## Glucose
- Glucose is used for
	- ATP production (oxidation)
	- Amino acid synthesis
	- Glycogenesis (glycogen synthesis) = stored in hepatocytes (liver cells) and muscle fibers
	- Triglyceride synthesis = converted to fatty acids for lipogenesis and storage in adipose cells when glycogen stores are full
- Glycogenesis = glucose to glycogen synthesis, stimulated by insulin
- Glycogenolysis = splitting glycogen into glucose subunits, stimulated by glucagon and epinephrine. Hepatocytes can release this glucose into the blood; skeletal muscle fibers cannot (they lack the necessary enzyme, phosphatase)
- Gluconeogenesis = formation of new glucose from noncarbohydrate sources (glycerol from triglycerides, lactic acid, and certain amino acids). Stimulated by cortisol and glucagon
## Lipids
### Formation
- Chylomicrons = formed in the gastrointestinal tract during fat absorption, carrying dietary fat and cholesterol.
	- They consist of triglycerides, cholesterol, phospholipids, and apolipoproteins
	- Lipoprotein lipase (LPL) = enzyme synthesized by adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, and heart tissue, hydrolyzes the chylomicron triglycerides on the capillary endothelial cells, releasing fatty acids and glycerol
- The released fatty acids diffuse into the cells (fat or muscle) to be used for fuel or synthesized back into triglycerides
- Chylomicron remnants, which are cholesterol-enriched, are rapidly cleared by the liver after binding to receptors on liver endothelial cells (a process mediated partly by Apolipoprotein-E)
### Transport
- Lipids are transported in the blood as spherical lipoproteins
	- Apoproteins on the outer shell help solubilize the lipoprotein and have specific functions.
	- Chylomicrons: Transport dietary lipids (triglycerides) from the GI tract to adipose tissue via the lymph (lacteals)
	- VLDLs (Very-Low-Density Lipoproteins): Transport endogenous triglycerides (from the liver) to adipocytes
	- LDLs (Low-Density Lipoproteins): Deliver cholesterol to body cells (known as "bad" cholesterol; high levels increase coronary artery disease risk)
	- HDLs (High-Density Lipoproteins): Remove excess cholesterol from cells and transport it to the liver for elimination (known as "good" cholesterol; high levels decrease coronary artery disease risk)
### Storage 
- Stored as triglycerides in adipose tissue
### Processing
- Lipolysis
	- Triglycerides are split into glycerol and fatty acids, catalyzed by lipases. Enhanced by epinephrine, norepinephrine, and cortisol; inhibited by insulin
	- Glycerol: Can be converted to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate, which can enter glycolysis or gluconeogenesis
	- Fatty Acids: Undergo beta oxidation in mitochondrial matrix, removing 2-carbon fragments to form acetyl CoA, which enters the Krebs cycle
		- Fatty acids ionize in the plasma and combine immediately with albumin molecules; these are called free fatty acids (FFA) or non-esterified fatty acids.
		- FFA concentrations are low, but their turnover is rapid (half the plasma FFA is replaced every 2 to 3 minutes), allowing them to fulfill almost all the body's normal energy requirements
		- FFA concentration increases significantly (fivefold to eightfold) in states of high fat utilization, such as starvation and diabetes mellitus, when little energy is derived from carbohydrates
- Ketogenesis
	- In hepatocytes, two acetyl CoA molecules condense to form ketone bodies (acetoacetic acid, $\beta$-hydroxybutyric acid, acetone)
	- Ketone bodies leave the liver and are used as fuel (acetyl CoA) by other tissues, notably the heart and kidneys
	- Excessive production during high fat catabolism (e.g., fasting, starvation, uncontrolled diabetes) leads to ketosis
- Lipogenesis
	- Made from glucose or amino acids, occurs in liver and adipose cells
	- Stimulated by insulin = promotes glucose entry into the cells, which provides both the acetyl-CoA/NADPH needed for fatty acid chain production and the essential $\alpha$-glycerophosphate required to form the glycerol backbone of the triglyceride molecule
	- Carbohydrates are converted to acetyl-CoA via the glycolytic system.
	- Acetyl-CoA is converted into fatty acids using malonyl-CoA and NADPH primarily supplied by the pentose phosphate pathway
	- Fatty acids combine with $\alpha$ -glycerophosphate (a product of glucose degradation) to form triglycerides
### Regulation 
- Fat-Sparing Effect of Carbohydrates: Carbohydrates are preferentially used for energy when available.
	- The availability of excess carbohydrates increases $\alpha$ -glycerophosphate production, which binds free fatty acids into stored triglycerides, thus limiting the free fatty acids available for energy use.
	- Excess carbohydrates also increase intermediates of the citric acid cycle, which accelerate the enzyme acetyl-CoA carboxylase, the rate-limiting step in fatty acid synthesis.
- Hormonal mobilization
	- Epi and NE = released during sympathetic stimulation (e.g., heavy exercise). These hormones directly activate hormone-sensitive triglyceride lipase in fat cells, causing rapid breakdown and mobilization of fatty acids into the blood for energy use
	- ACTH and Glucocorticoids: Secreted during stress, they activate the same or a similar hormone-sensitive lipase, enhancing fatty acid removal from fat tissues. Excess secretion of these hormones can lead to ketosis (a ketogenic effect)
	- Growth hormone = mild ketogenic effect by activating hormone-sensitive lipase
	- Thyroid hormone = increases the overall rate of energy metabolism, indirectly causing fat mobilization if carbohydrate and fat stores are insufficient

# Layers of the GI tract
- Given from deep to superficial
## Mucosa
- Nonkeratinized stratified squamous in the mouth, pharynx, esophagus, and anal canal for protection
- Stomach and intestines are lined by simple columnar epithelium for secretion and absorption
- Lamina propria is areolar connective tissue containing blood/lymph vessels and large concentrations of MALT (mucosa-associated lymphatic tissue) for disease protection
- Muscularis mucosa is a thin layer of smooth muscle that creates folds in the stomach and small intestine to increase surface area
## Submucosa
- Areolar connective tissue binding the mucosa to the muscularis, containing blood/lymph vessels that receive absorbed food, and the submucosal plexus of neurons 
- Submucosal (Meissner's) plexus = involved in secretion 
## Muscularis
- Generally consists of an inner sheet of circular smooth muscle and an outer sheet of longitudinal smooth muscle
- Skeletal muscle is present in the mouth, pharynx, superior/middle esophagus, and the external anal sphincter, permitting voluntary control of swallowing and defecation
- Involuntary contractions of smooth muscle mix food and propel it along the tract
- Myenteric plexus (Auerbach's) of neurons is located between the circular and longitudinal muscle layers
## Serosa or Adventitia
- Serosa: Found in portions suspended in the abdominal cavity; composed of simple squamous epithelium and areolar connective tissue; also called the visceral peritoneum
- Adventitia: Found in the esophagus instead of serosa; composed only of areolar connective tissue that attaches the organ to surrounding structures
# Innervation
## ENS
- Myenteric and submucosal plexus, as mentioned
- Sensory neurons (chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors/stretch receptors) in the mucosal epithelium detect chemical stimuli and stretching
- Acetylcholine = primary excitatory transmitter for both motility and secretion; acts on muscarinic receptors
- Nitric oxide and VIP (vasoactive intestinal peptide) = primary inhibitory transmitters; essential for relaxing sphincters (LES, Pylorus) and producing "caudad relaxation" during peristalsis
- Serotonin (5-HT) = released from enterochromaffin cells; critical for interneuron communication and initiating motility reflexes
## ANS
- Parasympathetic Nerves (Vagus/Pelvic Splanchnic): Generally increase GI secretion and motility by increasing ENS activity
- Sympathetic Nerves: Generally decrease GI secretion and motility by inhibiting ENS neurons. Emotional states like anger or fear can slow digestion via sympathetic stimulation
# Peritoneum
- Largest serous membrane, divided into the parietal peritoneum (lining the cavity wall) and the visceral peritoneum (covering organs, serving as the serosa)
- Retroperitoneal organs are located behind the peritoneum and covered only on their anterior surfaces
	- Some are secondarily retroperitoneal = start inside the peritoneal cavity but later fuse to the posterior abdominal wall during development, becoming fixed behind the peritoneum
	- Mnemonic: SAID PUCKER = suprarenal (adrenal) glands, aorta, IVC, duodenum (later parts), pancreas (head, neck body), colon (ascending and descending), kidneys, esophagus, rectum 
# Phases of digestion
- Cephalic phase = thought, smell, sight, or taste of food; prepares the mouth and stomach for eating by stimulating salivary and gastric gland secretion
- Gastric phase = food enters the stomach; promotes gastric secretion and motility
	- Neural regulation through stretch and chemoreceptors, increasing peristalsis and juices
	- Hormonal regulation through gastrin → gastric glands 
- Intestinal phase = food enters the small intestine; primarily has inhibitory effects that slow gastric emptying (to prevent duodenal overload) and promote continued digestion in the small intestine
	- Neural regulation = enterogastric reflex = triggered by duodenal distension, inhibits gastric motility and decreases gastric emptying
# Food intake control
- Ghrelin (from the stomach) stimulates hunger via the hypothalamus, while CCK and PYY (from the intestine) signal satiety
- Mechanisms like the "ileal brake" (triggered by fat in the ileum) slow gastric emptying to ensure the proximal intestine is not overwhelmed
# Oral cavity
- Lips and cheeks = contain muscles (orbicularis oris and buccinator) that keep food between the teeth during chewing (mastication)
	- Masseter, temporalis, medial pterygoid, lateral pterygoid all from motor fibers of mandibular nerve (branch of trigeminal CN V)

- Tongue = skeletal muscle forming the floor of the oral cavity; maneuvers food into a soft, flexible mass called a bolus for swallowing
	- The dorsum contains papillae, some housing taste buds.
	- Lingual glands secrete lingual lipase, an enzyme that begins triglyceride digestion, though it is activated later in the acidic environment of the stomach
- Teeth (dentes) = accessory digestive organs anchored by the periodontal ligament
	- The internal structure is primarily dentin (calcified connective tissue)
	- The crown is covered by enamel, the hardest substance in the body
	- Humans have two sets: 20 deciduous (primary) teeth and 32 permanent (secondary) teeth
	- Incisors cut, canines tear/shred, and molars/premolars crush and grind food
## Salivary glands
- Parasympathetic innervation promotes secretion
- Parotid glands = serous (watery) fluid
	- Salivary amylase, some kallikrein (family of serine proteases)
- Submandibular glands = mixed serous and mucus 
	- Salivary amylase, also kallikrein, lysozyme, lactoperoxidase (antimicrobial), and lactoferrin (also antimicrobial)
- Sublingual glands = thicker mucus-rich, minimal amylase
	- Lingual lipase, other stuff
# Pharynx and Deglutition
- Pharynx = muscular contractions propel the bolus to the esophagus
- Deglutition (Swallowing) = moves the bolus from the mouth to the stomach in three stages:
	- Voluntary stage = bolus is moved to the oropharynx
	- Pharyngeal Stage (involuntary) = soft palate/uvula close off the nasopharynx, the epiglottis seals the larynx, and the bolus enters the esophagus
	- Esophageal Stage (involuntary) = the bolus is pushed toward the stomach via peristalsis (coordinated contractions/relaxations of the muscularis)
- Oropharyngeal stages
	- Tongue moves the bolus backward; the soft palate elevates to close the nasopharynx
	- Upper pharyngeal muscle contracts, forcing the bolus into the pharyngeal channel
	- Distal pharynx relaxes, the UES opens, and the glottis closes the trachea to protect the airway
# Esophagus
-  Upper 1/3 is striated muscle, the middle 1/3 is mixed, and the distal 1/3 is exclusively smooth muscle
- Contains the upper esophageal sphincter (UES) (skeletal muscle) and the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) (smooth muscle) 
	- 1st constrictor: UES has cricopharyngeal muscle
	- 2nd constrictor: crossing of aortic arch and left main bronchus
	- 3rd constrictor: esophageal hiatus of diaphragm, aided by LES
	- Opening of LES is done by NO and VIP 
- The superficial layer is the adventitia, not serosa
- Dimensions: The esophagus is a muscular tube approximately 18–25 cm long in adults
- Striated muscle control = regulated by the CNS via somatic nerves originating in the nucleus retrofacialis and nucleus ambiguus
	- These nerves release ACh, which acts directly on nicotinic receptors at motor endplates
- Smooth muscle control = regulated by CN X and ENS 
	- Dorsal vagal complex coordinates autonomic outflow 
	- Inhibitor pathways use NO and VIP to relax lower areas ahead of LES opening
	- ACh to contract above the bolus
- Movement into stomach is done by positive pressure of peristaltic wave and negative pressure within gastric cavity 
- Achalasia involves the LES not relaxing, makes a blockage → dysphagia
## Forms of peristalsis
- Primary peristalsis = propulsive wave
- Secondary peristalsis
	- Independently of a swallow and is restricted to the smooth muscle portion
	- Initiated by luminal distension (retained bolus) or luminal acidity (gastric reflux)
	- Housekeeping mechanism 
## Vomiting
- Esophagus acts as a passive conduit rather than using active propulsive motility
- Involves coordinated contraction of thoracic, diaphragmatic, and abdominal muscles against a closed glottis (retching), followed by LES relaxation and expulsion
- Nausea triggers increased salivary secretion; the resulting bicarbonate helps protect the esophageal mucosa from acid injury during reflux or emesis
# Stomach
- Includes rugae (macroscopic folds) and gastric pits leading to deep gastric glands (oxyntic or antral)
- Gastroparesis = delayed gastric emptying affects esophageal health; management includes small, liquid-heavy, low-fat meals to avoid excessive CCK-mediated feedback inhibition of motility
- Divided into the cardia, fundus, body, and pyloric part. The pyloric part connects to the duodenum via the pyloric sphincter. The mucosa forms large folds called rugae when the stomach is empty
- Begins protein digestion (by pepsin) and continues lipid digestion (by lingual/gastric lipase)
- Stomach absorbs small quantities of water, ions, short-chain fatty acids, certain drugs (aspirin), and alcohol
- Stomach does initiate protein and lipid digestion, but these are not necessary for survival and can be done later (so a gastrectomy isn't going to be a problem)
- Gastric secretion is essential for vit B12 via intrinsic factor → absorption of dietary non-heme iron 
- Gastric acid partially sterilizes meal by killing a lot of microorganisms
- Feedback from the small intestine (fat, acid, hypertonicity) triggers GIP and CCK to further inhibit gastric secretion
- Feedback Regulation → lead to more acid secretion and makes it more likely for bacteria to persist, make [Peptic Ulcer Disease](Gastroenterology/GI%20Pathophysiology/Peptic%20Ulcer%20Disease.md) more likely 
	- Enterogastric reflexes = distension, acidity, hypertonicity, or fat in the duodenum triggers reflexes to slow gastric emptying
	- "Ileal Brake" = fat or glucose in the ileum signals that the proximal intestine is overwhelmed, retarding further gastric exit
	- CCK released in response to lipids; delays gastric emptying by binding to CCK1 receptors on vagal sensory afferents
## Proximal
- Aka cardia, fundus, body = reservoir. The fundus/body contains ~75% of gastric glands (oxyntic glands) which secrete acid and intrinsic factor (parietal cells) and pepsinogen (chief cells)
## Distal 
- Aka antrum = mixing and grinding. Glands here contain G cells that secrete gastrin
## Musculature
- The muscularis has three layers (longitudinal, circular, and inner oblique)
- Churning results from mixing waves (peristalsis) which mix contents into chyme. Retropulsion forces large particles back into the stomach body
- Retropulsion = as waves move faster than the bolus, gastric contents are forced backward into the body, effectively mixing and grinding particles against a closed pylorus
- Migrating motor complex (MMC) = stereotypical motility cycles lasting ~100 minutes that sweep from the stomach to the ileum
	- Phase I: Quiescence (40–60% of cycle)
	- Phase II: Irregular, non-propulsive contractions (20–30% of cycle)
	- Phase III: Intense, occlusive contractions (5–10 minutes) that sweep the stomach clean
	- Strongly correlated with cyclical plasma motilin levels; the pylorus relaxes fully during Phase III to allow the passage of large, indigestible residues
- Empties fast with liquids, and fats leave the slowest to allow for emulsification 
## Innervation
- PNS is CN X, SNS is splanchnic 
- Basal electrical rhythm (BER) = rhythmic waves of depolarization originating from a pacemaker in the greater curvature of the gastric body.
- Interstitial cells of Cajal = underlying network responsible for pacemaker activity; however, smooth muscle contraction only occurs when neural/hormonal stimuli reach a threshold on top of these waves

## Gastric glands 
- Epithelial cells form gastric glands which secrete gastric juice into gastric pits
	- Parietal cells secrete HCl (kills microbes, denatures proteins, converts pepsinogen to pepsin) and intrinsic factor (aids B12 absorption) (see [[Anemia]] for a discussion on pernicious anemia, where parietal cells are targeted)
		- Intrinsic factor = protein secreted in response to the same stimuli as acid; it is resistant to acid and pepsin and is required for distal B12 absorption
		- Acid secretion
			- H+, K+ ATPase = sequestration of these pumps in tubulovesicles at rest keeps them inactive; stimulation moves them to the apical membrane to pump protons into the lumen
			- Protons are generated by carbonic anhydrase II
			- HCO3- is exported via a basolateral Cl-/HCO3- exchanger, leading to the "alkaline tide" after a meal
			- HCl is formed as Cl- follows H+ into the lumen via apical channels
		- These cells have a CCK B receptor on them 
	- Chief cells secrete inactive pepsinogen and gastric lipase
		- Pepsinogen = an inactive proenzyme auto-catalytically cleaved into active pepsin at low pH (optimum activity < pH 3.0) to initiate protein digestion
	- G cells (enteroendocrine cells) secrete the hormone gastrin
		- Gastrin = hormone that stimulates gastric glands to secrete gastric juice, increases stomach motility, and relaxes the pyloric sphincter
	- Surface mucus cells 
		- Mucus and bicarbonate = form a viscoelastic gel layer stabilized by trefoil peptides and cross-linked by disulfide bonds
	- ECL cells 
		- Histamine
	- D cells
		- Somatostatin = primary inhibitor 
		- pH drops below 3, somatostatin released to suppress gastrin  
	- Nerves
		- ACh (inhibits D cells)
		- Gastrin-releasing peptide (stimulates G cells)
## Drug interactions
- NSAIDs predispose to injury by inhibiting prostaglandins, which are vital mucus and bicarbonate secretion activators 
- Macrolides are associated with pyloric stenosis = acting as motilin agonists, stimulating excessive, prolonged contractions and hypertrophy (thickening) of the pyloric muscle, obstructing food passage
	- Increases risk for [Infantile Hypertrophic Pyloric Stenosis](Gastroenterology/GI%20Pathophysiology/Infantile%20Hypertrophic%20Pyloric%20Stenosis.md)
# Pancreas
- A retroperitoneal gland (except the tail) lying posterior to the stomach's greater curvature
- Acinar cells (exocrine portion) secrete pancreatic juice into the duodenum via the pancreatic duct
- Pancreatic juice is alkaline (pH 7.1–8.2) due to sodium bicarbonate, which buffers acidic chyme from the stomach and creates the proper pH for intestinal enzymes
- Pancreatic juice contains enzymes that digest starches (pancreatic amylase), proteins (trypsin, chymotrypsin, carboxypeptidase, elastase), triglycerides (pancreatic lipase), and nucleic acids (ribonuclease, deoxyribonuclease)
- Protein-digesting enzymes are secreted in inactive forms (e.g., trypsinogen) to prevent digestion of pancreatic cells
- The pancreatic duct joins the common bile duct to form the hepatopancreatic ampulla (Ampulla of Vater), which enters the duodenum
# Liver
- Body's heaviest gland, divided into right and left lobes by the falciform ligament
- Hepatocytes (liver cells) are the major functional cells; they secrete bile (800–1000 mL/day)
- Bile flows through bile canaliculi and ducts, eventually forming the common bile duct that leads to the duodenum
- Bile salts in bile are essential for the emulsification (breakdown into small globules) and absorption of dietary lipids
- Stellate reticuloendothelial cells (Kupffer cells) in the sinusoids are phagocytes that destroy worn-out blood cells and bacteria
- Major liver functions 
	- Bile secretion
	- Maintaining normal blood glucose levels (glycogenesis, glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis)
	- Lipid and protein metabolism (e.g., producing plasma proteins, converting ammonia to urea)
	- Excretion of bilirubin and processing of drugs/hormones
## Blood supply
- Portal circulation = unlike other organs, venous blood from the intestine flows first to the liver via the portal vein. This allows for first-pass metabolism and detoxification of absorbed substances by hepatocytes and immune regulation by Kupffer cells
- Blood flows through hepatic sinusoids, which receive oxygenated blood via the hepatic artery and nutrient-rich blood via the hepatic portal vein
# Gallbladder
- Sac that stores and concentrates bile produced by the liver until needed in the duodenum
# Small intestines
- Surface area is massively amplified by villi (finger-like projections) and crypts (invaginations) to facilitate absorption
	- Villi has lacteal (lymph capillary) for lipid absorption and blood capillaries
	- Microvilli (brush border) = microscopic projections on absorptive cell surfaces that house brush-border enzymes (like lactase and peptidase)
- Circular folds = permanent ridges of mucosa and submucosa that cause chyme to spiral
- Intestinal juice (water and mucus) is secreted by intestinal glands (crypts of Lieberkühn)
- Duodenal (Brunner’s) glands in the duodenum secrete bicarb to neutralize stomach acid
- Mechanical = Involves segmentations (localized mixing contractions) and migrating motility complexes (MMC) (peristalsis that propels chyme toward the large intestine)
- Absorption = absorbs about 90% of water and nutrients
	- Monosaccharides and amino acids enter the blood capillaries of the villus
	- Lipids = bile salts form micelles to ferry large fatty acids and monoglycerides to absorptive cells. Inside the cells, they are packaged into large droplets called chylomicrons, which enter the lacteals and travel via the lymphatic system to the blood
	- Water absorption = osmosis 
## Duodenum
- Duodenal cluster unit = comprising the proximal duodenum, pancreas, and biliary system, this unit acts as a critical regulator of digestion, monitoring luminal contents (chemo/mechanosensing)
- Sphincter of Oddi = regulates the flow of biliary and pancreatic secretions into the duodenum
- CCK = released by I cells in the duodenum/jejunum in response to fatty acids and amino acids; coordinates the "duodenal cluster unit" by contracting the gallbladder, relaxing the sphincter of Oddi, and signaling satiety to the brain via vagal afferents
- Secretin = released by S cells in the duodenum when luminal pH < 4.5; stimulates pancreatic and biliary bicarbonate secretion to neutralize acid
- Glucose-dependent Insulinotropic Peptide (GIP) = released by K cells in the duodenum/jejunum; inhibits gastric acid secretion and enhances insulin release from the pancreas in response to luminal glucose/fat
- Motilin = released by M cells in the duodenum/jejunum cyclically during the fasting state; the primary initiator of the Migrating Motor Complex (MMC), which sweeps the gut clean between meals

# Large intestines
- Contains only crypts interspersed with surface epithelium; haustra (folds) and a larger diameter support its reservoir function
- Reservoir for waste and a site for fluid reclamation and salvage of dietary by-products (e.g., short-chain fatty acids) via the commensal microbiome
- Regions = cecum, colon (ascending, transverse, descending, sigmoid), rectum, and anal canal
- Muscularis features bands of thickened longitudinal muscle called teniae coli, whose tonic contractions create pouches called haustra
	- Haustral churning, peristalsis, and mass peristalsis (a strong wave that forces contents into the rectum, typically after a meal)
- Mucosa lacks villi and circular folds but has numerous goblet cells that secrete mucus
- No enzymes, chemical digestion by bacteria
	- Bacteria ferment carbohydrates (releasing flatus), break down amino acids, decompose bilirubin into stercobilin (colouring the feces brown), and synthesize some B vitamins and vitamin K
- Absorption = water (solidifying feces), ions, and vitamins
- Defecation = elimination of feces, typically initiated by a defecation reflex when mass peristalsis pushes feces into the rectum and distends the wall
	- Opens the involuntary internal anal sphincter, while the external anal sphincter is under voluntary control
