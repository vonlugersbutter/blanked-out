# Definition

# Etiology
- Plasma glucose concentrations are maintained within a narrow range 
	- ~81 mg/dl (45 mmol/l) = endogenous insulin secretion is suppressed
	- ~67 mg/dl (37 mmol/l) = stimulation of glucagon, epinephrine, and GH occurs
	- ~60 mg/dl (33 mmol/l) = autonomic symptoms (tremor, anxiety, palpitations, sweating, and hunger) appear, and cortisol is stimulated
	- ~50 mg/dl (28 mmol/l) = cognitive dysfunction and cerebral neuroglycopenia (confusion, weakness, blurred vision) begin
	- ~30 mg/dl (16 mmol/l) = profound hypoglycemia can lead to coma and convulsions
- Mechanisms of maintaining euglycemia
	- Postabsorptive state (4 to 6 hours after a meal), glucose must be produced endogenously to meet the needs of the brain (~125 mg/min) and red blood cells (~25 mg/min)
	- Liver = initially provides glucose via glycogenolysis (breakdown of stored glycogen) as glycogen stores deplete after several hours, gluconeogenesis begins, utilizing substrates like alanine (from muscle), lactate (from blood cells), and glycerol (from fat)
	- Kidney = while its net contribution is minimal after an overnight fast, it can contribute 20% to 25% of endogenous glucose production after 60 hours of fasting
		- Lacks glycogen stores and relies solely on gluconeogenesis, primarily using glutamine as a substrate
	- During prolonged fasting, the oxidation of free fatty acids provides ketone bodies (acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate), which serve as alternative fuels for the CNS
- Fasting hypoglycemia
	- With hyperinsulinism = includes pancreatic β-cell tumors (insulinoma), iatrogenic administration of insulin or sulfonylureas, and autoimmune disorders
	- Without hyperinsulinism = includes severe hepatic dysfunction, chronic renal insufficiency, inanition, hypercortisolism, and nonislet cell tumor hypoglycemia (NICTH)
	- Non-fasting (postprandial) hypoglycemia = includes late dumping syndrome after gastric surgery, non-insulinoma pancreatogenous hypoglycemia syndrome (NIPHS), and occult diabetes
## Adult disorders
- Iatrogenic [Diabetes](14%20Endocrinology/Endocrine%20Pathologies/Diabetes/Diabetes.md) = common in type 1 and insulin-treated type 2 patients
	- Severe cases can result in "dead-in-bed" syndrome or permanent neurological damage, though the latter is rare in adults
- Factitious hypoglycemia = self-induced hypoglycemia is most common in health professionals or relatives of diabetic patients a combination of low glucose, high insulin, and suppressed c-peptide (ratio of insulin/c-peptide > 10) is pathognomonic for exogenous insulin use
- Drug-induced = fluoroquinolones (eg, gatifloxacin) cause initial hypoglycemia
	- Pentamidine is cytotoxic to β cells, causing acute insulin release
	- Alcohol inhibits gluconeogenesis by increasing the NADH to NAD+ ratio, leading to hypoglycemia once hepatic glycogen is depleted
- Autoimmune hypoglycemia = high titers of insulin autoantibodies can paradoxically cause hypoglycemia 3 to 4 hours post-meal as free insulin is released from immune complexes
	- Rare insulin receptor autoantibodies can also cause severe fasting or postprandial hypoglycemia
## Pancreatic β-cell tumors (insulinoma)
- Insulinomas are the most common cause of spontaneous fasting hypoglycemia in healthy adults
- Diagnosis = the supervised 72-hour fast is the diagnostic standard the fast is terminated if laboratory glucose is <45 mg/dl and the patient is symptomatic
- Diagnostic criteria = positive findings include plasma insulin ≥3 μu/ml, c-peptide ≥200 pmol/l, proinsulin ≥5 pmol/l, and β-hydroxybutyrate ≤27 nmol/l, with a negative sulfonylurea screen
- Localization = helical CT and MRI identify most tumors, but endoscopic ultrasound is preferred if imaging is negative the selective arterial calcium stimulation test can regionalize the source of hyperinsulinism to specific parts of the pancreas
- Treatment = surgical resection is the treatment of choice
	- Diazoxide is used preoperatively to inhibit insulin secretion by opening K<sub>ATP</sub> channels
## Nonislet cell tumor hypoglycemia (NICTH)
- Often caused by large mesenchymal tumors that release incompletely processed insulin-like growth factor II (big IGF-II) 
- The "big IGF-II" binds to insulin receptors in muscle and liver, increasing glucose transport and reducing glucose output
- Hypoglycemia with suppressed insulin (<5 μu/ml) and decreased GH and IGF-I levels
## Postprandial issues
- Gastric surgery (late dumping syndrome) = rapid delivery of carbohydrates to the small bowel causes a hyper-insulinemic response it is increasingly seen after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass
- NIPHS (non-insulinoma pancreatogenous hypoglycemia syndrome) = characterized by hyper-insulinemic hypoglycemia due to diffuse islet hyperplasia unlike insulinoma, it typically features a negative 72-hour fast and a positive calcium stimulation test in multiple arteries
## Pediatric hypoglycemia
- Hypoglycemia in children requires prompt diagnosis to prevent brain injury
- Transient forms = includes transitional neonatal hypoglycemia (incomplete suppression of insulin after birth) and infants of diabetic mothers (IDM)
- Congenital hyperinsulinism (CHI) = most common cause of persistent pediatric hypoglycemia
- Channel defects = mutations in ABCC8 (SUR1) or KCNJ11 (KIR62) genes lead to non-functional K<sub>ATP</sub> channels and inappropriate insulin secretion
- Metabolic defects = activating mutations of glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) cause hyperinsulinism-hyperammonemia syndrome
- Diagnosis and treatment = diagnosis relies on a "critical sample" taken during hypoglycemia diazoxide is the first-line medical therapy, though channel defects often respond poorly near-total pancreatectomy may be required for diffuse CHI

# Clinical features
- Hypoglycemia is clinically classified to guide diagnosis, often centered around Whipple’s triad
	- History of hypoglycemic symptoms, associated low plasma glucose, and immediate relief upon ingesting carbohydrates
