# Mechanisms of ATP generation
- Substrate-level phosphorylation = transfer of a high-energy phosphate group from an intermediate compound (substrate) directly to ADP
	- Occurs in cytosol
- Oxidative phosphorylation = removal of electrons from organic compounds, passing them through an electron transport chain to oxygen molecules ($O_2$). Occurs in the inner mitochondrial membrane
# Regulation of intake
- The hypothalamus (arcuate and paraventricular nuclei) regulates appetite and satiety (feeling of fullness)
- Leptin = hormone secreted by adipocytes proportional to fat mass; inhibits eating circuits and increases energy expenditure
- Neuropeptide Y = neurotransmitter that stimulates food intake, released when leptin/insulin are low
- Melanocortin = neurotransmitter that inhibits food intake
- Ghrelin = hormone produced by the stomach that increases appetite
# Carbohydrates
- Glucose is used for
	- ATP production (oxidation)
	- Amino acid synthesis
	- Glycogenesis (glycogen synthesis) = stored in hepatocytes (liver cells) and muscle fibers
	- Triglyceride synthesis = converted to fatty acids for lipogenesis and storage in adipose cells when glycogen stores are full
- Glycogenesis = glucose to glycogen synthesis, stimulated by insulin
- Glycogenolysis = splitting glycogen into glucose subunits, stimulated by glucagon and epinephrine. Hepatocytes can release this glucose into the blood; skeletal muscle fibers cannot (they lack the necessary enzyme, phosphatase)
- Gluconeogenesis = formation of new glucose from noncarbohydrate sources (glycerol from triglycerides, lactic acid, and certain amino acids). Stimulated by cortisol and glucagon
## Glucose transporters
- GLUT1 = ubiquitous; responsible for basal glucose uptake in skeletal muscle and fat
- GLUT2 = a low-affinity transporter in $\beta$ -cells and the liver that functions as a glucose sensor, ensuring uptake only when circulating glucose levels are high
- GLUT4 = the major insulin-responsive transporter found in muscle and adipose tissue
- GLUT5 = primarily a fructose transporter located in the small intestine and spermatozoa
# Lipids
## Formation
- Chylomicrons = formed in the gastrointestinal tract during fat absorption, carrying dietary fat and cholesterol.
	- They consist of triglycerides, cholesterol, phospholipids, and apolipoproteins
	- Lipoprotein lipase (LPL) = enzyme synthesized by adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, and heart tissue, hydrolyzes the chylomicron triglycerides on the capillary endothelial cells, releasing fatty acids and glycerol
- The released fatty acids diffuse into the cells (fat or muscle) to be used for fuel or synthesized back into triglycerides
- Chylomicron remnants, which are cholesterol-enriched, are rapidly cleared by the liver after binding to receptors on liver endothelial cells (a process mediated partly by Apolipoprotein-E)
## Transport
- Lipids are transported in the blood as spherical lipoproteins
	- Apoproteins on the outer shell help solubilize the lipoprotein and have specific functions.
	- Chylomicrons: Transport dietary lipids (triglycerides) from the GI tract to adipose tissue via the lymph (lacteals)
	- VLDLs (Very-Low-Density Lipoproteins): Transport endogenous triglycerides (from the liver) to adipocytes
	- LDLs (Low-Density Lipoproteins): Deliver cholesterol to body cells (known as "bad" cholesterol; high levels increase coronary artery disease risk)
	- HDLs (High-Density Lipoproteins): Remove excess cholesterol from cells and transport it to the liver for elimination (known as "good" cholesterol; high levels decrease coronary artery disease risk)
## Storage 
- Stored as triglycerides in adipose tissue
## Processing
- Lipolysis
	- Triglycerides are split into glycerol and fatty acids, catalyzed by lipases. Enhanced by epinephrine, norepinephrine, and cortisol; inhibited by insulin
	- Glycerol: Can be converted to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate, which can enter glycolysis or gluconeogenesis
	- Fatty Acids: Undergo beta oxidation in mitochondrial matrix, removing 2-carbon fragments to form acetyl CoA, which enters the Krebs cycle
		- Fatty acids ionize in the plasma and combine immediately with albumin molecules; these are called free fatty acids (FFA) or non-esterified fatty acids.
		- FFA concentrations are low, but their turnover is rapid (half the plasma FFA is replaced every 2 to 3 minutes), allowing them to fulfill almost all the body's normal energy requirements
		- FFA concentration increases significantly (fivefold to eightfold) in states of high fat utilization, such as starvation and diabetes mellitus, when little energy is derived from carbohydrates
- Ketogenesis
	- In hepatocytes, two acetyl CoA molecules condense to form ketone bodies (acetoacetic acid, $\beta$ -hydroxybutyric acid, acetone)
	- Ketone bodies leave the liver and are used as fuel (acetyl CoA) by other tissues, notably the heart and kidneys
	- Excessive production during high fat catabolism (e.g., fasting, starvation, uncontrolled diabetes) leads to ketosis
- Lipogenesis
	- Made from glucose or amino acids, occurs in liver and adipose cells
	- Stimulated by insulin = promotes glucose entry into the cells, which provides both the acetyl-CoA/NADPH needed for fatty acid chain production and the essential $\alpha$ -glycerophosphate required to form the glycerol backbone of the triglyceride molecule
	- Carbohydrates are converted to acetyl-CoA via the glycolytic system.
	- Acetyl-CoA is converted into fatty acids using malonyl-CoA and NADPH primarily supplied by the pentose phosphate pathway
	- Fatty acids combine with $\alpha$ -glycerophosphate (a product of glucose degradation) to form triglycerides
## Regulation 
- Fat-sparing effect of carbohydrates = carbohydrates are preferentially used for energy when available
	- The availability of excess carbohydrates increases $\alpha$ -glycerophosphate production, which binds free fatty acids into stored triglycerides, thus limiting the free fatty acids available for energy use
	- Excess carbohydrates also increase intermediates of the citric acid cycle, which accelerate the enzyme acetyl-CoA carboxylase, the rate-limiting step in fatty acid synthesis
- Hormonal mobilization
	- Epi and NE = released during sympathetic stimulation (e.g., heavy exercise). These hormones directly activate hormone-sensitive triglyceride lipase in fat cells, causing rapid breakdown and mobilization of fatty acids into the blood for energy use
	- ACTH and Glucocorticoids: Secreted during stress, they activate the same or a similar hormone-sensitive lipase, enhancing fatty acid removal from fat tissues. Excess secretion of these hormones can lead to ketosis (a ketogenic effect)
	- Growth hormone = mild ketogenic effect by activating hormone-sensitive lipase
	- Thyroid hormone = increases the overall rate of energy metabolism, indirectly causing fat mobilization if carbohydrate and fat stores are insufficient
## Phospholipids and cholesterol
- Phospholipids = major types include lecithins, cephalins, and sphingomyelin
	- Contain fatty acids, phosphoric acid, and usually a nitrogenous base
	- Functions include forming structural elements in cell membranes, being essential components of lipoproteins, acting as an electrical insulator (sphingomyelin in myelin sheath), and forming thromboplastin (a cephalin required for clotting)
	- Synthesis requires specific substances like choline (for lecithin) and inositol (for some cephalins)
- Cholesterol = found in the diet (exogenous) and synthesized by the body (endogenous)
	- Synthesized from multiple molecules of acetyl-CoA to form a sterol nucleus
	- Liver is the main source of circulating endogenous cholesterol
	- Serves as the precursor for bile acids (cholic acid) and several important steroid hormones (adrenal cortex, ovaries, testes)
	- HDL and atherosclerosis = may help prevent atherosclerosis by absorbing cholesterol crystals deposited in arterial walls. A high ratio of HDL to LDL is associated with a reduced likelihood of developing atherosclerosis.
# Proteins
- Amino acids are either oxidized to produce ATP or used rapidly to synthesize new proteins. They are not stored
- Taken up either through digestion or filtered by the kidneys are reabsorbed through the proximal tubular epithelium by secondary active transport
- Deamination = removal of the amino group ($NH_2$) from an amino acid in hepatocytes before the amino acid can be oxidized via the Krebs cycle
- The resulting toxic ammonia ($NH_3$) is converted to relatively harmless urea in the liver and excreted in urine
- Essential amino acids = ten amino acids that must be obtained from the diet because they cannot be synthesized by the body in adequate amounts
- Nonessential amino acids = can be synthesized by body cells via transamination (transferring an amino group to pyruvic acid or a Krebs cycle acid)
- Reactions here require vitamin B6 (pyridoxine)
## Storage
- Intracellular, labile and transported back to blood
- Liver, kidneys, intestinal mucosa
## Synthesis
- Liver synthesizes all of albumin and fibrinogen, disorders here lead to edema 
- Tissue macrophages can take up proteins through pinocytosis 
## Energy use
- Amino acids can be converted to ketone bodies, fatty acids (lipogenesis), or glucose (gluconeogenesis)
- Get deamidated in the liver, ammonia here needs to be excreted
- Keto acids then enter citric acid cycle 
## Regulation
- Growth hormone = increases the synthesis of cellular proteins
- Insulin = essential for protein synthesis
- Glucocorticoids = increase the breakdown of extrahepatic tissue proteins, raising plasma amino acid concentrations to allow the liver to synthesize more 
- Testosterone = causes a significant increase in protein deposition throughout the body, especially contractile proteins in muscles 
- Estrogen = causes some protein deposition, less than testosterone
- Thyroxine = increases the overall metabolic rate of cells; it can increase the rate of protein synthesis if adequate energy (fats and carbohydrates) is available, or cause rapid degradation if they are not
# Key molecules
1. Glucose 6-phosphate:
	- Synthesis of glycogen (storage)
	- Release of glucose into the bloodstream (only by hepatocytes, which have phosphatase)
	- Synthesis of nucleic acids (via ribose 5-phosphate) and NADPH
	- Glycolysis (conversion to pyruvic acid)
2. Pyruvic acid:
	- Production of lactic acid (if oxygen is low/anaerobic)
	- Production of alanine (amino acid) via transamination, linking carbohydrate and protein metabolism
	- Gluconeogenesis (conversion to oxaloacetic acid, then glucose 6-phosphate)
	- Conversion to acetyl coenzyme A (if ATP is low and oxygen is plentiful)
3. Acetyl CoA:
	- Entry into Krebs cycle (gateway for molecules being oxidized to generate ATP)
	- Synthesis of lipids (fatty acids, ketone bodies, cholesterol)
	- Note: fatty acids cannot be used to generate glucose because mammals cannot reconvert acetyl CoA back to pyruvic acid
