# Etiology

Usually happens because of atherosclerotic coronary plaque disruption → platelet aggregation and formation of intracoronary thrombus. This leads to severe or complete occlusion, and that causes an imbalance between how much oxygen the myocardium needs and how much it's actually getting. This diseases process exists on a spectrum of clinical features, from angina pectoris to a full-blown myocardial infarction. 

Partially occlusive thrombus → closely related symptoms of unstable angina and non-ST-segment elevation MI (NSTEMI). The difference between the two is that the unstable angina doesn't have myocardial necrosis, but the NSTEMI does. If there's a fully occluding thrombus and more necrosis, that's going to lead to a ST-segment-elevation MI (STEMI). 
## What happens when a normal blood vessel gets injured?
Primary hemostasis, the formation of a platelet plug when subendothelial tissue components get exposed and cause the coagulation cascade to ensure. Then, those same plasma coagulation proteins get activated and form a fibrin clot (that's secondary hemostasis). There are a few mechanisms that are used to prevent too much thrombosis and occlusion, summarized in this diagram in Lilly.
![400](00%20media/{AAC2884B-23C8-4DCA-93B7-85842E084DAC}.png)

But of course, things go wrong. [Atherosclerosis](08%20Cardiology/Cardiac%20Pathologies/Ischemic%20Heart%20Diseases/Atherosclerosis.md) is supposed to be the driving factor behind all this pathogenesis. Note that the plaque disruption, inappropriate vasoconstriction and loss of normal antithrombotic defenses because the endothelium gets disrupted (can't produce NO and other antithrombotic factors). 
![](00%20media/{DAA2A230-7324-4450-8C38-683B13CE5339}.png)

## What triggers that coronary thrombosis?
Plaque rupture, which is going to expose circulating blood to thrombogenic substances, superficial erosion of the surface endothelium (causes proteoglycans and ECM components to be exposed), and endothelial dysfunction. Note that if the blood vessel is occluded or constricted due to vasoconstriction, washout of thrombogenic factors is also less likely. 

A few things can cause that plaque rupture:
- Chemicals that destabilize plaques
	- Inflammatory substances like IFN-y (inhibits collagen synthesis from SMCs)
	- Cells within these lesions produce metalloproteinases that degrade the ECM, compromises plaque stability
- Physical stresses
	- High intraluminal blood pressure
	- Torsion from the beating myocardium 
- Certain triggers
	- Strenuous physical activity, emotional upset → SNS causes more blood pressure, heart rate, force of contraction
	- Also more likely to occur in the morning because physiologic stressors are just increased at the time

## What about other causes of acute MI?
- Coronary emboli from mechanical or infected cardiac valves (think inflammation from acute vasculitis, connective tissue disorders, rare spontaneous coronary artery dissection in peripartum women)
	- The spontaneous dissection happens due to a combination of hormonal and mechanical changes that weaken the coronary artery walls, making them more susceptible to tearing and dissection
- Type 1 MI refers to typical infarction due to disrupted atherosclerotic plaque with acute thrombus formation
- Type 2 MI is the description for infarction resulting from an oxygen supply-demand mismatch that is not the result of plaque disruption and thrombosis
- Cocaine → ↑ sympathetic tone → adrenal catecholamine release → vasospasm → ↓ myocardial O2 supply → increased demand because of sympathetic stimulation (high HR and BP) but low supply 
## Summary of etiologies of MI
- Atherosclerotic plaque rupture or erosion with superimposed thrombus
- Vasculitic syndromes 
- Coronary embolism (eg, from endocarditis, artificial heart valves)
- Congenital anomalies of the coronary arteries
- Spontaneous coronary artery dissection
- Severe coronary artery spasm (primary or cocaine-induced)
- States of markedly increased myocardial oxygen demand (eg, severe hypertension,
- Tachyarrhythmias, advanced aortic stenosis) or reduced oxygen supply (eg, severe anemia, hypoxemia, hypotension, marked bradycardia)
## Infarct types
- Transmural infarcts = entire thickness of the myocardial wall and result from total, prolonged occlusion
- Subendocardial infarcts = just the innermost layers of the myocardium
- Amount of infarcted tissue depends on
	- Mass of myocardium that lost perfusion
	- Magnitude + duration of impaired flow
	- O2 demand
	- Collateral flow
	- Degree of tissue response 
## Changes in infarction
![600](00%20media/{4CB14384-09E8-4C3A-8D59-F53280C5753A}.png)
### Early changes
- Cellular changes
	- ATP ↓ → ↓ Na+/K+ ATPase → ↑ intracellular Na+ and extracellular K+
	- Edema because of Na+ 
	- Arrythmia because of K+
	- 20 minutes is irreversible damage 
- Vascular permeability ↑ so intracellular proteins leak
- Wavy myofibrils = a sign of muscle fiber injury or incomplete contraction, and can be found in both muscle and heart tissue → fiber were not activated during contraction, or that the fiber has been shortened too much
- Contraction bands = a microscopic sign of myocardial infarction (MI) that indicate cell death in heart muscle tissue, particularly following reperfusion (restored blood flow). They appear as thick, intensely stained bands within the heart muscle cells, caused by a hypercontraction that leads to the cell's rupture due to a sudden influx of calcium
- Neutrophil infiltration, coagulation necrosis
### Late changes
- Macrophages start to come in → yellow softening = connective tissue elements are destroyed and removed along with dead myocardial cells
- Granulation tissue, scarring process, fibrosis and collagen deposition, clearing of the dead myocardium
## Functional issues that arise
- Impaired contractility and compliance
- Stunned myocardium
- Ischemic preconditioning = adenosine and bradykinin, less likely to get an MI and less morbidity and mortality if they've already had one
- Ventricular remodeling
	- Infarct expansion
	- Wall stress ↑ 
	- Function ↓ 
	- Aneurysm ↑ 
	- Arrythmia ↑

## Mechanisms that cause arrhythmias after MI
- Anatomic interruption of blood flow to conduction pathway
- Accumulation of toxic metabolic products, abnormal ion concentrations because membrane leaks
- Autonomic stimulation
- Administration of potentially arrhythmogenic drugs like dopamine (not first-line anymore, but it was used as a catecholamine to treat cardiogenic shock from MI, by increasing cardiac output and blood pressure)
## Mechanical complications
- Papillary muscle rupture
- Ventricular free wall rupture, pseudoaneurysm = a collection of blood outside an artery caused by a tear in the vessel's wall. It differs from a true aneurysm because it's contained by surrounding tissue, not the vessel wall itself
- Ventricular septal rupture
- True ventricular aneurysm
	- Thrombus formation in region of stagnant blood flow
	- Ventricular arrythmia because stretched fibers
	- Heart failure because ↓ forward CO 
## Other important complications
- Heart failure = impaired ventricular contractility, impaired diastole, leads to heart failure 
	- Dyspnea, pulmonary rales, S3
	- Treatments: diuretics, ACE inhibitor, beta blockers 
- Cardiogenic shock
	- Severely decreased cardiac output and hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 90 mm Hg) with inadequate perfusion of peripheral tissues
	- Self-perpetuating because → hypotension leads to ↓ coronary perfusion, which leads to ↑ ischemic damage → reduced SV → increased LV size → even more myocardial O2 demand 
	- Requires inotropic agents and vasopressors to ↑ CO
	- Percutaneous mechanical circulatory support (MCS) 
		- Intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) 
	- Percutaneous left ventricular assist devices (LVADs) 
	- Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO)
- Pericarditis
	- Inflammation extends from myocardium to pericardium
- Dressler syndrome = rare form of pericarditis that can occur weeks following an MI
- Thromboembolism = Stasis of blood flow in regions of impaired LV contraction after an MI may result in intracavity thrombus formation, especially when the infarction involves the LV apex or when a true aneurysm has formed

# Clinical features
## Unstable angina
- Crescendo pattern in which a patient with chronic stable angina experiences a sudden ↑ in frequency, intensity, curation
- Occurs at rest
- New onset of anginal episodes that are severe
## Acute MI
![500](00%20media/{AEDD842B-0845-4F36-AA94-4A46F66A745E}.png)
- Pain C7-T4, feeling of doom
- 5% of patients who sustain an MI are asymptomatic during the acute event → especially diabetic if neuropathy happening
- Pulmonary congestion, ↓ lung compliance, juxtacapillary (J) receptors → rapid shallow breathing 
	- Transudation of fluid into alveoli makes this worse 
- Mild fever because of released inflammatory compounds from macrophages and neutrophils

## Conservative vs early management of UA and NSTEMI
- Depends on Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) risk score → higher score warrants an early invasive strategy 
	- Age > 65 years old
	- ≥3 risk factors for coronary artery disease (as described in Chapter 5)
	- Known coronary stenosis of ≥50% by prior angiography
	- ST-segment deviations on the ECG at presentation
	- At least two anginal episodes in prior 24 hours
	- Use of aspirin in prior 7 days (ie, implying resistance to aspirin’s effect)
	- Elevated serum troponin or CK-MB
