# Definition
Abnormal or insufficient closure of the mitral valve during systole. Primary if it is due to structural defect, secondary if the valve is normal but there is regurgitation because the LV is enlarged. In the secondary case, the mitral annulus is enlarged, and there might be spatial separation of the papillary muscles. 
# Etiology
![400](00%20media/{1524222F-B91E-4541-B09D-194B45D33EA1}.png)

## Acute MR
Sudden damage to valvular apparatus. LA pressure will be increased. Pulmonary capillary wedge pressure, a proxy for LA pressure, will show a prominently increased *v* wave. 
- Rupture of infarcted papillary muscles after STEMI
- Sudden rupture of chordae tendineae due to endocarditis, blunt trauma, connective tissue disorders

Normal LA size and compliance (chamber is quite stiff) → high LA pressure → high pulmonary venous pressure → pulmonary edema
## Chronic MR
LA pressure is a bit increased, but it has also dilated to accommodate. 
- Myxomatous degeneration of the valve (tissue change where glycosaminoglycan accumulates, often leading to a breakdown of collagen and elastic fibers). 
	- Floppy leaflets, bowing excessively → [[08 Cardiology/Cardiac Pathologies/Valvular Diseases/Mitral Valve Prolapse]]
- Rheumatic deformity 
- Congenital valve defects
- Secondary chronic MR → prior MI, chronic ischemic heart diseases, dilated cardiomyopathy 

Increased LA size and compliance → normal LA and pulmonary venous pressures → ↓ forward CO

LV will also undergo gradual compensatory dilatation because of higher volume load, through eccentric hypertrophy. The Frank-Starling mechanism and increased ventricular compliance allows for pretty normal diastolic pressures. However, if it goes on for years, systolic ventricular dysfunction can occur → ↓ forward CO, heart failure. 
## Pathophysiology
Portion of the LV SV is ejected backward into the low-pressure LA during systole. Forward CO becomes less than the LV's total output
- LA volume and pressure ↑ 
- Forward cardiac output ↓ 
- Volume-related stress on LV because regurgitated volume goes back into the LV during diastole alongside normal pulmonary venous return 

LV stroke volume is going to increase, because it's getting that increased volume (think Frank-Starling) → elevated LV EDV → ↑ SV. 

Severity of MR and lessening of forward flow determined by:
- Size of mitral orifice
- Systolic pressure gradient between LA and LV (less LA pressure means fluid is going to rush in, more afterload to the aorta would also likely influence this)
- Systemic vascular resistance opposing forward LV flow (aha, there's that effect of afterload)
- Left atrial compliance
- Duration of regurgitation with each systolic contraction
## Regurgitant fraction
$$RF = \frac{V_{MR}}{LV_{total}}$$
Increases whenever resistance to aortic outflow increased, like if the afterload ↑ due to hypertension or aortic stenosis. Technically speaking, though, the afterload is actually ↓ because the LA is such a low-pressure area. So the LV afterload ↓, even if the heart's forward flow afterload ↑. 

# Epidemiology
# Prognosis
Acute severe MR is pretty poor, even with appropriate treatment. 20-25% 30-day mortality. Chronic MR depends on its cause. 
# Investigations
# Clinical features
- Acute MR: pulmonary edema
- Chronic MR: ↓ CO during exertion, fatigue, weakness
	- LV contractile dysfunction → dyspnea, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea 
	- Symptoms of right heart failure in chronic severe MR → pulmonary hypertension, which forces the right ventricle (RV) to work harder and eventually leads to its dysfunction and failure
## Auscultation
- Apical holosystolic murmur, radiating to axilla
	- Continued pressure gradient between LV and LA 
	- May be decrescendo if it is acute MR! Because of rapid equilibration of LA and LV pressures because LA compliance is so low 
- If only the posterior leaflet is undergoing prolapse, regurgitation will happen anteriorly, and murmur will radiate to the base of the heart (might sound like AS murmur)
- If the patient is asked to clench fists and forearms → ↑ SVR → ↑ MR murmur, but not AS murmur (the stenosis in AS is limiting, doesn't matter what the afterload is like. For MR, more SVR means larger regurgitation fraction) 
- Another way to tell between AS and MR
	- Systolic murmur of AS is louder after a longer cycle length, because more blood is being pushed through the smaller valve
	- Longer cycle length → ↑ LV EDV → but this won't change the pressure gradient between LA and LV, so there is no change in the MR murmur
- Will also hear an S3 (low-pitched, dull sound heard in early diastole, caused by a large volume of blood rapidly filling the ventricle) → because of increased volume returning to the LV in early diastole 
- LV enlargement will also make the point of maximal impulse displaced laterally

## CXR
- Pulmonary edema in acute MR
- Chronic asymptomatic MR → LV and LA enlargement, no pulmonary congestion 
- Calcification of mitral annulus, if that's the cause
## EKG
- LA enlargement
- LV hypertrophy
## Echocardiography
- Structural cause of MR, assess severity
## Cardiac catheterization
- Identify accompanying CAD (causes mitral regurgitation (MR) by damaging and changing the structure of the left ventricle and the connected mitral valve apparatus → ischemic heart disease)
- Left ventriculography to confirm MR severity
- Hemodynamic finding → the large systolic *v* wave in pulmonary capillary wedge pressure → but as the LA gets more dilated and ↑ compliance, this will go away

## Afterload mismatch after valve replacement
- Sudden removal of the low-impedance backflow into the left atrium forces the LV to pump against a higher resistance (afterload)
- When the leaky mitral valve is repaired, the blood can no longer flow backward. This sudden loss of a low-resistance path increases the pressure the left ventricle must pump against, leading to a temporary and acute decline in its pumping function
