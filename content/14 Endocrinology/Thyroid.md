- Vital endocrine organ composed of two lobes connected by a thin band of tissue called the isthmus in some individuals, a pyramidal lobe may also be present above the isthmus

![](00%20media/{41CDBD1F-4F4A-46A3-AA8B-5F02F7FD0F68}.png)

- Cell types
	- Follicular (epithelial) cells = synthesis of thyroid hormones
	- Endothelial cells = line the capillaries that supply blood to the follicles
	- Parafollicular (C Cells) = located in central regions of the lobes, these cells produce calcitonin, which is involved in calcium metabolism
		- Also secrete regulatory factors that modulate follicular cell activity 
	- Other Components = also contains fibroblasts, lymphocytes, and adipocytes
- Blood supply is derived from the superior thyroid artery (the first branch of the external carotid) and the inferior thyroid artery (originating from the thyrocervical trunk off the subclavian artery)
- Embryology and development = the thyroid develops from the floor of the pharynx and descends into the neck during gestation
	- Maintains a connection to the tongue via the thyroglossal duct
	- Can leave remnants like the foramen cecum at the base of the tongue or a **thyroglossal duct cyst**, typically presenting as a painless midline neck mass in children that moves with swallowing if the thyroid fails to descend properly
	- Ectopic thyroid tissue may remain at the base of the tongue, which can become the individual's only source of thyroid hormone

![300](00%20media/{D9C21323-4153-421F-AA8E-E377467D2E6B}.png)


- Follicular cells are functionally polarized; the apical surface faces the colloid for hormone synthesis, while the basolateral surface faces the blood circulation for iodide uptake and hormone release
- Histology and hormone synthesis = the gland is made of follicles filled with colloid and lined by follicular cells → synthesize thyroid hormones (T3 and T4) using tyrosine (from thyroglobulin) and iodine 
	- Uptake = iodide is transported into cells by the Na-iodine symporter (NIS)
		- Energy dependent process 
		- Once inside the cytosol, iodide leaves the cell through an iodide channel on the apical membrane to enter the colloid space
	- Oxidation and organification = the enzyme thyroid peroxidase (TPO) oxidizes iodide to iodine and adds it to tyrosine to form monoiodotyrosine (MIT) and diiodotyrosine (DIT)
	- Coupling = TPO then couples these molecules to create T3 (MIT+DIT) or T4 (DIT+DIT)
- T4 is produced in much larger quantities (>90%), T3 is the more potent hormone
- Most T3 is created in peripheral tissues when the enzyme 5’-deiodinase converts T4 (the prohormone) into T3
- Unlike other endocrine glands, the thyroid can store a 2- to 3-month supply of hormones within the thyroglobulin pool in the colloid
- Upon stimulation, the follicular cells endocytose the colloid, proteolytically release the hormones, and secrete them into the circulation from the basolateral side

# Regulation
- TRH = thyrotropin-releasing hormone is synthesized in the hypothalamus and travels to the anterior pituitary, where it binds to Gαq/11 receptors
- TSH = thyroid-stimulating hormone is released by the anterior pituitary and binds to G<sub>s</sub>-protein–coupled receptors on follicular cells
	- Utilizes cAMP as its main second-messenger system to stimulate every step of hormone synthesis, including iodine uptake, organification, hormone release, and thyroid growth
- Release of TSH is primarily inhibited by T3, which is often derived locally in the pituitary and hypothalamus through the conversion of T4
	- Additional factors that can inhibit TSH release include dopamine, somatostatin, and high cortisol 
# Transport
- Most plasma thyroid hormone is T4, thyroid hormones poorly soluble in water
- Most T4 is bound to TBG, some with transthyretin and albumin
- TBG present in small amount but has high affinity, produced in the liver
- Less TBG → less available T4/T3 to tissues
- Estrogen will raise TBG levels, important for pregnant and OCP users → liver failure will lower TBG levels 
- Peripheral metabolism involves the sequential removal of iodine by specific deiodinases
	- Type I (D1) = found in the liver and kidney; responsible for converting T4 to T3 in hyperthyroid patients
	- Type II (D2) = found in the brain, pituitary, and brown fat; primary producer of serum T3 in humans and essential for negative feedback in the pituitary
	- Type III (D3) = Inactivating enzyme found in the placenta, brain, and skin that converts T4 to inactive rT3 and T3 to T2; protects the fetus from excessive maternal T3
		- But remember that not enough causes cretinism 
# Physiologic effects
- Thyroid hormones increase the basal metabolic rate by upregulating Na+/K+ ATPase pumps, which consumes more ATP and oxygen
- Increasing body temperature and respiration
- Stimulate carbohydrate metabolism (glycogenolysis/gluconeogenesis) and fat metabolism (lipolysis), which leads to lower cholesterol levels
- Increase cardiac output, heart rate, and contractility by increasing the number of beta-1 receptors in the heart
- Essential for normal bone growth and brain maturation; a deficiency in childhood results in cretinism, characterized by stunted growth and mental retardation
	- Coarse features, umbilical hernia, enlarged tongue 
- Disorders are broadly categorized by whether the gland is overactive ([Hyperthyroidism](14%20Endocrinology/Endocrine%20Pathologies/Hyperthyroidism.md)) or underactive ([Hypothyroidism](14%20Endocrinology/Endocrine%20Pathologies/Hypothyroidism.md))
- Pregnancy → TBG levels rise (15-fold), and hCG acts as a weak TSHR agonist, potentially causing transient TSH suppression in the first trimester
	- Iodine requirements increase due to fetal needs and increased renal clearance
## Wolff-Chaikoff effect
- Rapid, temporary reduction in thyroid hormone synthesis caused by high levels of circulating iodide
- Autoregulatory mechanism prevents thyroid hormone overload by inhibiting the organification process
- Amiodarone is a class III antiarrhythmic, commonly used for [Atrial Fibrillation (AF)](08%20Cardiology/Cardiac%20Pathologies/Arrhythmias/Atrial%20Fibrillation%20(AF).md)
	- Contains iodine, and can cause hypothyroidism via excess iodine because of the Wolff-Chaikoff effect 
	- Can also mimic T4 → inhibit 5'deiodinase → ↓ T3 → ↑ TSH → TSH usually rises at the start of therapy, then normalizes 
- Most individuals "escape" this effect by downregulating NIS (the sodium iodine transporter, though those with underlying autoimmune disease may remain blocked, leading to iodide myxedema (a rare form of hypothyroidism caused by prolonged ingestion of excess iodine)
# Calcitonin
- Produced by the thyroid, synthesized by parafollicular C cells (chief cells)
- Lowers serum Ca++, increased Ca++ in the urine → not a huge role, no calcium issues in those with thyroidectomy 
- Used as a pharmacologic therapy for hypercalcemia
# Other syndromes
- Euthyroid sick syndrome = occurs during severe nonthyroidal illness (eg, sepsis, starvation); patients have altered hormone profiles (low T3, high rT3) due to increased D3 activity but appear clinically euthyroid
- Iodine Abnormalities = dietary iodine requirement is 150 μg/day
	- Excess or deficiency can lead to disease, and chemicals like perchlorate or thiocyanate (found in cigarette smoke) can inhibit iodine transport
- Thyroiditis = as opposed to hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism, not really clear pattern
	- Could be hypo or hyper
