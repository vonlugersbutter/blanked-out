# Overview of the GI system
- GI tract (alimentary canal) = continuous tube extending from the mouth to the anus. Organs include the mouth, most of the pharynx, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, and large intestine
- Accessory digestive organs = teeth, tongue, salivary glands, liver, gallbladder, and pancreas
	- Don't come into contact with food, except for teeth and tongue of course
- Ingestion, secretion, motility, digestion, absorption, defecation
# Phases of digestion
- Cephalic phase = thought, smell, sight, or taste of food; prepares the mouth and stomach for eating by stimulating salivary and gastric gland secretion
- Gastric phase = food enters the stomach; promotes gastric secretion and motility
	- Neural regulation through stretch and chemoreceptors, increasing peristalsis and juices
	- Hormonal regulation through gastrin → gastric glands 
- Intestinal phase = food enters the small intestine; primarily has inhibitory effects that slow gastric emptying (to prevent duodenal overload) and promote continued digestion in the small intestine
	- Neural regulation = enterogastric reflex = triggered by duodenal distension, inhibits gastric motility and decreases gastric emptying
# Food intake control
- Ghrelin (from the stomach) stimulates hunger via the hypothalamus, while CCK and PYY (from the intestine) signal satiety
- Mechanisms like the "ileal brake" (triggered by fat in the ileum) slow gastric emptying to ensure the proximal intestine is not overwhelmed
# Layers of the GI tract
- Given from deep to superficial
## Mucosa
- Nonkeratinized stratified squamous in the mouth, pharynx, esophagus, and anal canal for protection
- Stomach and intestines are lined by simple columnar epithelium for secretion and absorption
- Lamina propria is areolar connective tissue containing blood/lymph vessels and large concentrations of MALT (mucosa-associated lymphatic tissue) for disease protection
- Muscularis mucosa is a thin layer of smooth muscle that creates folds in the stomach and small intestine to increase surface area
## Submucosa
- Areolar connective tissue binding the mucosa to the muscularis, containing blood/lymph vessels that receive absorbed food, and the submucosal plexus of neurons 
- Submucosal (Meissner's) plexus = involved in secretion 
## Muscularis
- Generally consists of an inner sheet of circular smooth muscle and an outer sheet of longitudinal smooth muscle
- Skeletal muscle is present in the mouth, pharynx, superior/middle esophagus, and the external anal sphincter, permitting voluntary control of swallowing and defecation
- Involuntary contractions of smooth muscle mix food and propel it along the tract
- Myenteric plexus (Auerbach's) of neurons is located between the circular and longitudinal muscle layers
## Serosa or Adventitia
- Serosa: Found in portions suspended in the abdominal cavity; composed of simple squamous epithelium and areolar connective tissue; also called the visceral peritoneum
- Adventitia: Found in the esophagus instead of serosa; composed only of areolar connective tissue that attaches the organ to surrounding structures
	- Retroperitoneal structures have an adventitia made of loose connective tissue that is not lubricated
# Innervation
- Slow waves = these are oscillating membrane potentials originating in the interstitial cells of Cajal they set the maximum number of contractions per minute = 3 in the stomach, 12 in the duodenum, and 8 in the ileum
## ENS
- Myenteric and submucosal plexus, as mentioned
- Sensory neurons (chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors/stretch receptors) in the mucosal epithelium detect chemical stimuli and stretching
- Acetylcholine = primary excitatory transmitter for both motility and secretion; acts on muscarinic receptors
- Nitric oxide and VIP (vasoactive intestinal peptide) = primary inhibitory transmitters; essential for relaxing sphincters (LES, Pylorus) and producing "caudad relaxation" during peristalsis
- Serotonin (5-HT) = released from enterochromaffin cells; critical for interneuron communication and initiating motility reflexes
## ANS
- Parasympathetic Nerves (Vagus/Pelvic Splanchnic): Generally increase GI secretion and motility by increasing ENS activity
- Sympathetic Nerves: Generally decrease GI secretion and motility by inhibiting ENS neurons. Emotional states like anger or fear can slow digestion via sympathetic stimulation
# Blood supply
## Abdominal aorta and major branches
- Key branches (in order) = celiac trunk, SMA, renal, IMA, common iliac (external/internal), and testicular/ovarian arteries
## Celiac trunk
- Supplies foregut structures = esophagus, stomach, liver, gallbladder, spleen, and parts of the duodenum/pancreas
- Main branches = common hepatic, splenic, and left gastric
- Stomach supply = right and left gastric; right and left gastroepiploic
- Short gastric arteries = 5–7 vessels from the splenic artery supplying the fundus; vulnerable to ischemia because they lack dual blood supply
- Perforated ulcers
	- Gastric ulcers (lesser curvature) bleed from the left gastric artery
	- Posterior duodenal ulcers bleed from the gastroduodenal artery
## Ligaments and maneuvers
- Hepatoduodenal ligament = connects liver to duodenum; contains the proper hepatic artery, portal vein, and common bile duct
- Pringle’s maneuver = clamping this ligament to control liver bleeding
## Superior mesenteric artery
- Supplies midgut structures = distal duodenum to the first 2/3 of the transverse colon
- Major branches = middle colic, right colic, ileocolic, and ileal/jejunal arteries
- Arcades and vasa recta = anastomoses of ileal/jejunal arteries that extend into the bowel
- SMA syndrome = a rare obstruction where the distal duodenum is compressed between the aorta and SMA, often following massive weight loss
## Inferior mesenteric artery 
- Supplies hindgut structures = last 1/3 of the transverse, descending, and sigmoid colon
- Branches = left colic and sigmoid arteries
## Dual blood supply and collaterals
- Celiac – SMA = superior and inferior pancreaticoduodenal arteries
- SMA – IMA = marginal artery of Drummond (middle and left colic branches)
- IMA – iliac = superior rectal (ima) merges with middle rectal (iliac) in the rectum
- Watershed areas (highly vulnerable to ischemia during shock) 
	- Splenic flexure = supplied by small branches; the marginal artery of Drummond is very small here
	- Rectosigmoid junction = supplied by narrow branches of the IMA
## Intestinal ischemia
- Mesenteric ischemia (small intestine) = severe and life-threatening; most commonly caused by embolism (often from [Atrial Fibrillation (AF)](Cardiology/Cardiac%20Pathologies/Arrhythmias/Atrial%20Fibrillation%20(AF).md) /thrombus from [Atherosclerosis](Cardiology/Cardiac%20Pathologies/Ischemic%20Heart%20Diseases/Atherosclerosis.md))
	- Could also be venous thrombosis, especially in hypercoagulable states
	- Symptoms = sudden onset abdominal pain; physically, the patient has "pain out of proportion to exam" (mild tenderness, no peritoneal signs)
	- Labs = elevated WBC, lactate, and acidosis
	- Non-occlusive ischemia = caused by under-perfusion (shock), often resulting in ischemic colitis, especially in watershed areas 
- Chronic mesenteric ischemia (intestinal angina) = recurrent pain after eating ("fear of eating") leading to weight loss, typically in older patients with vascular disease
## Other disorders
- Angiodysplasia = aberrant blood vessels, common in the cecum/right colon, causing lower GI bleeding
- Hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia = also known as Osler-Weber-Rendu syndrome, an autosomal dominant disease causing telangiectasias throughout the GI tract, nosebleeds, and iron deficiency
# Peritoneum
- Largest serous membrane, divided into the parietal peritoneum (lining the cavity wall) and the visceral peritoneum (covering organs, serving as the serosa)
- Retroperitoneal organs are located behind the peritoneum and covered only on their anterior surfaces
	- Some are secondarily retroperitoneal = start inside the peritoneal cavity but later fuse to the posterior abdominal wall during development, becoming fixed behind the peritoneum
	- Mnemonic: SAID PUCKER = suprarenal (adrenal) glands, aorta, IVC, duodenum (later parts), pancreas (head, neck, body), colon (ascending and descending), kidneys, esophagus, rectum 
- Mesentery is a double layer of the peritoneum that suspends abdominal organs from cavity walls 
	- If it surrounds the stomach, it is the mesogastrium
	- Same with the mesoduodenum, the mesocolon 
- Greater omentum
	- Hangs from greater curvature of stomach
	- Covers intestines
	- Formed from mesogastrium
- Lesser omentum
	- Between stomach and liver
	- Formed from ventral mesentery
- Retroperitoneal Bleeding: Visible on CT scans; often a complication of surgical procedures
- Greater Sac = covers the entire width of the abdomen from the diaphragm to the pelvic floor
- Lesser Sac = closed space located behind the liver, stomach, and lesser omentum
- Epiploic Foramen (Winslow’s): The opening between the greater and lesser sacs
## Pectinate line
- Above the line
	- Derived from the hindgut; consists of columnar epithelium
	- Arterial supply = superior rectal artery (branch of the IMA)
	- Venous drainage = superior rectal vein → inferior mesenteric → portal system (may swell in portal hypertension
	- Lymph drainage = internal iliac nodes
	- Innervation = visceral (no pain)
	- Internal hemorrhoids and adenocarcinoma
- Below the line
	- Derived from the proctodeum (ectoderm); consists of stratified squamous epithelium
	- Inferior rectal artery (branch of internal pudendal)
	- Inferior rectal vein → internal pudendal → internal iliac → IVC
	- Lymph drainage = superficial inguinal nodes
	- Somatic innervation, painful
	- External hemorrhoids, squamous cell carcinomas 
# Inguinal anatomy
- Femoral vessels = these are organized lateral to medial as nerve-artery-vein-lymphatics (mnemonic = "navel") the femoral triangle is bounded superiorly by the inguinal ligament, medially by the adductor longus, and laterally by the sartorius
- Femoral sheath = this is a tunnel of fascia below the inguinal ligament containing the femoral vein, artery, and ring, but it does not contain the femoral nerve
- Femoral ring and canal = the femoral ring is the opening to the femoral canal and the site where femoral hernias occur it contains lymph vessels and deep inguinal nodes
- Inguinal canal = this passage in the abdominal wall carries the spermatic cord in males and the round ligament in females its floor is formed by the inguinal ligament, with the deep inguinal ring as the entrance and the superficial inguinal ring as the exit
- Spermatic cord = this travels through the inguinal canal and contains the ductus deferens, arteries, veins, and nerves, protected by three fascial layers = external spermatic, cremasteric, and internal spermatic fascia
