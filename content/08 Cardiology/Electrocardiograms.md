## Depolarization and repolarization
- Sequence by which regions repolarize is actually opposite to that of their depolarization → myocardial action potential durations are more prolonged in cells near the inner endocardium (the first cells stimulated by Purkinje fibers) than in myocytes near the outer epicardium (the last cells to depolarize
- Cells close to the endocardium are the first to depolarize but are the last to repolarize
- Deflections of depolarization and repolarization of the normal heart are oriented in the same direction
- Rest of the body's electrical activity is filtered out, isolated based on frequency

- **The Wilson Central Terminal (WCT)** is an artificially constructed reference for surface electrocardiography, which is assumed to be near zero and steady during the cardiac cycle; namely it is the simple average of the three recorded limbs (right arm, left arm and left leg) composing the Einthoven triangle
$$
V_W = \frac{1}{3}(RA+LA+LL)
$$
- **Goldberger's modification** is now used to produce each augmented limb lead, aVF, aVR, and aVL, which produces 50% larger amplitude leads than the standard WCT
$$
aVR = RA - \frac{LA+LL}{2}
$$
$$
aVL = LA - \frac{RA+LL}{2}
$$
$$
aVF = LL - \frac{RA+LA}{2}
$$

- Q = if first deflection is downward, that's a Q wave
- R = first upward deflection
- S = downward deflection after R 
- Height of the R wave becomes greater than depth of the S wave in leads V3 or V4 → transition lead 
- On EKG, horizontal is small box = 0.04 s, big box = 0.2 s; vertical is small box = 0.1 mV 
- P wave = atrial depolarization, <0.12 s, <0.25 mV 
- PR interval = beginning of P wave, ends at beginning of QRS complex 
	- Normally 0.12 to 0.20 s 
	- Reflects health of the AV node 
	- We don't really see atrial repolarization
- QRS complex
	- J point between QRS complex and ST segment
	- Narrow <0.12 s, which is normal
- ST segment
	- Beginning of ventricular repolarization
	- Normally isoelectric
- T wave 
	- Ventricular repolarization
	- Shape is important, if it's inverted
	- <0.5 mV in limb leads, <1.5 mV in precordial leads
- U wave = late repolarization of Purkinje fibers, not always visible, 5-20% of T amplitude 
- Approach
	- Check voltage calibration at 1.0 mV 
	- Rate
		- Normal = 60-100 bpm
		- Depends on patient status and cardiac output
		- Atrial rate (P) =QRS rate 
		- Six second stops → # cycles * 10 = the heart rate 
		- Use the 300, 150, 100, 75, 60, 50 method
	- Rhythm
		- R-R interval and P-P interval 
	- P waves
	- PR interval
	- QRS complex
	- QT interval = varies with heart rate, so it needs to be corrected → if QT interval is less than half interval between two QRS complexes, it is normal 
	- Axis deviation
		- If QRS is primarily positive in both I and II, normal
		- Isoelectric QRS complex means mean electrical axis of the ventricles is perpendicular to that lead 
		- Inspect the lead that is perpendicular to that isoelectric lead. If QRS is upward, the mean axis points to + of the lead. If -ve, mean QRS points to -ve 

$$
QT_c \; interval = \frac{QT}{\sqrt{RR}}
$$

## Pathologic Q waves in MI
- Irreversible necrosis of heart muscle marked by pathologic Q waves 
![500](00%20media/{C0C6CE84-F30C-43E5-A4F6-5D0CAC33F26F}.png)

- Necrotic muscle does not generate electrical forces → ECG electrode records electrical currents on the opposite region of the ventricle, directed away from the infarct 
- Q wave in only one lead is not diagnostic
- Q waves disregarded in aVR because electrical forces normally directed away from the right arm
- In LBBB, depolarization is too weird to make decisions about Q waves and MI

### Why does the ST elevation happen?
#### Diastolic current theory
- Damaged cells are capable of depolarization, but abnormally leaky → cannot fully repolarize
- Relatively negative, downward shift
- Abnormally displaced downward baseline, so the ST looks elevated 
- When it repolarizes, it goes back to the regular baseline
#### Systolic current theory
- Action potential duration altered, shortened
- Ischemic cells repolarize faster → positive change compared to surroundings 
- Electrical current towards ischemic area, so we see that elevation 

Note that if the ST remains elevated, it's probably a bulging fibrotic scar, a ventricular aneurysm. 

![500](00%20media/{3E6AEA3C-4575-401C-8900-FF663EE5824D}.png)

- In STEMI, we see the whole depolarization issue, and based on the diastolic theory, everything's normal, but the baseline is shifted downward 
- In NSTEMI, ionic leak arises typically from subendocardial tissue, so electrical forces are directed towards the recording electrode. Baseline shifted upward.

## Torsades de pointes
- Early afterdepolarizations = happen during repolarization, usually when action potential is too long
	- Long plateau means L-type Ca²⁺ channels reopen or Na⁺ channels re-trigger
- Delayed afterdepolarizations = happen after repolarization, too much intracellular Ca++, like in digoxin toxicity or too much catecholamines 
	- Extra Ca²⁺ gets released erratically from the sarcoplasmic reticulum
- “Torsades” literally means twisting of the points — it’s a form of polymorphic ventricular tachycardia where the QRS amplitude seems to twist around the baseline.
	- Most often triggered by EADs in settings of prolonged QT
	- EADs in some ventricular regions, dispersion of repolarization, re-entrant, chaotic rhythm.
	- Mg++ inhibits L-type Ca²⁺ channels, so those sneaky early afterdepolarizations can’t re-fire
