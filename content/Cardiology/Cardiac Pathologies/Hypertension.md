- Hypertensive crisis = severe increase in blood pressure, defined as systolic blood pressure 
- Hypertensive urgency = high blood pressure without any signs of organ damage
- Hypertensive emergency = includes signs of new or worsening organ damage, making it a medical emergency that requires immediate treatment
- Refractory or resistant hypertension = a severe form of high blood pressure that is uncontrolled despite using five or more different classes of antihypertensive medications, including a thiazide-type diuretic and a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist

## Normal blood pressure control
Recall that 
$$
BP = CO \times TPR
$$
$$
CO = HR \times SV
$$
- Things that contribute to blood pressure control
	- Heart
	- Blood vessel tone
	- Kidneys
	- Hormones
- Pressure ↑ → urine volume, bp back to normal
		- This is pressure natriuresis, blunted in hypertensive patients
- Carotid sinus → glossopharyngeal IX
- Aortic sinus → vagus X
- Pressure ↑ → ↑ PNS → ↓ SNS, ↓ CO (HR ↓, reduced force)
## Essential or primary hypertension
- Most types of hypertension are primary (essential) hypertension, which makes up 85% to 95% of cases and has no identifiable single cause. Secondary hypertension, which is caused by an underlying medical condition or medication, accounts for the remaining 5% to 15% of cases
- Genetic component from angiotensinogen, alpha adducin (cytoskeletal protein, renal Na+ absorption)
- Insulin resistance
	- Insulin ↑ → ↑ SNS, smooth muscle hypertrophy
- Obesity
	- Angiotensinogen from adipocytes
	- Blood volume ↑ from body mass ↑ 
	- Blood viscosity ↑ , adipocytes release profibrinogen
- EH caused by multiple defects → genetic or acquired contribute to the disease process → combination of genetics, lifestyle, and environmental factors, such as age, obesity, high salt intake, lack of exercise, family history, alcohol use, and tobacco use
- Diabetes
	- Kidney damage, which impairs fluid regulation and increases salt retention
	- High blood sugar also stiffens blood vessels, activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and sympathetic nervous system, increases inflammation, and promotes oxidative stress

## Nonessential or secondary hypertension
- No screening for secondary hypertension unless there are clinical features
- Age = before 20 or older than 50
- Severity = dramatic, quick rise
- Onset = abrupt
- Associated symptoms, like renal artery bruit for renal artery stenosis
- Family history is usually more visible in EH 
- Lab tests
	- Urinalysis to check for renal abnormalities
	- Glucose
	- Serum K+ (really low in renovascular hypertension or aldosteronism)
	- Serum cholesterols
	- EKG for LV hypertrophy
- Exogenous causes
	- Oral contraceptives → estrogen → ↑ angiotensinogen from liver
		- The mechanism is primarily related to exogenous estrogen stimulating hepatic production of angiotensinogen, leading to activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and sodium retention
		- In contrast, endogenous estrogen in premenopausal women is generally protective against hypertension, contributing to lower blood pressure compared to men and postmenopausal women. Endogenous estrogen blunts aldosterone synthesis and supports vasorelaxation, maintaining normal blood pressure
	- Glucocorticoids
	- EPO
	- NSAIDs (Na+ retention)
	- Ethanol and cocaine (both SNS ↑)
- Renal causes
	- Renal parenchymal disease
		- Nephrons don't excrete Na+ and water, so blood volume higher
	- Renal artery stenosis
		- Emboli, vasculitis, compression, atherosclerosis, fibromuscular dysplasia (proliferation, generally in young women)
			- Fibromuscular dysplasia → rare vascular disease that causes high blood pressure (hypertension) by narrowing arteries, most commonly the renal (kidney) and carotid (neck) arteries
				- Percutaneous transluminal balloon angioplasty (PTA) → procedure involves advancing a balloon catheter to the site of stenosis and inflating it to dilate the affected segment
				- Aspirin given later due to risk of thromboembolic events due to arterial webs and areas of dilatation, which may serve as a nidus for platelet aggregation
		- Blood flow to the kidney ↓, GFR ↓, ↑ renin, ↑ blood pressure 
		- Abdominal bruit or unexplained hypokalemia → renal excretion of K+ because ↑ aldosterone 
		- Use ACE inhibitors to treat
- Mechanical causes
	- Coarctation of aorta → congenital 
		- Blood flow to the kidneys ↓ → ↑ RAAS activity
		- High pressure proximal to coarctation → ↑ medial hyperplasia and atherosclerosis → baroreceptor response gets stunted 
		- Inadequate blood flow to legs/left arm, claudication, midsystolic murmur because aortic segment is stenosed → audible between scapulae 
- Endocrine causes 
	- Pheochromocytoma = catecholamine-secreting tumor, usually of adrenal medulla
		- Use some kind of alpha and beta blocker, or remove tumor 
	- Adrenocortical hormone excess
		- Primary aldosteronism = adrenal adenoma or bilateral hyperplasia of adrenal glands 
			- Conn syndrome = primary hyperaldosteronism, is the excess production of the hormone aldosterone from the adrenal glands
		- Secondary aldosteronism = rare renin-secreting tumors 
		- Women taking oral contraceptives
		- Excess glucocorticoids → ↑ blood volume, RAAS 
		- Cushing syndrome = too much glucocorticoids → hypertension 
	- Thyroid hormone
		- Inducing Na+/K+ ATPases in the heart and vessels
		- Increasing blood volume
		- Stimulating tissue metabolism and O2 demand 
		- Hyperthyroidism → ↓ SVR
			- Too much T3 → vasodilation through ↑ NO → compensatory ↑ heart rate and CO
		- Hypothyroidism → ↑ SVR 
			- Reduced endothelium-mediated vasodilation, ↑ arterial stiffness, ↑ vascular tone and resistance → ↑ diastolic hypertension 
## Isolated systolic hypertension
- Isolated systolic hypertension is a condition where systolic is high and diastolic is normal. It is the most common type of high blood pressure, especially in older adults, and is caused by the stiffening of arteries over time
## Modifiable versus nonmodifiable risk factors
- Modifiable risk factors for hypertension are lifestyle-related, including diet, physical activity, smoking, and alcohol consumption, and can be changed to reduce risk
- Non-modifiable risk factors include age, family history, genetic makeup, race, and ethnicity, which cannot be altered
## Consequences of hypertension
![](00%20media/{A57926DA-7B8C-41FB-ADE6-86DAEB349640}.png)
- Flushing, sweating, blurred vision
- Headache, nose bleeds (epistaxis), dizziness 
	- Dizziness caused by damage high blood pressure causes to inner ear blood vessels
- Organ damage
	- Workload of heart, arterial damage, atherosclerosis
- Heart
	- LV hypertrophy and diastolic dysfunction
	- Systolic dysfunction
	- Coronary artery disease
- Cardiovascular system
	- Cerebrovascular accidents 
		- Hemorrhagic CVAs → rupture of microaneurysms in cerebral parenchymal vessels induced by long-standing hypertension
		- Atherothrombotic (also called thromboembolic) CVAs arise when portions of atherosclerotic plaque within the carotids or major cerebral arteries 
- Aorta and peripheral vasculature
	- Coronary artery atherosclerosis
	- Abdominal aortic aneurysm, rupture 
	- Aortic dissection! 
- Kidney
	- Hypertension-induced nephrosclerosis 
	- Perpetuation of elevated BP → a positive feedback cycle
	- Renal exudates → microvascular injury in the renal parenchyma
		- BP ↑ → ↑ glomerular capillary pressure → endothelial damage → disruption of glomerular filtration barrier → ↑ permeability to proteins and inflammatory cells 
	- Abdominal bruits in hypertension = turbulent blood flow through stenotic or atherosclerotic renal arteries, most commonly in renovascular hypertension
- Retina → hypertensive retinopathy 
	- Burst small vessels, local infarction, nicks, indents
	- Silver-lined = walls of the arterioles have become significantly thickened due to chronic, high blood pressure. This is due to changes like medial hyperplasia and the replacement of tissue with hyaline material, which decreases the vessel lumen size
- Elevated ICP associated with Cushing's triad and Cushing's reflex 
	- Hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respirations → late pre-terminal response to ↑ ICP → impending brainstem herniation 
	- ICP approaches or exceeds MAP → ↓ cerebral perfusion, brainstem ischemia → ↑ medullary vasomotor center activation → sympathetic surge → ↑ BP to try to restore cerebral perfusion pressure 
	- Stimulates baroceptors in carotid sinus and aortic arch → reflex bradycardia 
	- Herniation progresses, brainstem compromised → irregular respirations 
## Hypertensive crisis
- Medical emergency, severe elevation
- Acute hemodynamic insult or acute renal disease
- Hypertensive encephalopathy 
- Accelerated-malignant hypertension → acute damage to retinal vessels 
- A 25% reduction in blood pressure is a key treatment goal for a hypertensive crisis to avoid organ damage. During a hypertensive emergency without aortic dissection, preeclampsia, or eclampsia, doctors typically aim to reduce the systolic blood pressure by about 25% in the first hour and then to a level of 160/100–110 mmHg over the next 2 to 6 hours. This cautious approach prevents complications like cerebral, renal, or coronary ischemia

- Allegedly, ACE inhibitors are teratogenic = cause teratogenic effects during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy by disrupting the fetal renin-angiotensin system (RAAS). This leads to fetal kidney damage, resulting in a lack of amniotic fluid (oligohydramnios), which causes anencephaly (underdevelopment of the skull), pulmonary hypoplasia (underdeveloped lungs), and limb deformities
- Do not prescribe an ACEI and ARB together or in combination with a direct renin inhibitor → ↑ risk of hyperkalemia and renal dysfunction and does not provide additional benefit
- 'Triple whammy' in which combinations of diuretics, NSAIDs, ACEi and/or angiotensin receptor antagonists (ARA) may impair renal function
- NSAIDs reduce the effectiveness of diuretics → ↑ risk of AKI 
- Ca++ blocker and beta blockers together → don't do that because really ↓ heart activity 
- Have to wean people off drugs → otherwise risk refractory hypertension 
## White coat hypertension
- When a person's blood pressure reading is high in a clinical setting, such as a doctor's office, but normal when measured at home or with an ambulatory monitor → anxiety of the medical environment
