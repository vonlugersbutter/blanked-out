- Dual functionality = the pancreas serves two distinct purposes = the exocrine pancreas handles digestive enzyme production, while the endocrine pancreas modulates cellular nutrition and nutrient homeostasis through hormone secretion
- Islets of Langerhans = these represent approximately 1 million small glands scattered throughout the exocrine tissue, comprising 1% to 15% of the total pancreatic mass (1 to 2 g)
- Cellular composition and secretory products
	- $\beta$ cells (55%) = produce insulin, c-peptide, proinsulin, islet amyloid polypeptide (IAPP), urocortin 3 (UCN3), and GABA
	- $\alpha$ cells (25%) = produce glucagon and proglucagon
	- $\delta$ cells (10%) = produce somatostatin-14
	- PP cells, or F cells (5%) = produce pancreatic polypeptide
	- $\epsilon$ cells (3%) = produce ghrelin
- Anatomical distribution = PP cells are concentrated in the posterior lobe of the pancreatic head, which originates from the primordial ventral bud and is supplied by the superior mesenteric artery, distinct from the celiac artery supply for the rest of the pancreas
- Innervation and vasculature = islets receive five to ten times more blood flow than exocrine tissue and are innervated by sympathetic, parasympathetic, and sensory neurons
	- Sympathetic = thoracic spinal cord (T5–T12), passing through the celiac and superior mesenteric plexuses via greater/lesser splanchnic nerves
	- Parasympathetic = vagus nerve, dorsal motor nucleus

# Insulin
## Biosynthesis pathway 
- Human insulin gene is located on chromosome 11
- Preproinsulin is translated in the rough endoplasmic reticulum and cleaved by microsomal enzymes to proinsulin
- Proinsulin is packaged in the Golgi apparatus into clathrin-coated granules during maturation, enzymes PCSK1 and PCSK2 cleave proinsulin into equimolar amounts of insulin and c-peptide
- Insulin consists of 51 amino acids across two chains (a and b) linked by disulfide bridges
- Brief circulatory half-life of 3 to 5 minutes, with the liver removing ~50% in a single pass
- C-peptide has a longer half-life (3-4 times that of insulin) and is primarily excreted by the kidneys, making it a useful marker for endogenous insulin production
- If someone is injecting insulin because they are sick (like if they have Munchausen's) → there will be no c-peptide production 
## Mechanism of secretion
- Glucose sensing = glucose enters $\beta$ cells via GLUT2 transporters and is phosphorylated by the rate-limiting enzyme glucokinase
	- GLUT2 is bidirectional, allowing it to come in and out 
	- Fed state: high glucose → glucose traveling in → ↑ insulin
	- Fasting state: low glucose → glucose traveling out → ↓ insulin

- Metabolism increases the ATP-ADP ratio, which closes ATP-sensitive potassium channels (composed of SUR1 and KIR62 subunits)
- Resulting cell depolarization opens voltage-gated calcium channels; the influx of $Ca^{2+}$ triggers insulin exocytosis
- Release pattern = release is biphasic, coming first from a readily releasable pool (5% of granules) and then from a reserve pool (over 95%) a meal increases insulin through higher frequency and amplitude of pulsatile release
## Downstream signaling
- Insulin binds to the $\alpha$ -subunit of its receptor, causing autophosphorylation of the $\beta$ -subunit's tyrosine kinase domain
- Recruits insulin receptor substrates (IRS), activating two main pathways
	- Metabolic pathway = activated via PI3 kinase and Akt/PKB, driving GLUT4 translocation for glucose uptake, glycogen synthesis, and protein synthesis (via mTOR)
	- Mitogenic pathway = activated via the RAS/MAP kinase cascade, mediating cell growth and proliferation
- Signaling begins when insulin binds to its tyrosine kinase cell surface receptor, causing a conformational change that allows for transphosphorylation
- Three critical nodes = downstream signaling is mediated by three primary nodes = IRS proteins (1-4), PI3K, and AKT (protein kinase b)

## Insulin-dependent organs
- Insulin-dependent organs and tissues rely on insulin for efficient glucose uptake and metabolic regulation, with the primary targets being skeletal muscle, adipose tissue (fat), and the liver
- Use insulin to initiate glucose uptake via GLUT4 transporters (muscle/fat) or manage glucose production/storage (liver)
## Tissue effects 
- Insulin is anabolic
- Liver = promotes glycogen synthesis (inducing glucokinase and glycogen synthase) and triglyceride/VLDL formation while inhibiting gluconeogenesis and ketogenesis
	- GLUT2 is also found here, again bi-directional nature is important, decreased gluconeogenesis if there is also glucose 
- Muscle = increases amino acid transport, protein synthesis, and glucose transport (via GLUT4) for glycogen storage
	- Enhancing synthesis and suppressing degradation via the suppression of FOXO transcription factors
- Adipose tissue = promotes triglyceride storage by inducing lipoprotein lipase, increasing glucose transport to provide glycerol phosphate, and inhibiting intracellular lipase

# Other hormonal function 
- Glucagon (catabolic) = secreted in response to low glucose; it acts on the liver to stimulate glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, fatty acid oxidation, and ketogenesis
	- Glucagon stimulates the expression of phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) for gluconeogenesis and inactivates PFK-2 to inhibit glycolysis
	-  Release is stimulated by hypoglycemia and the sympathetic nervous system, while it is inhibited by hyperglycemia and somatostatin
- Somatostatin = acts as a paracrine inhibitor, restraining the release of insulin, glucagon, and growth hormone, and slowing the movement of nutrients from the GI tract into circulation
- Incretins (GLP-1 and GIP) = gut hormones released upon meal ingestion that amplify glucose-induced insulin secretion by increase cAMP levels
	- GLP-1 also inhibits glucagon secretion and delays gastric emptying
- Epinephrine and NE usually ↓ insulin 
- Amylin = co-released with insulin and c-peptide from $\beta$-cells it helps regulate glucose by suppressing postprandial glucagon secretion and slowing gastric emptying
