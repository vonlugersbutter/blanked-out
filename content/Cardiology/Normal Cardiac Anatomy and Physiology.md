# Structure of the heart
We start, again, with some lovely diagrams from Tortora, depicting the heart and its location in the mediastinum.
![](00%20media/{DC9938B3-8A38-401D-9E94-EFC238547A6C}.png)
The pericardium surrounds and protects the heart, and consists of the fibrous (dense irregular connective tissue) and serous pericardium. The fibrous pericardium is fused to the central tendon of the diaphragm through the pericardiophrenic ligament; anchors the heart, movement of the diaphragm eases movement of blood. The serous pericardium, then, is double-layered, made up of the parietal and visceral layers. The visceral layer of the serous pericardium is the epicardium. 
![](00%20media/{261EB008-5D12-44D3-B4A0-ECE082EC3213}.png)

The serous pericardium has serous fluid between its layers, and the potential space that it occupies it is called the pericardial cavity. This can get inflamed, in pericarditis, and cause a pericardial friction rub. There are grooves called sulci on the surface of the heart that house the coronary vessels (discussed below). 
## Epicardium
This also has two layers (so many layers!). Beneath the mesothelium that forms the visceral layer of the serous pericardium, there is another layer of fibroelastic and adipose tissue.
## Myocardium
The muscle, makes up most of the heart. Consists of bundles of involuntary striated cells. 
## Endocardium
Smooth lining inside the heart, that would help prevent friction. It is continuous with the endothelial lining of the great vessels attached to the heart. 
## Blood supply of the heart
The aorta has small branches that come off of it, called the left and right coronary arteries. These branch out from behind two of the cusps of the aortic valve. 
![300](00%20media/{97E4FE59-C0AC-46DA-829D-7891B80A74DF}.png)

As blood recoils after ventricular systole, it fills those aortic sinus, and then fills the arteries. Makes sense; we wouldn't want the intense pressure of blood coming straight out of the left ventricle to blow up these small arteries. There is the deep coronary sulcus, that encircles the heart, and posterior and anterior interventricular sulci. 

The arteries divide as follows. 
- Left coronary artery
	- Left anterior descending artery (lies in the AIVS, supplies LV and RV)
	- Left circumflex artery (wraps around heart, supplies LA and LV)
- Right coronary artery (small branches to right atrium)
	- Posterior interventricular artery (lies in the PIVS, supplies RV and LV)
	- Right marginal artery (supplies RV)

![500](00%20media/{53BF151D-88D4-4F2F-A2E3-E68E167E1D26}.png)

The venous return of the heart is as follows.
- Small cardiac vein (drains RA and RV)
- Anterior cardiac vein (drains RV, opens into RA)
- Middle cardiac vein (lies in the PIVS, drains LV and RV)
- Great cardiac vein (runs beside circumflex artery, drains LA, LV, RV)
- Gray's also suggests there's a posterior cardiac vein, the one that looks like it's branching off from the great cardiac vein. Unclear what that's draining, but it's probably the LV. 
All these veins, except the anterior cardiac vein, drain into the coronary sinus. It has a thin wall, which then drains into the RA. 

![500](00%20media/{F6D0358B-16A0-4EBC-B226-EFE15EEE8639}.png)

Also note that there are some discrepancies from person to person. In most people, the posterior interventricular artery is derived from the right coronary artery, while in some, it is from the left. In some people, there is a conus artery that provides further blood flow to the heart when the left anterior descending artery is occluded. 
## Heart chambers
Note the chambers, the great vessels. 
![](00%20media/{80CAC11D-A043-4D56-B0C6-98A17F641693}.png)

In the right atrium, the anterior and posterior walls are pretty different. The posterior inside wall is smooth, but the anterior wall is rough due to pectinate muscles (allegedly useful in increasing the power of contraction without increasing heart mass substantially). There's the fossa ovalis in the interatrial septum, remnant from the foramen ovale. 

Note the trabeculae carneae, papillary muscles, and cusps that make up the tricuspid and bicuspid valves. Note also the positioning of the great vessels. Aorta starts off in the back, pulmonary trunk in the front, then pulmonary trunk wraps to the back. Superior vena cava is to the right. Note also the ligamentum arteriosum. 
![](00%20media/{3E4E27CC-A003-4C2C-850B-FB12309D63C6}.png)

The fibrous skeleton is dense connective tissue that can be found in the heart wall, and it also surrounds the valves of the heart. Great for electrical separation and providing structural support for these valves, at least according to Tortora.

# Cardiac muscle tissue and conduction
Ends of cardiac muscle fiber cells connect with intercalated discs. Discs have desmosomes (hold things together) and gap junction (allow action potentials to jump from cell to cell)

There's an inherent rhythmic electrical activity in the heart, produced by self-excitable autorhythmic fibers. We normally go from the sinoatrial node → atrioventricular node → atrioventricular bundle or bundle of His → left and right bundle branches through the IV septum → Purkinje fibers that go from the apex back up to the rest of ventricular myocardium.

## Steps of cardiac cell excitation
- Resting membrane potential here is around -90 mV, maintained by the Na+/K+ ATPase
- Depolarization
	- Voltage-gated fast Na+ channels open, and then quickly close
- Plateau
	- Voltage-gated slow Ca++ channels open, allow Ca++ to come in
	- Ca++ from the SR also pours out
	- Increased Ca++ is what allows for muscle contraction (see the diagram below)
	- Just before the plateau phase, K+ voltage-gated channels open, allowing K+ to leave 
	- Depolarization is sustained because the two match each other
	- Longer plateau compared to skeletal muscle and neurons, longer refractory period prevents tetany 
- Repolarization
	- Ca++ channels on the SR and sarcolemma close, and K+ channels open → as the K+ goes out, resting membrane potential of -90 mV is achieved

# Cardiac output control
$$CO = HR \times SR$$

Cardiac reserve is the difference between a person's maximum CO versus the CO at rest. 
## Factors that influence stroke volume
### Preload
Basically, the degree of stretch on the heart before it contracts. Three important things to consider here are EDV, duration of ventricular diastole, and venous return.
$$preload \propto EDV$$
$$EDV \propto VD$$
Where VD is the duration of ventricular diastole. Higher heart rate means less time for ventricular filling, less VD, less EDV.
$$EDV\propto VR$$
Where VR is venous return. 

**Frank-Starling law of the heart** = the more you fill the heart in diastole, the greater the force of contraction during systole. For example, let's say that the left side of the heart is pumping more blood. More blood means the left side is going to pump harder. It would send more blood to the right side. That would make the right side pump harder. The right side EDV goes up, and it pumps more, balancing the two out. 
### Contractility
Strength of a contraction at a given preload. 

You can have positive inotropic agents, that increase contractility. These usually include things that increase Ca++ inflow.
- SNS activation
- Epinephrine or norepinephrine
- Digitalis 
	- Inhibits Na+/K+ ATPase → ↑ Na+ intracellular → lower Na+ concentration gradient → Na/Ca exchanger reverses → ↑ Ca++ intracellular → ↑ contractility

You can have negative inotropic agents, that decrease contractility.
- Ca++ channel blockers
- SNS inhibition
- Anoxia
- Acidosis
- ↑ K+ levels  
	- Cell becomes depolarized, some of the Na+ voltage-gated channels become inactivated and do not open properly when a cell is ready for depolarization.

> [!note] Why does hyperkalemia make the Na+ channels less likely to fire?
> 
> The Na+ voltage-gated channels have two gates (m and h) while the K+ channel has only one gate (n). **Gate m (the activation gate)** is normally closed, and opens when the cell starts to get more positive. This allows for that depolarization. **Gate h (the deactivation gate)** is normally open, and swings shut when the cells gets too positive.
> 
> There's also a gate n, which is normally closed, but slowly opens when the cell is depolarized (very positive).
> 
> So, these channels exist in three states. Deactivated (closed) - at rest, channels are deactivated. The m gate is closed, and does not let sodium ions through. Activated (open) - when a current passes through and changes the voltage difference across a membrane, the channel will activate and the m gate will open. Inactivated (closed) - as the neuron depolarizes, the h gate swings shut and blocks sodium ions from entering the cell.
> 
> In our case, more K+ outside means the cell is more positive than -90 mV, maybe around -70 mV. This means that those h gates are never going to open because the inside of the cell doesn't get negative enough to reset them. 
> 

### Afterload
Pressure that must be overcome before a semilunar valve opens.
$$afterload \propto \frac{1}{SV}$$
$$afterload \propto ESV$$
More pressure from the aorta, for example through hypertension or atherosclerosis, means that the ventricle has to push harder before the valve opens. This would decrease the stroke volume. 
## Factors that influence heart rate
### Autonomic regulation
There is a cardiovascular center in the medulla, which receives signals from the cerebral cortex and limbic system. It also receives signaling from proprioceptors (in the limbs and muscles), chemoreceptors and baroreceptors (which detect the structure and stretch of the vessels, especially the carotids and aorta). 

SNS neurons in the medulla → spinal cord (thoracic) → cardiac accelerator nerves → SA node → ↑ norepinephrine → ↑ $\beta$ -1 receptor activation → increased rate of depolarization in SA and AV nodes, and ↑ Ca++ entry through voltage-gated Ca++ channels
- More specifically,
	- ↑ $\beta$ -1 receptor activation → AC → ↑ cAMP → PKA → phosphorylates several key channels 

Parasympathetic stimuli reach the heart through right and left vagus nerves. They release ACh, which slows the heart rate. There aren't many vagal fibers in the ventricles, so changes in parasympathetic tone don't do much. 
### Chemical regulation
Hormones, such as norepinephrine and epinephrine. Thyroid hormone is also one of them.

Tortora says that ↑ K+ and Na+ decrease heart rate, while ↑ Ca++ speeds heart rate. Unclear if this is actually the case, or if it is just changing contractility.
### Other factors
Age makes heart rate go down. Hypothermia also slows the heart.
