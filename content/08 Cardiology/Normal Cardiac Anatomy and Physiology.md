# Structure of the heart
We start, again, with some lovely diagrams from Tortora, depicting the heart and its location in the mediastinum.
![](00%20media/{DC9938B3-8A38-401D-9E94-EFC238547A6C}.png)
The pericardium surrounds and protects the heart, and consists of the fibrous (dense irregular connective tissue) and serous pericardium. The fibrous pericardium is fused to the central tendon of the diaphragm through the pericardiophrenic ligament; anchors the heart, movement of the diaphragm eases movement of blood. The serous pericardium, then, is double-layered, made up of the parietal and visceral layers. The visceral layer of the serous pericardium is the epicardium. 
![](00%20media/{261EB008-5D12-44D3-B4A0-ECE082EC3213}.png)

The serous pericardium has serous fluid between its layers, and the potential space that it occupies it is called the pericardial cavity. This can get inflamed, in pericarditis, and cause a pericardial friction rub. There are grooves called sulci on the surface of the heart that house the coronary vessels (discussed below). 

Purpose of the pericardium: fix heart and limit movement, prevent extreme dilatation, limit spread of infection from lungs. 
## Epicardium
This also has two layers (so many layers!). Beneath the mesothelium that forms the visceral layer of the serous pericardium, there is another layer of fibroelastic and adipose tissue.
## Myocardium
The muscle, makes up most of the heart. Consists of bundles of involuntary striated cells. 
## Endocardium
Smooth lining inside the heart, that would help prevent friction. It is continuous with the endothelial lining of the great vessels attached to the heart. 
## Blood supply of the heart
The aorta has small branches that come off of it, called the left and right coronary arteries. These branch out from behind two of the cusps of the aortic valve. 
![300](00%20media/{97E4FE59-C0AC-46DA-829D-7891B80A74DF}.png)

As blood recoils after ventricular systole, it fills those aortic sinus, and then fills the arteries. Makes sense; we wouldn't want the intense pressure of blood coming straight out of the left ventricle to blow up these small arteries. There is the deep coronary sulcus, that encircles the heart, and posterior and anterior interventricular sulci. 

The arteries divide as follows. 
- Left coronary artery
	- Left anterior descending artery (lies in the AIVS, supplies LV and RV)
	- Left circumflex artery (wraps around heart, supplies LA and LV)
- Right coronary artery (small branches to right atrium)
	- Posterior interventricular artery (lies in the PIVS, supplies RV and LV)
	- Right marginal artery (supplies RV)

![500](00%20media/{53BF151D-88D4-4F2F-A2E3-E68E167E1D26}.png)

The venous return of the heart is as follows.
- Small cardiac vein (drains RA and RV)
- Anterior cardiac vein (drains RV, opens into RA)
- Middle cardiac vein (lies in the PIVS, drains LV and RV)
- Great cardiac vein (runs beside circumflex artery, drains LA, LV, RV)
- Gray's also suggests there's a posterior cardiac vein, the one that looks like it's branching off from the great cardiac vein. Unclear what that's draining, but it's probably the LV. 
All these veins, except the anterior cardiac vein, drain into the coronary sinus. It has a thin wall, which then drains into the RA. 

![500](00%20media/{F6D0358B-16A0-4EBC-B226-EFE15EEE8639}.png)

Also note that there are some discrepancies from person to person. In most people, the posterior interventricular artery is derived from the right coronary artery, while in some, it is from the left. In some people, there is a conus artery that provides further blood flow to the heart when the left anterior descending artery is occluded. Some people are more left-dominant, meaning more of these branches come off the LCA, while some are right-dominant. Some are co-dominant. 

The branches travel from the epicardium, to smaller branches in the ventricular muscle. These go on to form plexuses, with lots of capillaries. 

Thebesian veins help supply the muscles under the endocardium and the papillary muscles. They are ventricular cavity vascular channels. While they are a normal part of cardiac anatomy, abnormally large Thebesian veins can be associated with certain medical conditions, such as congenital heart disease. In some rare cases, extensive veins can cause symptoms that mimic a heart attack by causing a "coronary steal" where blood flow is diverted away from the heart muscle itself. Coronary steal is a phenomenon where blood flow is diverted from a narrowed coronary artery to a healthier artery, often triggered by medications that dilate blood vessels or by certain surgical procedures. This can worsen ischemia.

Collateral connections also allow for blood flow if one of the arteries is occluded.
## Lymphatic drainage
Lymph → plexus of valved vessels in subendocardial connective tissue → epicardial plexus → lymphatic vessels → AV groove conduit → mediastinal lymphatic plexus → thoracic duct. 
## Heart chambers
Note the chambers, the great vessels.

# Cardiac innervation
## Parasympathetic
Preganglionic start in dorsal motor nucleus of medulla, go down as branches of vagus nerve. Vagal afferents → cardiac reflexes. Vagal efferents to SA/AV nodes → electrical impulse initiation and conduction. 
## Sympathetic 
Preganglionic, upper 5-6 thoracic levels → synapse with 2nd-order cervical sympathetic ganglia and travel with cardiac nerves.

# Cardiac muscle tissue and conduction
Ends of cardiac muscle fiber cells connect with intercalated discs. Discs have desmosomes (hold things together) and gap junction (allow action potentials to jump from cell to cell). These help keep the fibers together, and allow ions to pass through. 

T-tubules are invaginations, and ↑ the surface area of the sarcolemma. SR abuts t-tubules at 90°, creating terminal cisternae. Heart has lots of mitochondria to supply it. Most of the heart's energy comes from fatty acids, and relies mostly on aerobic cell respiration → presence in blood of creatine kinase (CK) is a sign of MI, injured or dying cells release this into the blood.

There's an inherent rhythmic electrical activity in the heart, produced by self-excitable autorhythmic fibers. We normally go from the sinoatrial node → atrioventricular node → atrioventricular bundle or bundle of His → left and right bundle branches through the IV septum → Purkinje fibers that go from the apex back up to the rest of ventricular myocardium. 

Fibrous tissue is at the tricuspid and mitral valves, so current can only go from atria → ventricles through the AV node.

- Tachycardia (more than 100 bpm)
- Fibrillation = rapid, uncoordinated heartbeats
- Supraventricular/atrial arrhythmias 
- Ventricular arrhythmia

## Steps of cardiac cell excitation

| Current involved               | Description                                                                                                                                                            |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| I<sub>f</sub>                  | Pacemaker current, does the phase 4 depolarization in pacemaker cells                                                                                                  |
| I<sub>Na</sub>                 | Na current, responsible for phase 0 rapid depolarization in non-pacemaker cells                                                                                        |
| I<sub>Ca.L</sub>               | Slow, long-lasting Ca++ current. Responsible for phase 0 depolarization in pacemaker cells. Major contributor to inward current during phase 2 of non-pacemaker cells. |
| I<sub>K1</sub>                 | Maintains resting potential, inward rectifying potassium channel.                                                                                                      |
| I<sub>to</sub>                 | Transient outward potassium current, phase 1 of action potential.                                                                                                      |
| I<sub>Ks</sub>, I<sub>Kr</sub> | Delayed rectifier K+ currents of slow (Ks) and rapid (Kr) types. Repolarizing active during phase 2 and 3 of action potential.                                         |
![](00%20media/{2FDD954B-BBBD-4E4B-B3A6-121B77EF6095}.png)

We start with the process for ==cardiac muscle cells==. 
### Phase 4
Resting membrane potential, around -90 mV. Maintained by the Na+/K+ ATPase and the inward rectifier K+ channels. 
- K+ wants to flow out due to chemical gradient, but wants to flow in due to electrostatic gradient. This makes its movement net zero, with the potential set to -91 mV
- Na+ leaks a bit out of the cell, setting it down to -90 mV
- Na+ and Ca++ channels closed. 
### Phase 0
- Depolarization
- Voltage-gated fast Na+ channels open
- Threshold potential of -70 mV achieved, self-sustaining Na+ current (I<sub>Na</sub>) present, exceed exit of K+ through inward rectifier channels 
- Quickly inactivate
### Phase 1
- Brief repolarization, return to ~0 mV 
- I<sub>to</sub>, outflow of K+ by transiently activated K+ channel
### Phase 2
- Voltage-gated slow Ca++ L-type channels begin to open in phase 0 around -40 mV, allow Ca++ to come in
- Ca++ from the SR also pours out
- Increased Ca++ is what allows for muscle contraction (see the diagram below)
- Outward K+ current due to I<sub>Ks</sub>, I<sub>Kr</sub> 
- Depolarization is sustained because the two match each other, nearly net zero current 
- Longer plateau compared to skeletal muscle and neurons
- Ca++ channels gradually deactivate, efflux of K+ allows phase 3 to begin
### Phase 3
- Transmembrane voltage back to -90 mV, repolarization
- K+ out, membrane is not permeable to other ions
- Ca++ removed with Na+-Ca++ exchanger and sarcolemmal Ca++-ATPase
- Na+/K+ ATPase helps establish usual gradient 

![](00%20media/{9F30CF28-1E94-49C2-A876-F58E77FCE1C6}.png)

For ==specialized conduction fibers==, like the Purkinje fibers, the process is the same as above, except the resting membrane potential is even lower, and phase 0 is even more rapid.

For ==pacemaker cells==,
- Automaticity → other cells only show this in ischemia
- -60 mV resting, so Na+ channels stay inactivated (see the whole m and h gate discussion below)
- Pacemaker current I<sub>f</sub> (funny current) opens during repolarization, and this gradual inflow of Na+ makes it less negative
- Upstroke is solely dependent on the Ca++ influx (I<sub>Ca.L</sub>), which is slower 
- Repolarization happens through K+ efflux through I<sub>Ks</sub>, I<sub>Kr</sub>

## Why the long refractory period?
Need it to allow the ventricles to relax, refill, and prevent tetany. 

Degree of refractoriness depends on the percentage of fast Na+ channels that recovered from their inactive state and can open. 
- Absolute period = depolarization not gonna happen
- Effective period = localized action potential, not propagated
- Relative period = conducted, but action potential is lower because some Na+ channels inactivated, some delayed rectifier K+ channels remain activated
- Supranormal period = extra sensitive to depolarization

![400 ](00%20media/{28751039-E693-4A26-84C5-1F449B785106}.png)
Atria have shorter refractory period than ventricular muscle cells, so their rates are higher than ventricular rates during arrhythmias. 
## Speed of conduction
- Speed of tissue depolarization and conduction velocity depend on inward current (basically number of Na+ channels), resting potential (degree of Na+ channel inactivation), and resistance to flow
- High concentration of Na+ channels, like Purkinje fibers = large, fast inward current!
- AV node has delay of conduction, because fibers are small diameter, relies on slow Ca++ channel depolarization 
	- Allows atria enough time to fully fill up the ventricles

## Wiggers diagram and heart sounds
- SA node depolarizes in late diastole → spread to atrial myocytes, final boost of blood that we see as 2 below → a wave, atrial kick
- Pause at the AV node, then ventricular systole
### S1
- Mitral and tricuspid valves close (S1) → mitral goes first because electric conduction in the left ventricle is earlier 
	- In RBBB, components might be distinguishable due to delayed RV contraction and delayed tricuspid closure 
- Things that change intensity of S1
	- Distance separating leaflets of open valves at onset of contraction
	- Mobility of leaflets
	- Rate of ventricular pressure rise 
- Conditions that accentuate S1
	- Short PR interval = valve leaflets don't have time to go back together and forced shut by ventricular contraction from a larger size 
	- Mild mitral stenosis = high pressure gradient keeps mobile components apart, so leaflets that can move are quite wide 
	- High cardiac output or tachycardia = shortened diastole 
- Conditions that diminish S1
	- Lengthened PR interval, first degree AV block
	- Mitral regurgitation
	- Severe mitral stenosis
	- Stiff left ventricle
### S2 
- Semilunar valves close, S2 → aortic normally precedes pulmonic because pressure gradient is greater on the left than on the right, so aortic valve shuts more readily 
- Two sounds (A2 and P2) that become separated during inspiration → physiologic splitting
	- During inspiration... Intrathoracic pressure ↑ → ↑ capacitance of pulmonary veins, so they can hold more → delay in diastolic back pressure causing pulmonic valve closure → P2 is delayed on inspiration
		- Basically, the right side afterload ↓, so the valve closes later
	- During inspiration... Intrathoracic pressure ↑ → ↑ capacitance of pulmonary veins, so they can hold more → ↓ LA + LV filling → A2 occurs sooner on inspiration 
- Intensity of S2
	- Velocity of blood coursing back on valves after closure
	- Suddenness of motion arrest 
	- In hypertension → ↑ intensity
	- Severe SL stenosis → valve commissures nearly fixed, ↓ intensity 
- Types of pathological splitting
	- Widened splitting = delayed closure of the pulmonic valve
	- Fixed splitting = atrial septal defect, chronic volume overload of the right-sided circulation → high-capacitance, low-resistance pulmonary system 
		- Inspiration doesn't change the already elevated capacitance
		- More filling of RA is counterbalanced by ↓ in trans-atrial shunt 
	- Paradoxical splitting = P2 precedes A2
		- LBBB, delayed ventricle contraction, late closure of A2 
		- Could also happen in aortic stenosis → LV ejection is prolonged

## Factors that influence stroke volume
### Preload
Basically, the degree of stretch on the heart before it contracts. Three important things to consider here are EDV, duration of ventricular diastole, and venous return.
$$preload \propto EDV$$
$$EDV \propto VD$$
Where VD is the duration of ventricular diastole. Higher heart rate means less time for ventricular filling, less VD, less EDV.
$$EDV\propto VR$$
Where VR is venous return. 

Let's imagine a muscle that is stretched, and then stimulated while its ends are held fixed. This is isometric contraction, and is equivalent to isovolumetric contraction. In this case, the total tension is $\propto$ the length at the time of stimulation. There is ↑ myosin-acting cross-bridges, and ↑ sensitivity to Ca++. This is the Frank-Starling law of the heart.

**Frank-Starling law of the heart** = the more you fill the heart in diastole, the greater the force of contraction during systole. For example, let's say that the left side of the heart is pumping more blood. More blood means the left side is going to pump harder. It would send more blood to the right side. That would make the right side pump harder. The right side EDV goes up, and it pumps more, balancing the two out. 

In Lilly's words, ventricular output is $\propto$ preload, where **preload is the stretch on the myocardial fibers before contraction**. It is $\propto$ EDV. 
![200](00%20media/{DD94462C-9829-4D5C-81F5-0A77267B9089}.png)

Imagine that one muscle is stretched to a, while one is stretched to c. The muscle stretched to c will show a higher tension d, compared to the the lower tension b for a. More preload, more tension. 

Frank-Starling curves show this relationship:
![400](00%20media/{632A9C44-FF2C-4AD9-B63E-9389892F69F6}%201.png)
### Afterload
Pressure that must be overcome before a semilunar valve opens.
$$afterload \propto \frac{1}{SV}$$
$$afterload \propto ESV$$
More pressure from the aorta, for example through hypertension or atherosclerosis, means that the ventricle has to push harder before the valve opens. This would decrease the stroke volume. 

An isotonic contraction is where the muscle is allowed to shorten during stimulation against a fixed load (the afterload). The tension here is determined by the magnitude of the load, and is independent of the length of the muscle. The maximum tension here is the same as the isometric contraction at the same length. This represents the ventricular ejection part of the cardiac cycle. Lilly states that the pressure and size of the ventricle is dependent on the afterload, but independent of the stretch on myocardial fibers or preload. I take this to mean that the preload determines how much the fibers *can* stretch, but afterload determines how much they *will* stretch. If the load is huge, then the preload won't be of much use. 
![200](00%20media/{67D0F3C3-A373-4D8E-A8C2-873F6A664BBD}.png)

Here, if the load is e, the tension will be fixed to that load even if they started at different muscle lengths a and c. 

Afterload can also be defined as the wall stress ($\sigma$) that develops during systolic ejection, given by the Laplace relationship, where P is pressure, r is radius, and h is wall thickness. 
$$
\sigma = \frac{P \times r}{2h}
$$
### Contractility
Strength of a contraction at a given preload and afterload. Lilly defines this as changes in force of contraction independent of initial fiber length and preload, through chemical and hormonal influences. 
![200](00%20media/{69007819-93CD-4176-9880-82024F591213}.png)

A word that gets thrown around a lot here is the 'intrinsic' properties of the muscle. Either way, let's say that the muscle gets some NE. Against the same load, e versus g, the presence of an inotropic agent makes the ending muscle length shorter, and causes a greater change in muscle length. The Frank-Starling curve (SV vs LVEDV) will shift up or down based on the contractility, as shown in the preload section.  

You can have positive inotropic agents, that increase contractility. These usually include things that increase Ca++ inflow.
- SNS activation
- Epinephrine or norepinephrine
- Digitalis 
	- Inhibits Na+/K+ ATPase → ↑ Na+ intracellular → lower Na+ concentration gradient → Na/Ca exchanger reverses → ↑ Ca++ intracellular → ↑ contractility

You can have negative inotropic agents, that decrease contractility.
- Ca++ channel blockers
- SNS inhibition
- Anoxia
- Acidosis
- ↑ K+ levels  
	- Cell becomes depolarized, some of the Na+ voltage-gated channels become inactivated and do not open properly when a cell is ready for depolarization.

> [!note] Why does hyperkalemia make the Na+ channels less likely to fire?
> 
> The Na+ voltage-gated channels have two gates (m and h) while the K+ channel has only one gate (n). **Gate m (the activation gate)** is normally closed, and opens when the cell starts to get more positive. This allows for that depolarization. **Gate h (the deactivation gate)** is normally open, and swings shut when the cells gets too positive.
> 
> There's also a gate n, which is normally closed, but slowly opens when the cell is depolarized (very positive).
> 
> So, these channels exist in three states. Deactivated (closed) - at rest, channels are deactivated. The m gate is closed, and does not let sodium ions through. Activated (open) - when a current passes through and changes the voltage difference across a membrane, the channel will activate and the m gate will open. Inactivated (closed) - as the neuron depolarizes, the h gate swings shut and blocks sodium ions from entering the cell.
> 
> In our case, more K+ outside means the cell is more positive than -90 mV, maybe around -70 mV. This means that those h gates are never going to open because the inside of the cell doesn't get negative enough to reset them. 
>

## Factors that influence heart rate
### Autonomic regulation
There is a cardiovascular center in the medulla, which receives signals from the cerebral cortex and limbic system. It also receives signaling from proprioceptors (in the limbs and muscles), chemoreceptors and baroreceptors (which detect the structure and stretch of the vessels, especially the carotids and aorta). 

Note that the aortic ones can only detect increases in pressure. The ones in the carotid sinus can detect increases and decreases. 

SNS neurons in the medulla → spinal cord (thoracic) → cardiac accelerator nerves → SA node → ↑ norepinephrine → ↑ $\beta$ -1 receptor activation → increased rate of depolarization in SA and AV nodes, and ↑ Ca++ entry through voltage-gated Ca++ channels
- More specifically,
	- ↑ $\beta$ -1 receptor activation → AC → ↑ cAMP → PKA → phosphorylates several key channels 
	- Increase in release of Ca++ from the SR, more force of contraction 

![500](00%20media/{1ABA98A0-2137-4F6F-953E-869D3EB00804}.png)

Phospholamban (PL) is a small protein in the SR membrane. It inhibits Ca++ uptake by SERCA
- Beta-adrenergic activation of PKAs causes PL phosphorylation, blunts PL's inhibitory effect
- More uptake of Ca++, removal from the cytosol
- So this actually promotes relaxation of the myocyte
- TnI also gets phosphorylated, which inhibits actin-myosin interactions and also relaxes the cell 

Parasympathetic stimuli reach the heart through right and left vagus nerves. They release ACh, which slows the heart rate. There aren't many vagal fibers in the ventricles, so changes in parasympathetic tone don't do much. 
- Bind to muscarinic M2 receptors 
- Coupled to G inhibitory proteins, ↓ cAMP formation
### Chemical regulation
Hormones, such as norepinephrine and epinephrine. Thyroid hormone is also one of them.

Tortora says that ↑ K+ and Na+ decrease heart rate, while ↑ Ca++ speeds heart rate. Unclear if this is actually the case, or if it is just changing contractility.
### Other factors
Age makes heart rate go down. Hypothermia also slows the heart.
