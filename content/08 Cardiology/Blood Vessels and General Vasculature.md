## Types of arteries
### Elastic arteries
Largest ones. Well-defined internal and external elastic laminae. Examples include aorta and pulmonary trunk. Propel blood, act as a pressure reservoir. Also called conducting arteries. Recoil of elastic arteries keeps blood flowing during ventricular diastole. So the blood can be pushed, even if the semilunar valve has closed. 
### Muscular arteries
More smooth muscles, fewer elastic fibers. Relatively thick walls, can vasoconstrict and vasodilate really well. Also called distributing arteries. 
### Anastomoses
Blood vessel branches where area receives supply from more than one artery. Collateral circulation, as opposed to end arteries. 
### Arterioles
Smol baby arteries. Terminal end of arteriole called metarteriole, has a precapillary sphincter that monitors the blood flow into the capillaries. Arterioles are important for regulated resistance. Called resistance vessels. Remember Poiseuille's law! So, vasoconstriction and vasodilation is really important here. 
### Capillaries 
![500](00%20media/{2206A181-1868-4F01-AA4E-AE4F4E6F6C07}.png)


Blood flows through capillaries because of alternating contraction and relaxation of smooth muscle of metarterioles and precapillary sphincters (5 to 10 times per minute) → **vasomotion**. 

- Three types of capillaries
	- Continuous plasma membranes of endothelial cells form a continuous tube that is interrupted only by intercellular clefts → CNS, lungs, muscle, skin
	- Fenestrated capillaries = small pores and holes → kidneys, small intestine villi, choroid plexuses, ciliary processes of eyes, endocrine glands
	- Sinusoids = wider, more windowed, little to no basement membrane → in the liver, contain phagocytic cells that remove bacteria and other debris 

Portal systems = blood flows from capillary beds in the digestive organs and spleen, through the portal vein, into capillary beds (sinusoids) in the liver, and then drains into hepatic veins to return to the heart (for example). There's also a hypothalamic-hypophyseal portal system. Efficient because there's two capillary beds and efficient transfer (whao).
## Types of veins
- Vascular or venous sinus = endothelial wall with no smooth muscle
- Superficial veins are not accompanied by parallel arteries
- Deep veins = travel between skeletal muscles
- Veins and venules are an important blood reservoir

# Capillary exchanges
## Ways that things get across
### Diffusion
Normally, things like gases and hormones can get across. In sinusoids, we can even have proteins and blood cells get across like in the liver. In contrast, you could have really tight capillaries in the BBB.
### Transcytosis
Enclosed within tiny pinocytic vesicles that first enter endothelial cells by endocytosis, then move across the cell and exit on the other side by exocytosis. For large, lipid-insoluble substances, like insulin or antibodies into fetal circulation. 
### Bulk flow
Lots of stuff in the same direction, useful for solute exchange or changing blood or interstitial fluid volume. Think filtration and reabsorption.
## Filtration and reabsorption 
**Starling's law of capillaries** → volume of filtration = volume of reabsorption

Blood → interstitium is filtration. Influenced by blood hydrostatic pump, pressure from heart hump, interstitial oncotic fluid pressure. Interstitium → blood = reabsorption. 

We also have interstitial fluid hydrostatic pressure, which is close to zero, and blood colloid osmotic pressure, which is mainly caused by proteins like albumin.

Combining these, the net filtration pressure (NFP) = (BHP + IFOP) - (BCOP + IFHP). On the arterial side this is 10 mmHg while it is -9 mmHg on the venous side. 
## Edema
Caused by ↑ capillary BP, ↑ capillary permeability, or ↓ plasma protein levels.
## MAP
Mean arterial pressure = diastolic + 1/3(systolic - diastolic). Heart spends 2/3 of its time in diastole.

$${MAP} = \frac{(P_{\text{systolic}} \times t_{\text{systole}}) + (P_{\text{diastolic}} \times t_{\text{diastole}})}{t_{\text{systole}} + t_{\text{diastole}}}$$
$$\text{If } t_{\text{systole}} : t_{\text{diastole}} \approx 1 : 2,\ \text{then:}$$
$$\text{MAP} = \frac{1}{3} P_{\text{systolic}} + \frac{2}{3} P_{\text{diastolic}}$$
$$\text{MAP} = P_{\text{diastolic}} + \frac{1}{3}(P_{\text{systolic}} - P_{\text{diastolic}})$$
$$\text{At higher heart rates (when } t_{\text{systole}} \approx t_{\text{diastole}}),\ 
\text{MAP} \approx P_{\text{diastolic}} + \frac{1}{2}(P_{\text{systolic}} - P_{\text{diastolic}})$$

In terms of its relationship with cardiac output,
$$CO = \frac{MAP}{R}$$
$$MAP = CO \times R$$

In an exam context, maybe mention that resistance is also reduced when the blood vessels are in parallel. Kirchhoff's circuit laws! 
## Vascular resistance
- Governed by 
	- Size of the lumen → inverse relationship
	- Blood viscosity → think polycythemia
	- Total blood vessel length 
Systemic vascular resistance is mainly governed by the arterioles. 
## Venous return
- Governed by
	- Heart pump
	- Skeletal muscle pump
	- Respiratory pump → diaphragm changing abdominal pressure
