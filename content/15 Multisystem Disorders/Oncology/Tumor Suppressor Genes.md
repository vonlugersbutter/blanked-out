Knudson's two hit hypothesis for the origin of cancer states that 2 mutations are required for the development of cancer, and these are mutations in tumor suppressor genes. This is a loss of heterozygosity, where a heterozygous mutation leads to no disease, while a mutation of both alleles would cause cancer. 

![](Pasted%20image%2020260317161809.png)

Functions of tumor suppressor genes include:
- Intracellular proteins that are crucial in controlling the progression of cell cycle stages - (e.g., pRB and p16)
- Receptors or signal transducers which orchestrate signals that inhibit cell proliferation (like TGF B)
- Checkpoint-control proteins, which are useful in triggering cell cycle arrest in case of DNA damage or chromosomal defects (like BRCA1)
- Proteins useful for apoptosis induction (e.g., p53)
- Proteins involved in DNA repair (MSH2, and p53)

Examples of these mutations include:
- Point mutation = single nucleotide change 
	- RAS gene family, where mutations (e.g., codons 12, 13, 61) impair GTPase activity, keeping the protein constantly active
- Gene amplification = multiple copies of a gene are created, leading to excessive protein production
	- HER2 (breast cancer) or MYC (neuroblastoma) amplification
- Chromosomal translocation = gene is moved to a new chromosomal location, often placing it under the control of a highly active promoter
	- Philadelphia chromosome in Chronic Myelogenous Leukemia (CML)
- Chromosomal rearrangements/fusion = translocations or inversions can create new fusion proteins that are constitutively active
	- Insertion Mutation (Retroviral Insertion) = virus inserts its own genetic material near a proto-oncogene, activating it
- Epigenetic alterations = changes in DNA methylation or histone modification that turn up expression

- Haploinsufficiency = genetic condition where a diploid organism has only one functional copy of a gene (heterozygosity) instead of two
	- Single copy is inadequate to produce enough protein product to maintain normal function
	- Dosage sensitivity leads to a mutant phenotype, often resulting in dominant genetic disorders or cancer predisposition
- Partial haploinsufficiency = one functional copy of a gene is insufficient to produce a normal phenotype, but the deficit is less severe than a complete knockout
