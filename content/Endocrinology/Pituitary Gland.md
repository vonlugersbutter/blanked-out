- Hypothalamus = critical region of the diencephalon located below the thalamus, forming the floor and walls of the third ventricle
- Serves as a command centre, integrating environmental cues, signals from other brain regions, and visceral afferents to maintain homeostasis
- Coordinates physiological responses including energy expenditure, fluid balance, body temperature, and the sleep cycle
- Magnocellular neurons = located in the supraoptic (SON) and paraventricular (PVN) nuclei, these large neurons have long axons forming the hypothalamo-hypophyseal tract
	- Traverses the median eminence and terminates in the posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis), where the hormones oxytocin (OT) and arginine vasopressin (ADH) are released directly into the systemic circulation.
- Parvicellular neurons = smaller neurons project to the median eminence, where they release hypophysiotropic hormones (releasing or inhibiting factors) into the specialized blood supply that carries them to the anterior pituitary
- Median eminence = bridge-like region where the two halves of the hypothalamus rejoin at the floor of the third ventricle
	- Funnels into the infundibular stalk, which connects to the pituitary gland
	- Posterior pituitary is considered an extension of the hypothalamus
- Pituitary sits in the sella turcica and is connected to the hypothalamus via the pituitary stalk
# Blood supply
- Anterior pituitary supply = superior hypophyseal arteries form a primary capillary plexus at the median eminence
	- Hypophysiotropic peptides enter this plexus and are transported via long hypophyseal portal veins down the infundibular stalk to a secondary plexus in the anterior pituitary
	- Secondary plexus consists of fenestrated sinusoid capillaries, allowing neuropeptides to diffuse easily to reach their target cells
- Posterior pituitary supply = middle and inferior hypophyseal arteries
	- Short portal vessels provide venous connections between the neural lobe and the anterior lobe, allowing neuropeptides released from the posterior pituitary to influence cells in the anterior pituitary
- Systemic drainage = blood from both lobes drains into the intercavernous sinus, then into the internal jugular vein
# Hypothalamic nuclei
- Supraoptic Nucleus = responsible for osmoregulation and ECF volume via antidiuretic hormone (ADH), as well as uterine contractions and milk ejection via oxytocin
- Paraventricular Nucleus (PVN) = contains magnocellular neurons for ADH and oxytocin, and parvocellular neurons that secrete TRH (thyroid regulation) and CRH (adrenal and sympathetic regulation)
- Arcuate nucleus = secreting GHRH, GnRH, somatostatin, and dopamine, also appetite regulation
	- Not protected by BBB
	- One population of arcuate neurons expresses POMC to inhibit food intake, while another expresses neuropeptide Y to stimulate intake
	- Peripheral signals that converge on the hypothalamus include leptin, adiponectin, insulin, and gastrointestinal hormones like ghrelin and cholecystokinin
	- Severely obese patients often show impaired GH dynamics
- Suprachiasmatic nucleus = pacemaker (zeitgeber) for circadian rhythms and pineal function
- Ventromedial nucleus = satiety center and is involved in GHRH and somatostatin regulation
- Lateral hypothalamic area = hunger center
- Anterior and posterior hypothalamus = Involved in thermoregulation, with the anterior area acting as a cooling center and the posterior as a heating center
## Hormones
Most of these are very conveniently named. 
- Dopamine = primary prolactin (PRL)-inhibitory hormone; it is found in the portal circulation and has a short half-life of 1 to 2 minutes
- Thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) = tripeptide 
- Corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) = makes ACTH, action is potentiated by ADH and inhibited by oxytocin
- Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) = A linear decapeptide that controls both LH and FSH Its pulsatile release is essential, as continuous infusion leads to receptor downregulation and hormone suppression
- Somatostatin (GHIH) = inhibition of GH secretion
- Growth hormone–releasing hormone (GHRH) 
# Posterior pituitary
- Posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis) = secretes ADH (vasopressin) and oxytocin, which are produced in the hypothalamus → both nonapeptides
- Precursor includes a signal peptide, the hormone, a neurophysin, and (for avp) a glycopeptide called copeptin following cleavage in the er, the precursor moves through the golgi apparatus and is packaged into neurosecretory vesicles for axonal transport
- Neurophysins = by-products of prohormone processing are essential for the proper transport and targeting of hormones to secretory granules
	- Mutations in neurophysins can lead to the retention of precursors in the er, resulting in neuronal cell death and familial neurogenic diabetes insipidus
- Release mechanism = release is triggered by neuronal depolarization, which opens voltage-gated calcium channels at the axon terminals. resulting calcium influx facilitates the docking and exocytosis of vesicle contents into the extracellular space
## Oxytocin
- Principal stimuli = mechanical stimulation of the uterine cervix by the fetus and the suckling of the nipple
- OT binds to gq/11 protein-coupled receptors, activating phospholipase c (plc) this leads to an increase in inositol trisphosphate (ip3) and cytosolic calcium, which activates myosin light chain kinase (MLCK) to produce smooth muscle contraction
- Stimulates milk ejection in the lactating breast and rhythmic uterine contractions during labor it also promotes uterine involution postpartum
- Sensitivity to OT increases dramatically toward the end of pregnancy due to upregulation of ot receptors (mediated by estrogen) and increased gap-junction formation between smooth muscle cells
	- Responsiveness to oxytocin increases dramatically as labor approaches, and its release is further stimulated by the dilation of the cervix (the Fergusson reflex)
- Vasodilators like beta-agonists and NO go against the actions of oxytocin

# Anterior pituitary
- Anterior pituitary (adenohypophysis)
- Embryology = highly vascularized epithelial structure derived from the ectodermal lining of the roof of the mouth
- Consists of the pars anterior and the pars intermedia, separated by a remnant of Rathke’s pouch
- Gonadotrophs (LH, FSH) and somatotrophs (GH) = located primarily in the posterolateral region; their high number makes them more vulnerable to mechanical trauma
- Corticotrophs (ACTH) and thyrotrophs (TSH) = Located predominantly in the anteromedial region, making them more resilient to traumatic injury
- Lactotrophs (prolactin) = Dispersed throughout the gland and are also considered a resilient population
## Hormone classification
- Glycoprotein family = includes TSH, FSH, and LH These are heterodimeric proteins consisting of a common $\alpha$ -subunit and a unique $\beta$ -subunit that confers biological specificity
- Proopiomelanocortin (POMC) family = derived from post-translational cleavage of the precursor pro-hormone POMC into ACTH, $\beta$ -endorphin, and melanocyte-stimulating hormones ($\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$ -MSH)
- GH and prolactin Family = Structurally and genetically similar to human placental lactogen

## LH and FSH (gonadotropins)
- Pulsatile secretion = GnRH must be released in a pulsatile manner to stimulate the pituitary
- Frequency of GnRH pulses determines which gonadotropin is released
	- Slow pulses (90–120 min) = Favor FSH secretion during the luteal-to-follicular transition
	- Fast pulses (60 min) = Favor LH secretion during the mid-to-late follicular phase
- See [Reproductive Hormones](Endocrinology/Reproductive%20Hormones.md)
- Low sex steroids in the presence of inappropriately low LH/FSH levels indicate hypogonadotropic hypogonadism
## Growth hormone
- Growth hormone (GH/somatotropin) = important for linear growth in childhood
	- Stimulated by GHRH and sleep; inhibited by glucose and somatostatin
	- GH stimulates the liver to produce IGF-1 (somatomedin), which mediates growth effects on bone and muscle
		- IGF-Binding Proteins (IGFBPs) = six identified proteins regulate IGF-1 bioavailability; IGFBP-3 carries nearly 80% of circulating IGF-1
	- Direct effects of GH include raising blood sugar (diabetogenic effect) and promoting lipolysis, stimulating amino acid uptake 
	- Pathology = excess GH causes gigantism in children and acromegaly in adults (enlarged jaw, hands, and feet; visceral organ enlargement; insulin resistance)
- Insulin Tolerance Test (ITT) is the gold standard for diagnosing GH deficiency (GHD) Age-stratified IGF-1 levels serve as a reliable serum marker
- Leuprolide is a GnRH agonist → resistant to degradation
	- Initial binding can stimulate LH/FSH release, but chronic will lead to downregulation of the receptor and desensitation
	- Can be used to suppress ovarian follicular growth and ovulation, chemical menopause induction 
		- For endometriosis, precocious puberty, uterine fibroids 
	- Pulsatile administration → stimulates LH/FSH release → used to create LH surge for ovulation in infertility, but we have better treatments now 
- See [Growth and Puberty](Reproduction/Growth%20and%20Puberty.md)
## Prolactin
- Prolactin = regulates milk production; under inhibitory control by dopamine from the hypothalamus
	- Excess prolactin (hyperprolactinemia) can cause galactorrhea and amenorrhea in women, and decreased libido or impotence in men
- Suckling is important stimulus → ↓ dopamine
- Prolactin regulates itself through a short-loop feedback by increasing hypothalamic dopamine synthesis
- High PRL levels may be masked by the "hook effect" in assays Conversely, physiologically inactive macroprolactin can cause falsely elevated readings
# Pituitary disorders
- Hypopituitarism = damage to the anterior pituitary from tumors, ischemia, or hemorrhage
- Sheehan syndrome = pituitary infarction following postpartum hemorrhage
	- Failure to lactate, other pituitary issues 
- Pituitary apoplexy = sudden hemorrhage into the pituitary, often a pre-existing adenoma, causing severe headache and vision loss
- Empty sella syndrome = the sella turcica fills with CSF, occasionally compressing the pituitary
- Hierarchy of loss = pituitary function is typically lost in a specific order = GH > FSH > LH > TSH > ACTH
- Patients with hypothalamic/pituitary masses, prior head irradiation, or significant head trauma should undergo regular screening for insufficiency
## Adenomas
- Size-based classification =
	- Microadenomas = <1 cm
	- Macroadenomas = ≥1 cm to <4 cm
	- Giant Adenomas = ≥4 cm
- Functional vs nonfunctioning
- Functioning adenomas = hypersecrete hormones, and their location often reflects the distribution of corresponding normal pituitary cells 
	- Somatotrophs (GH) make up about 50% of anterior cells, while thyrotrophs (TSH) account for only 5%
- Nonfunctioning adenomas = typically macroadenomas that efface pituitary landmarks and do not produce a clinical hormone excess syndrome
- Lineage-based classification (pathology) = modern classification uses transcription factors to identify cell-type origin
	- PIT1 Lineage = somatotrophs (GH), lactotrophs (PRL), and thyrotrophs (TSH)
	- TPIT Lineage = corticotrophs (ACTH)
	- SF1 Lineage = gonadotrophs (FSH/LH)
	- Null cell adenomas = express no differentiated products or transcription factors and are often more aggressive
- Pituitary adenomas are monoclonal, suggesting an intrinsic pituitary defect rather than surrounding hyperplasia
### Clinical features
- Visual field defects = expanding masses can compress the optic chiasm, typically leading to bitemporal hemianopia 
	- Advanced compression may lead to "hemifield slide" phenomena where the nasal and temporal fields lose linkage
- Lateral extension into the cavernous sinus can disrupt the oculomotor (III), trochlear (IV), abducens (VI), and sensory trigeminal (V1, V2) nerves → cranial nerve palsy
- Direct pressure on hormone-secreting cells or the stalk can compromise hormone synthesis, leading to pituitary failure
- Headaches, CSF rhinorrhea (leaks), and occasionally seizures if the temporal lobe is involved
### Specific pituitary adenomas
- Prolactinomas = most common
	- Hyperprolactinemia causes hypogonadism, amenorrhea, infertility, galactorrhea, and decreased libido
	- Management = primarily medical, using dopamine agonists like cabergoline or bromocriptine
- Acromegaly (GH-Secreting)
	- Visceromegaly (tongue, thyroid, heart), cardiovascular disease, sleep apnea, insulin resistance/diabetes, and oily skin
	- Elevated IGF1 and failure to suppress GH during an OGTT
	- Resection is the primary goal 
	- Medical options include somatostatin receptor ligands (SRLs) (eg, Octreotide, Lanreotide) and GH antagonists (Pegvisomant)
- Thyrotrophin-secreting adenomas = rare tumors causing hyperthyroidism with measurable or high TSH → treated with surgery or SRLs
### General management strategy
- Micro-incidentalomas (<10 mm) = usually followed by expectant observation with serial MRI, as many do not grow or may even decrease in size
- Macroadenomas (≥10 mm) = more than 20% continue to grow, requiring closer monitoring or surgical intervention if they impinge on vital structures
- Silent adenomas = some tumors immunostain for hormones (eg, silent corticotroph or TSH adenomas) but do not hypersecrete them into the circulation
