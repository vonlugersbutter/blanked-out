- Penetrance = proportion with allele that express phenotype
- Expressivity = variations in phenotype of gene
- Pleitropy = one gene with multiple phenotypic effects and traits
- Mosaicism = gene differences in cells of same individual
	- Germline mosaicism can be passed, is difficult to detect 
	- Somatic mosaicism occurs in non-reproductive cells, not passed on 
- Genetic heterogeneity = same phenotype from different genes/mutations
- Allelic heterogeneity = different mutations or variants (alleles) within the same single gene locus cause the same or similar disease or phenotype
- Locus heterogeneity = mutations in different genes (at different chromosomal loci) cause the same disease

See [Oncogenes](Oncogenes.md) and [Tumor Suppressor Genes](Tumor%20Suppressor%20Genes.md). 

- Nondisjunction = chromosome pairs don't separate 
	- See [Down Syndrome](Down%20Syndrome.md)
- Trisomy causes can be suggested by the phenotype 
	- Failure of maternal homologous chromosomes to separate → meiosis I problem
	- Failure of sister chromatids to separate → meiosis II problem
- Uniparental disomy = two copies of one parent chromosome 
	- Isodisomy = one parent, failure of sister chromatids to separate, meiosis II error
	- Heterodisomy = one parent, failure of homologous chromosomes to separate, meiosis I error 
	- Child is going to normal, euploid → but recessive diseases more likely to show up 
- Robertsonian translocation = fusion of long arms of two chromosomes → occurs with acrocentric chromosomes, where the centromere is near the end
	- 13, 14, 21, 22

In the example below, we have a carrier mother with a fused 14-21 long arm chromosome. In a carrier child, there are 45 chromosomes, but are healthy because they retain genetic material. In Down syndrome for this translocation etiology, there are three copies of chromosome 21. Same thing for the trisomy 14 (which is mosaic; germline is lethal). We also have the monosomy 14 and 21, where the fused chromosome is not inherited. If germline and not mosaic, these are lethal (like all monosomies, except for [[Turner Syndrome]]).

- Every chromosome has a short (p) arm and a long (q) arm separated by a centromere
- Common abbreviations
	- del = Deletion
	- inv = Inversion
	- t = Translocation
	- arr = Microarray analysis
	- ish / nuc ish = Fluorescence in situ hybridization (metaphase vs interphase)
- Triploidy = Having three full sets of chromosomes (69 total); phenotypic expression varies based on whether the extra set is of maternal or paternal origin due to imprinting
- Tetraploidy = Four sets of chromosomes (92 total), usually resulting from a failure in early postzygotic cleavage; these pregnancies invariably result in loss
- Translocations = Carriers of balanced translocations are generally phenotypically normal but are at risk of producing offspring with unbalanced translocations, which involve duplications or deletions
- Isochromosomes = Formed when a chromosome splits transversely rather than longitudinally, resulting in two identical arms (either two p or two q arms) This functionally causes trisomy of the retained arms and monosomy of the lost arms
- Inversions
	- Pericentric = Involves the centromere; carriers risk producing offspring with duplication/deletion syndromes
	- Paracentric = Does not involve the centromere; primarily associated with an increased risk of early pregnancy loss
- Detection = Standard karyotypes cannot detect changes smaller than 3 to 5 million base pairs; Chromosomal Microarray Analysis (CMA) is required to identify these genomic copy number variants
- Contiguous Gene Syndromes = Occur when a microdeletion involves multiple adjacent genes, often leading to complex, unrelated phenotypic traits
- Non-Mendelian
	- Mitochondrial = Inherited exclusively from the mother (eg, Leigh syndrome)
	- DNA Triplet Repeat Expansion = Certain genes contain repeats that expand during transmission, leading to anticipation (earlier/more severe symptoms in offspring), such as in Huntington disease or Fragile X syndrome
	- Imprinting = Gene expression is determined by the parent of origin (eg, Angelman syndrome vs Prader-Willi syndrome)
	- Multifactorial = Resulting from gene-nutrient or gene-environment interactions (eg, Neural-tube defects influenced by folic acid intake)
