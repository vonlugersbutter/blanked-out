# Aortic aneurysm
- Aneurysm = abnormal localized dilatation of the artery
- Ectasia = generalized, lesser ↑ in diameter
- Aneurysm has to be ↑ by 50%
- True aneurysm = all three layers bulging
	- Fusiform = symmetrical, common
	- Saccular = localized outpouch
- Pseudoaneurysm = false aneurysm, rupture out of the intima and media, contained by the adventitia, perivascular organized thrombus 
- Causes 
	- Aging, hypertension, connective tissue disorders → cystic medial generation → elastic to collagen and mucoid tissue → aneurysm of ascending aorta
	- Atherosclerosis + genetic disposition + inflammation + ECM breakdown → descending/abdominal aneurysm
	- Other causes
		- Infections
		- Vasculitis
			- Takayasu arteritis
			- Giant cell arteritis 
- Clinical presentation
	- Cough, dyspnea, pneumonia because compression of trachea
	- Dysphagia (esophagus)
	- Hoarseness (recurrent laryngeal nerve)
	- Dilate annular ring → [Aortic Regurgitation](08%20Cardiology/Cardiac%20Pathologies/Valvular%20Diseases/Aortic%20Regurgitation.md) → [Heart Failure](08%20Cardiology/Cardiac%20Pathologies/Heart%20Failure.md)
- Aneurysm can rupture slow or fast, leading to massive bleed and hypotension
- Risk is given by Laplace's law because wall stress 
- Treatment
	- Surgical repair and risk reduction
# Aortic dissection
- Tear in intima, blood spreads through medial layers
- Intramural hematoma, penetrating aortic ulcer, and aortic rupture 
- Etiology 
	- Circumference or transverse tear, rupture of the vasa vasorum
	- Hypertension, aging, everything that causes aortic aneurysm 
- Most common in men 60-70 
- Type A dissection is proximal, more dangerous because it's near heart and coronary circulation
- Type B is distal 
- Clinical presentation
	- Tearing or ripping sudden pain in the anterior chest or between the scapulae, can radiate anywhere in the thorax or abdomen
	- Painless is possible but uncommon 
- Complications
	- Rupture
	- Occlusion of aortic branch vessels
	- Distortion of aortic annulus 
- Often presents with hypertension, could also happen from SNS response to severe pain, diminished renal flow, activation of RAAS system
- Accompanied by neurologic symptoms if it goes to the carotids 
- Treatment
	- Reduce blood pressure
	- Decrease LV contraction
	- Use beta blockers and vasodilators 
	- Use surgical therapy, or insert a synthetic graft 
	- Uncomplicated type B dissections can sometimes be managed through therapy alone 
# Peripheral atherosclerotic vascular disease
- Caused by atherosclerosis 
- Ischemia of affected region, stenosed artery can't provide adequate supply because Poiseuille's law 
- Denervation and dropout of muscle fibers, atrophy due to intermittent ischemia
- If the demands can't even be met at rest → tissue necrosis and gangrene
- Intermittent claudication = exertional limb fatigue and pain
- Prone to infection, ulceration, and necrosis
- Check all the pulses, check for bruits 
- Ischemic ulcers = non-healing sores caused by poor blood flow, most commonly to the legs and feet. The underlying cause is often peripheral artery disease (PAD), where narrowed arteries prevent adequate oxygen and nutrients from reaching the tissues, leading to cell death and tissue damage
- Treatment
	- Atherosclerotic risk factor management
	- Reduce cardiovascular morbidity and mortality with platelet inhibitors like aspirin and clopidogrel 
	- Formal exercise program 
	- Vasodilators and platelet inhibitors
# Acute arterial occlusion
- Paradoxical embolism = when a blood clot from the venous circulation travels to the systemic arterial circulation by passing through a right-to-left heart shunt, such as a patent foramen ovale (PFO)
- Symptoms of abrupt flow reduction
	- Pain
	- Pallor
	- Paralysis
	- Paresthesia = burning or prickling
	- Pulselessness
	- Poikilothermia = coolness   
- Origins of arterial emboli
	- Cardiac origin, like mitral stenosis or valvular lesions in IE
	- Aortic origin
	- Venous origin 
- Atheroembolism = peripheral artery occlusion from more proximal sites, like atherosclerotic lesions or aneurysms
	- Blue tow syndrome, gangrene, necrosis
	- Livedo reticularis (purplish mottling of skin)
	- Kidney failure (renal atheroembolism) 
	- Intestinal ischemia 
# Vasculitis
- Vessel wall inflammation from immune complexes 
- Takayasu arteritis = chronic vasculitis of unknown etiology that targets the aorta and major branches
	- Granulomatous inflammation with lymphocytes, histiocytes, and multinucleated giant cells, resulting in intimal proliferation, disruption of the elastic lamina, and fibrosis
	- Anti-endothelial antibodies might be involved
- Giant cell arteritis or temporal arteritis = medium to large arteries, especially cranial vessels or aortic arch branches
- Thromboangiitis obliterans (Buerger disease) = strong association with smoking, segmental inflammatory disease of small and medium-sized arteries 
	- Some genetic factors 
# Vasospasm: Raynaud phenomenon
- Vasospastic disease of digital arteries, when exposed to cool temperatures or emotional stress
- Temporarily obliterates vascular lumen, inhibiting blood flow
- Starts out white, then cyanosis to blue, then ruddy color as blood flow resumes
- Numbness, paresthesia, or pain 
- Usually women between 20 and 40 
- Secondary Raynaud phenomenon = component of other conditions
	- Connective tissue disorders like SLE 
- Etiology might be exaggerated SNS activity, too much vasoconstrictor stimuli like serotonin
# Venous diseases
- Varicose veins = dilated, tortuous superficial vessel in lower extremities 
- Could also happen in anorectal area, which is hemorrhoids, in the lower esophageal veins (esophageal varices), or spermatic cord (varicocele)
- Intrinsic weakness of vessel wall from ↑ pressure, or congenital defects
- Primary varicose veins start in superficial = caused by pregnancy, prolonged standing, obesity
- Secondary varicose veins = deep venous insufficiency or occlusion or when the perforating veins (which connect the deep and superficial veins) are incompetent
	- Blood shunted retrograde to superficial 
- Treat conservatively, like with avoiding prolonged standing, or wearing compression stockings
# Chronic venous insufficiency
- Occurs when your leg veins don't allow blood to flow back up to your heart
- Valvular incompetence 
# Venous thromboembolism 
- Deep or superficial venous thrombi
- Starts out as platelets and fibrin, and then blood vessels come in, and thrombus starts to propagate 
- Could obstruct blood flow, or break off and become thromboemboli 
- DVT
	- Usually in the calves
	- Caused by stasis of blood flow, hypercoagulability, vascular damage
	- Vascular damage can cause this
	- Less severe damage can cause endothelial dysfunction → disrupt production of vasodilation and antiplatelet substances 
	- Women commonly get this because in the third trimester, fetus compresses IVC and can cause stasis of blood flow
	- Oral contraceptives can also increase thrombus formation
	- Calf or thigh discomfort, unilateral leg swelling 
	- Homan sign = calf pain produced by the dorsiflexion of the foot 
	- Diagnosis = d-dimer, a by-product of fibrin degeneration
	- Treat with elevation of affected extremity, use anticoagulation
- Pulmonary embolism 
	- Usually DVT that goes up and gets into the right heart chambers, and then lodges in the pulmonary vasculature
	- Gas exchange impaired 
	- Use anticoagulation, treat for RV dysfunction 
- Superficial thrombophlebitis = benign disorder associated with inflammation and thrombosis of a superficial vein, just below the skin
# Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI) 
- Non-invasive test that compares the blood pressure in your ankle to the blood pressure in your arm to check for peripheral artery disease (PAD)
