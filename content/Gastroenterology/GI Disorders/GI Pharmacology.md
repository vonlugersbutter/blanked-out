# Antacids
Antacids are over-the-counter therapies frequently utilized to manage symptoms of [[GERD]]. They function by reacting with gastric HCl to neutralize stomach contents.
- Sodium bicarbonate (Alka seltzer) = the reaction ($NaHCo_3 + HCl \leftrightarrow NaCl + H_2O + CO_2$) produces carbon dioxide, which can lead to bloating and belching absorption of bicarbonate
	- Can cause alkalosis, while the resorption of sodium chloride may result in fluid retention
- Calcium carbonate (tums) = the reaction ($CaCO_3 + 2HCl \leftrightarrow CaCl_2 + H_2O + CO_2$) also produces carbon dioxide, leading to bloating and belching
	- Can cause constipation due to decreased gastrointestinal motor activity and may lead to hypercalcemia or alkalosis
	- Specifically used to treat hypocalcemia
- Milk alkali syndrome = this syndrome results from high intake of calcium carbonate and presents as a triad of hypercalcemia, metabolic alkalosis, and renal failure
- Acid rebound = patients may experience a mild surge in acid secretion once the antacid leaves the stomach, likely due to calcium stimulating acid secretion
- Aluminum hydroxide = this antacid does not cause bloating or alkalosis
	- Decreases GI motor activity, leading to constipation because it binds phosphate in the gut, it is used as a "phosphate binder" in patients with renal failure to lower phosphate levels
	- Side effects = excessive use can cause hypophosphatemia, leading to muscle weakness
	- Aluminum toxicity = primarily occurring in renal failure patients, toxicity can cause bone pain, osteomalacia, dementia, and a microcytic [Anemia](Hematology/Anemia.md) that is resistant to iron treatment
- Magnesium hydroxide (milk of magnesia) = this agent does not cause bloating or alkalosis
	- Poorly absorbed and acts as an osmotic laxative, drawing fluid into the colon to promote peristalsis, which often results in diarrhea
	- Toxicity = hypermagnesemia can cause hypotension, bradycardia, and cardiac arrest
- Maalox = this is a combination of magnesium and aluminum hydroxide designed to offset the individual side effects of diarrhea and constipation
- Drug interactions = all antacids can alter the absorption of other drugs by binding to them or increasing gastric pH
	- Key drugs affected include tetracycline, fluoroquinolones, isoniazid, and iron supplements
# Acid suppression therapy
- histamine ($H_2$) blockers = drugs such as famotidine, ranitidine, nizatidine, and cimetidine block histamine receptors in parietal cells
	- Generally well-tolerated, they can cause confusion, particularly in the elderly, and rarely lead to elevated liver enzymes or cardiac arrhythmias (more so older drugs)
- Cimetidine = this first-generation blocker is rarely used now
	- Potent p450 inhibitor
	- Has anti-androgen effects (gynecomastia, impotence), increases prolactin, crosses the blood-brain barrier (dizziness, confusion), and reduces creatinine excretion
- Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) = drugs like omeprazole, pantoprazole, lansoprazole, and esomeprazole irreversibly inhibit the $H^+/K^+$ pump in parietal cells
- Long-term adverse effects = potential risks include *C. difficile* infection and [Pneumonia](Respirology/Respiratory%20Pathologies/Pneumonia.md) due to the loss of protective gastric acid
	- Chronic use is linked to malabsorption of magnesium, calcium (increasing hip fracture risk), B12 (as acid is required to cleave it from protein), iron, and vitamin c

# Mucosal protective agents
- Bismuth salicylate (Pepto-Bismol) = coats ulcers and erosions to protect them from acid the salicylate component 
	- Inhibits prostaglandins to reduce stool frequency in diarrhea, while the bismuth reacts with hydrogen sulfide in the colon to blacken the stools
	- Also has antimicrobial activity against *H. pylori* and is used in "quadruple therapy" alongside a PPI, clarithromycin, and amoxicillin or metronidazole (see [Peptic Ulcer Disease](Gastroenterology/GI%20Disorders/Peptic%20Ulcer%20Disease.md))
- Sucralfate = sulfated polysaccharide combined with aluminum hydroxide
	- Binds to positively charged proteins in ulcers to form a protective barrier against acid, ulcer healing
# Laxatives
- Osmotic laxatives = draw water into the intestines to facilitate bowel movements and are used for constipation or colonoscopy prep
	- Examples include magnesium citrate, polyethylene glycol (MiraLAX), sorbitol, and sodium phosphate
- Sodium polystyrene sulfonate (kayexalate) = cation exchange resin used specifically to bind potassium in hyperkalemia
- Lactulose = a synthetic disaccharide broken down by colonic bacteria into fatty acids this lowers the pH, converting ammonia ($NH_3$) into ammonium ($NH_4^+$), which is trapped in the colon and excreted, thereby lowering plasma ammonia levels in hyperammonemia
- Stimulant laxatives = bisacodyl and senna increase GI motility through poorly understood mechanisms
- Stool softeners = docusate makes the stool soft and slippery
- Laxative abuse = this is seen in cases of bulimia or factitious diarrhea clinical clues include dehydration, hypokalemia, and metabolic acidosis from bicarbonate loss
# Anti-emetics and prokinetics
- 5- $HT_3$ receptor antagonists (ondansetron) = used primarily as an anti-emetic for patients receiving chemotherapy
	- Block serotonin receptors in the medulla's vomiting center and the GI tract
	- Side effects include headache and constipation
- Metoclopramide (Reglan) = a D2 (dopamine) receptor antagonist
	- GI effects = by blocking dopamine's inhibitory effect on acetylcholine, it increases esophageal and gastric motility, making it useful for gastroparesis
	- CNS effects = it blocks the chemoreceptor trigger zone in the medulla to decrease nausea and vomiting
	- Adverse effects = common issues include drowsiness and extrapyramidal symptoms like parkinsonian movements, akathisia, and dystonia
		- Can also elevate prolactin or lower the seizure threshold
	- Contraindications = it should not be used in patients with seizure disorders, Parkinson’s disease, or bowel obstructions
