# Nephrons
- Renal corpuscle = glomerulus (capillary network) + glomerular capsule or Bowman's capsule
- Renal tubule = proximal convoluted tubule, nephron loop or loop of Henle (descending and ascending), and distal convoluted tubule 
- Empties into the collecting duct 
- Most nephrons are **cortical nephrons** → their corpuscles lie mainly in cortex, they have short loops that penetrate only to the outer medulla
	- Receive supply from peritubular capillaries from efferent arterioles
- Some are **juxtamedullary nephrons** → renal corpuscles lie close to the medulla, and they have a long loop that goes down into the deepest regions 
	- Receive supply from peritubular capillaries and vasa recta from efferent arterioles 
	- Their loops have a thin and then thick ascending limb 
- All of it is one single layer of epithelial cells 
- Glomerular filtration = where water and most solutes move into the glomerular capsule and into the renal tubules
- Tubular reabsorption = reabsorption of most water and many useful solutes
- Tubular secretion = removes substances from the blood 
## Glomerulus
![](00%20media/{B7343363-4F30-4438-AC9E-5622DFC09296}.png)
- Bowman's capsule is visceral and parietal layers
	- Visceral = simple squamous epithelial cells called podocytes with foot-like projections (pedicles)
- Juxtaglomerular apparatus = juxtaglomerular cells (renin and angiotensin II-producing cells of the afferent arteriole) + macula densa + glomerular mesangial cells 
	- Regulates blood pressure in the kidneys 
- Fluid enters the space = glomerular filtrate, usually a filtration fraction of 16-20%
- Mesangium
	- Mesangial cells are contractile and help regulate blood flow → when relaxed, filtration is high
		- Also secrete proinflammatory cytokines, and exhibit phagocytic activity 
		- Mesangial cells outside the glomerulus are called extraglomerular mesangial cells 
	- Mesangial matrix 
- Efferent arteriole is smaller than afferent, so resistance to outflow of blood from the glomerulus is high → pressure in the glomerular capillaries is higher than other capillaries
- Filtration membrane
	- Fenestrations of glomerular endothelial cells
	- Basement membrane of glomerulus
	- Slit membrane between pedicles of podocytes 
		- Lots of proteins here, like nephrin, NEPH-1, podocin 
- Filtration pressures
	- Moving things into the glomerulus
		- Glomerular blood hydrostatic pressure 
	- Moving things out of the glomerulus
		- Capsular hydrostatic pressure
		- Blood colloid osmotic pressure 
- NFP = GBHP - CHP - BCOP

This can be expressed in a needlessly complicated fashion using the formulas we saw in [Normal Fluid Physiology](11%20Nephrology/Normal%20Fluid%20Physiology.md). Note that $\pi_{BS}$ is almost always zero. 
$$
GFR = K_f[(P_{GC}-P_{BS})-\sigma(\pi_{GC}-\pi_{BS})]
$$
- Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) = amount of filtrate formed in all renal corpuscles per minute

Basically, whatever goes into the kidney, if it's not being made or modified, is going to either come out through the pee or through the venous drainage. To express this in a needlessly complicated way that reflects how medical schools seem to want their students to pretend like they're very good at math,
$$
P_x^a \times RPF^a = (P_x^v \times RPF^v) + (U_x+\dot{V})
$$
This is the principle of renal clearance. We can emphasize the urine clearance part of it.
$$
C_x=\frac{U_x \times \dot{V}}{P_x^a}
$$

We can measure this using creatinine, which isn't really reabsorbed after being filtered out.
$$
GFR=\frac{U_{Cr} \times \dot{V}}{P_{Cr}}
$$
I think this is the same thing as inulin, which is mentioned in [Evaluation of Kidney Function](11%20Nephrology/Evaluation%20of%20Kidney%20Function.md).

$$
filtration \; fraction = \frac{GFR}{RPF}
$$
We can also measure RBF, which is derived from flow equations, think Ohm's law.
$$
RBF=\frac{aortic \; pressure - renal \; venous \; pressure}{renal \; vascular \; resistance}
$$
#### Things that regulate GFR
- Adjusting blood flow in and out of glomerulus
- Altering glomerular capillary surface available 
- Myogenic mechanism = stretching causes contraction of smooth muscle cells in afferent arterioles
	- BP ↑ → GFR ↑ → afferent arteriole constriction → GFR ↓ 
- Tubuloglomerular feedback 
	- GFR ↑ → less time for tubular reabsorption → macula densa cells detect Na+, Cl-, water and increase their uptake of this through Na+/K+/2Cl- symporter → ↑ ATP and ↑ adenosine 
	- ATP binds to P2X and adenosine binds to the adenosine A1 receptor → both ↑ Ca++ → constriction of the afferent arteriole 
	- Inhibit nitric oxide synthetase, to allow for vasoconstriction 
	- Inhibit renin production, which would decrease blood pressure and decrease GFR 
- Neural regulation of GFR
	- SNS → NE → vasoconstriction through $\alpha_1$ receptors (lots in afferent arterioles) → constriction and ↓ GFR 
- Hormonal regulation
	- Angiotensin II = potent vasoconstrictor, preferentially acts on the efferent arteriole, increases GFR in the face of ↓ renal perfusion pressure to maintain it at normal 
	- Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) = caused by stretching of the atria, relaxes glomerular mesangial cells, ↑ GFR 
	- Bradykinins = ↑ RBF, ↑ GFR
	- During pathology, prostaglandins can also be produced locally to ↑ RBF without changing GFR
		- Vasoconstriction ↓ by inhibiting effects of SNS and angiotensin II
		- This is why NSAIDs are contraindicated in renal ischemia and hemorrhagic shock 
		- Traditional NSAIDs inhibit both COX-1 and COX-2, while selective COX-2 inhibitors (coxibs) predominantly block COX-2. Inhibition of COX-2 in vascular endothelium suppresses prostacyclin, a vasodilator and inhibitor of platelet aggregation, without affecting thromboxane A₂ (a pro-thrombotic agent produced by platelets via COX-1). This shift favors vasoconstriction and thrombosis, increasing the risk of myocardial infarction and stroke

## Renal tubule
- Simple cuboidal epithelial cells
- Reabsorbed substances: glucose, AAs, urea, Na+, K+, Ca++, Cl-, HCO3-, HPO4 2-
- Secreted: H+, K+, NH4+, creatinine, drugs 
- Types of movement
	- Paracellular reabsorption
	- Transcellular reabsorption
- Obligatory water reabsorption = water follows the solutes
- Facultative water reabsorption = regulated by ADH in the collecting ducts 
- Passive transport/diffusion = solutes moving from high to low concentration
	- Could occur based on chemical concentration gradient and/or electrical potential difference
- Solvent drag = solute that follows water during osmotic pressure gradients → really important int he PCT 
- Endocytosis = pinching off and forming vesicles, for small proteins and macromolecules at PCT, requires ATP 
- Tight junctions = integral membrane proteins, occludins, claudins, and immunoglobulin proteins
	- Allows some ions to get through 
### Proximal convoluted tubule 
- Most solute reabsorption involves Na+ here 
- The Na+/K+ ATPase is exclusively at the basolateral side, so Na+ goes into the blood, and K+ goes into the cell → so now Na+ can move in down its concentration gradient from the apical side
- Note the sodium glucose symporter SGLT2 on the apical side, and then the glucose gets transported across with the GLUT2 transporter 
	- This is why SGLT2 inhibitors are given to ↓ glucose reabsorption in pt with T2DM 

![400](00%20media/{91AE9FE6-096C-4CFE-A1C8-33C5D490EE9A}.png)

- And the Na+/H+ antiporter, called the NHE3, which also allows for reabsorption of HCO3-
	- The Na+/HCO3- symporter is NBC1
	- Carbonic anhydrase is in the middle converting CO2 + H2O → H+ + HCO3-

![400](00%20media/{295844E7-9280-4ED1-88A3-44B63DBD741C}%201.png)

- Na+ transport is also coupled to amino acids, Pi, and lactate, which each have their own symporters, and then they have their own transporters across the basolateral side, very similar to glucose
- Ca++ and K+ transport in the PCT is paracellular, and usually occurs through solvent drag 
- That's how it all happens in the first half of the PCT
	- Changes in Na+ plasma concentrations can be huge in determining the reabsorption of other solutes and substances! 
- In the first half of the PCT, there is a negative transepithelial voltage that gets formed, which allows Cl- to start moving paracellular along its electrical gradient in the second half
- The osmolality on the basolateral side is > than the tubular side, so water reabsorption will occur through AQP-1 and tight junctions 
	- There are also aquaglyceroporins, which are permeable to both water and glycerol 
	- It is greater than Cl- reabsorption in the first half, so at this stage, the Cl- concentration will increase in this first half
	- Cl- starts leaving in the second half, 
	- Koeppen says this in the worst way possible 
- When water leaves, in the second half of the PCT, Cl-, K+, Ca++, Mg++, and urea also diffuse into the capillaries 
	- Cl- leaves the most, so it also creates an electrical gradient for the cations to follow 

- Below is the second half of the PCT 
![400](00%20media/{40078373-C319-4D04-942C-EB94DA5CD248}.png)
- Now, we get reabsorption of Na+ with Cl- as well
	- Na+ gradient is established using the Na+/K+ ATPase
	- Cl- moves down its concentration gradient on the apical side, together with Na+ 
	- This also allows H+ and bases (could be oxalate, formate, bicarbonate) to be secreted
	- Cl- is reabsorbed into the blood through KCC, K+/Cl- symporter, and a Cl- channel on the basolateral side 

- Ammonia is a poisonous waste product derived from the deamination of AAs, a reaction that occurs mainly in hepatocytes → convert most of this ammonia to urea, a less-toxic compound
	- Urea and ammonia both filtered at the glomerulus, secreted by PCT into tubular fluid 
	- PCT cells can produce more NH3 by deaminating glutamine, also generating HCO3-
	- Na+/H+ antiporters can also use ammonium 
- About 65% of the water is reabsorbed here 
- Protein reabsorption also occurs in the PCT
	- Happening through multiligand endocytic receptors with megalin and cubilin (part of the LDL family) 
	- Proteinuria is usually a problem with the glomerular filtration barrier 
	- But if there are trace amounts, it's going to be either that the glomerular filtration rate > reabsorption in the PCT, or it's the secretion of Tamm-Horsfall glycoprotein in the thick ascending LoH
- A lot of organic compounds are normally bound to proteins in plasma, so they can't be filtered → so, they're secreted in the PCT
	- This includes xenobiotics, toxic compounds
	- And, these compounds compete with each other for the secretory pathways
	- So giving multiple compounds that use the same pathways in pt can ↑ half-life and ↑ drug toxicity
	- Penicillin was given with hippurates for this reason → ↑ the half-life of the drug

### Loop of Henle
- Reabsorbs 15% of water and a whole bunch of ions
#### Thin descending limb
- High water permeability, low ion permeability
#### Thin ascending limb
- Low water permeability, high ion permeability 
#### Thick ascending limb
- Low water permeability 
- Apical membrane cells have Na+/K+/2Cl- symporters, while the basolateral membrane still has the Na+/K+ ATPase
- ROMK (renal outer medullary potassium) is on the apical membrane
- Voltage across the thick ascending membrane → the tubular fluid is positive compared to the blood 
- Increased NaCl transport increases the magnitude of the positive voltage → that serves as a driving force for Na+, K+, Mg++, and Ca++ reabsorption
- Distal segment of ascending limb is called diluting segment → produces fluid that is dilute relative to plasma 
- Bartter syndrome = autosomal recessive diseases with hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, hyperaldosteronism
	- Inactivating mutations in the NKCC2, ROMK, and CICNKB (basolateral Cl- channel) that decrease NaCl and K+ reabsorption in the thick ascending limb 
	- Fall in ECF volume causes aldosterone secretion

### Distal convoluted tubule
- 80% of the water has already been reabsorbed
- Initial segment reabsorbs Na+, Cl-, and Ca++, but is impermeable to water
	- Through the NCC transporter 
	- Already, the urine is getting more dilute
- Gitelman syndrome involves mutations of the NCC gene, and thiazide diuretics work to inhibit this transporter

## Ducts
- Two types of cells in the last part of the DCT and the collecting duct, both simple cuboidal 
	- Principal cells = receptors for ADH and aldosterone
		- Reabsorb Na+ and secrete K+ 
		- Use the Na+/K+ ATPase 
		- ENaCs (epithelial Na+-selective channels) 
			- Liddle syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by gain-of-function mutations in the genes encoding the subunits of the Epithelial Sodium Channel (ENaC)
		- Na+ reabsorption generates a negative luminal voltage, provides the driving force for the Cl- paracellular reabsorption 
		- Water reabsorption here is mediated by arginine vasopressin (AVP, aka ADH), which stimulates AQP2 water channels in the apical membrane and AQP3 and AQP4 located on the basolateral side
	- Intercalated cells = homeostasis of blood pH 
		- Reabsorb HCO3- and secrete H+

## Dilute versus concentrated urine 

![400](00%20media/{8A5E0113-348D-48F0-8990-200D5149C990}.png)

![](00%20media/{1D111C73-F964-4232-BA6F-2DA14A882F8D}.png)

- Ability of ADH to cause excretion of concentrated urine depends on osmotic gradient of solutes in the interstitial fluid of the renal medulla 
- Two factors towards osmotic gradient
	- Differences in solute and water permeability and reabsorption
	- Countercurrent flow of fluid through structures in renal medulla 
		- Countercurrent multiplication 
			- The long loop of Henle are the countercurrent multiplier 
			- Symporters in thick ascending limb cause buildup of Na+ and Cl- in the renal medulla
			- Countercurrent flow through the descending and ascending limbs of the loop establishes osmotic gradient in the renal medulla
			- Cells in the collecting ducts reabsorb more water and urea
			- Urea recycling causes a buildup of urea in the renal medulla 
		- Countercurrent exchange
			- Vasa recta is the countercurrent exchanger
- Fluid coming out of the PCT is iso-osmotic to plasma 
- Making hypo-osmotic (dilute) urine → diuresis
	- Reabsorb solute, not water
	- Happens in ascending limb, a bit of descending limb, collecting duct and DCT (without ADH)
	- Water entering descending limb is iso-osmotic
	- Water reabsorbed from thin descending limb via AQP1
	- Terminal descending thin limb and all of ascending thin limb are impermeable to water, but they do express CLCK1 for Cl- reabsorption and Na+ reabsorption paracellularly → this all dilutes the urine
	- Thick ascending limb actively reabsorbs NaCl, further dilution down to hypoosmotic to plasma → this is the diluting segment of the kidney 
	- DCT and cortical collecting duct reabsorb NaCl → without ADH, water doesn't get reabsorbed 
	- Urine ends up having an osmolality as low as 50 mOsm/kg H2O 
![400](00%20media/{C5C505EE-0887-494A-9DC4-062399EADAA1}.png)
- Making hyper-osmotic (concentrated) urine → antidiuresis 
	- Reabsorb solute, not water
	- Happens in ascending limb, a bit of descending limb, collecting duct and DCT (without ADH)
	- Water entering descending limb is iso-osmotic
	- Water reabsorbed from thin descending limb via AQP1
	- Terminal descending thin limb and all of ascending thin limb are impermeable to water, but they do express CLCK1 for Cl- reabsorption and Na+ reabsorption paracellularly → this all dilutes the urine
	- Thick ascending limb actively reabsorbs NaCl, further dilution down to hypoosmotic to plasma → this is the diluting segment of the kidney
	- BUT all of this means that the interstitium has a very high concentration of solutes 
	- Note that ADH stimulates NaCl reabsorption by the thick ascending limb, which maintains this medullary interstitium osmolality 
	- When ADH is present → water permeability of the DCT and collecting duct because of insertion of AQP2 ↑ → water diffuses out of the tubule lumen, osmolality goes way up 
	- Can get up to 1200 mOsm/kg H2O, with high concentrations of urea and nonreabsorbed solutes 
- There's lots of urea and NaCl in the medullary interstitium, with more as you go away from the cortex 
- Urea accumulation ↑ when antidiuresis in medullary interstitium
- And conversely, dilute urine → ↓ osmolality of the medullary interstitium because of ↓ urea
	- Washout by vasa recta
		- Vasa recta are capillary networks → bring nutrients and O2, remove water and solutes
		- More vasa recta blood flow → ↓ medullary interstitial gradient → ↓ ability to concentrate the urine 
	- Urea recycling
		- Urea goes out from the collecting duct, into the interstitium
		- Then, it goes back into the thin descending limb of the LoH
		- Then it goes into the collecting duct through normal flow
		- And the process repeats 
		- That allows the accumulation of a little bit of urea in the interstitium to again, drive more water flow out and keep medullar interstitium osmolality ↑ 
		- Most of it gets secreted out, because of the maintenance of this high gradient

# Water loss and thirst
- Insensible water loss = fluid loss from the body that is not noticeable or easily measured, occurring continuously through diffusion from the skin and evaporation from the respiratory tract
- Positive versus negative water balance = a positive water balance occurs when fluid intake is greater than fluid loss, leading to fluid retention or overload. In contrast, a negative water balance happens when fluid output exceeds intake, resulting in dehydration
- When Na+ concentration is disturbed, it's usually an issue with water
- P<sub>osm</sub> ↑ → neurological issues because of swelling of the brain cells

# Regulation of tubules
## RAAS system
### Renin
- Blood pressure ↓ → less stretch of arterioles → juxtaglomerular (granular) cells (particularly afferent arteriole) secrete renin → coverts angiotensinogen from liver into angiotensin I → ACE from vascular endothelial cells (especially in the lungs) converts it to angiotensin II 
- Perfusion pressure, SNS activity, delivery of NaCl to macula densa (regulating GFR through the tubuloglomerular feedback)
- Decrease in intracellular Ca++ and ↑ cAMP → renin release 
	- So anything that ↑ Ca++ intracellular (like stretch of the afferent arteriole, angiotensin II, and endothelin) → ↓ renin 
- ACE also degrades bradykinin, which is a potent vasodilator 
### Angiotensin II
- GFR ↓ through vasoconstriction of afferent arterioles, but also preferentially acts on the efferent arteriole, increases GFR in the face of ↓ renal perfusion pressure to maintain it at normal
- Enhances reabsorption of Na+ and water in PCT through more activity of Na+/H+ antiporters
- Makes adrenal cortex make aldosterone → stimulates principal cells → reabsorb more Na+, secrete K+ 
- Arteriolar vasoconstriction 
- Stimulates release of aldosterone 

![400](00%20media/{CC0F4552-083F-427D-9D71-46E9EC953664}.png)

### Aldosterone
- Synthesized by the glomerulosa cells of the adrenal cortex
- Stimulates the reabsorption of NaCl by the thick ascending limb of the LoH, late distal tubule, collecting duct 
- All these parts are called the aldosterone-sensitive distal nephron (ASDN)
- Increasing the amount of Na+/K+ ATPase 
- Increasing amount of ENaC channels 
- Elevating serum glucocorticoid-stimulated kinase SGK1
- Stimulating channel-activating protease (CAP1) → serine protease that activates ENaC

Note that during volume depletion, we have RAAS activation, which leads to aldosterone production. This leads to ↓ NaCl excretion but not that much K+ excretion. In this state, angiotensin II → WNK → ↓ K+ secretion in the principal cells by ROMK and ↑ NaCl reabsorption in the early DCT through ↑ NCC → ↓ Na+/Cl- in cell → ↓ K+ secretion. 
- Also, aldosterone → SGK1 → ↑ ENaCs → ↑ Na+ reabsorption

But in hyperkalemia, we have aldosterone production without RAAS activation → SGK1 → ↓ WNK → ↑ ROMK → ↑ K+ secretion, but no change in NaCl secretion. This is the aldosterone paradox. 

![400](00%20media/{E9A88A3B-CEAA-4C3A-B775-17DA406E9B7B}.png)

#### Pseudo-hyperaldosteronism
- Kidneys reabsorb less NaCl as if there was low aldosterone, so we see hyperkalemia and hyponatremia, even if there is a lot of aldosterone
	- Mutations in ENaC or mineralocorticoid receptors (NR3C2)
#### Aldosterone/mineralocorticoid escape
In ↓ aldosterone → ↓ Na+ reabsorption by ASDN → ↑ NaCl lost in the urine → ↓ ECF volume. But, when ↑ aldosterone → ↑ BP, but Na+ retention does not occur! The kidneys escape from this extra Na+ reabsorption.

Why? Pressure natriuresis. Allegedly, ↑ BP → ↓ Na+ reabsorption in the PCT, ↓ Na+/Cl- symporter in the DCT, ↓ ATP which means ↓ ENaC → natriuresis even when aldosterone is ↑. 

Pressure natriuresis = process where increased renal perfusion pressure causes the kidneys to increase sodium excretion, helping to lower blood pressure and extracellular fluid volume

### ADH or vasopressin
- Synthesized in neuroendocrine cells located in supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus
- Stored in the posterior pituitary → secretion happens through stimulation of anterior hypothalamus osmoreceptors → only respond to effective osmoles (so not urea)
- ADH has a very quick degradation 
- Things that make ADH ↑ (make you lose less volume)
	- Osmolality ↑ 
		- Set point is the point at which ↑ ADH, and this happens pretty rapidly
		- ~280 mOsm/kg H2O 
		- This differs biologically, and can change in pregnancy
			- Relaxin and chorionic gonadotropin change the set point 
			- Osmolality of the mother's fluids ↓ 
	- Low volume/pressure
		- LA + pulmonary vessels mainly detect changes in volume, while aortic arch and carotid sinus mainly detect changes in pressure
		- These are baroreceptors 
	- Nausea
	- Nicotine
	- Angiotensin II
- Things that make ADH ↓ (make you lose more volume)
	- ANP
	- Ethanol 
- Actions of ADH
	- V2 receptor 
		- Increases water permeability of principal cells in DCT and collecting duct
			- Principal cells have aquaporin-2, and ADH makes them go into the apical membranes from preformed vesicles 
			- Hypercalcemia and hypokalemia also ↓ AQP2
			- [Heart Failure](08%20Cardiology/Cardiac%20Pathologies/Heart%20Failure.md) and pregnancy also ↓ AQP2
		- Permeability to urea ↑ 
			- ADH → AC → cAMP/PKA → ↑ UT1 and UT3 → ↑ urea permeability in the collecting duct 
		- NaCl reabsorption ↑ 
			- Na/K/Cl symporter in the loop of Henle ↑ 
			- NaCl symporter in the DCT ↑ 
			- ENaC in DCT ↑ 
	- V1 receptor
		- Vasoconstriction → ↑ BP
		- Pressure in the peritubular capillaries ↑ → ↓ Na+ reabsorption 
- ADH ↑↑ = central/pituitary diabetes insipidus
	- Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus is mutations in V2 or AQP2 genes 
- ADH ↓↓ = SIADH
	- Caused by infections, cancers of the brain
	- Drugs
	- Conivaptan and tolvaptan are used for treatment 
- Nephrogenic syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis = gain of function V2 mutations leading to constitutive activation
- Depends on hydration level 
	- Changes in osmolality are more potent in producing thirst response than blood volume or pressure
	- Thirst stimulated when ADH secretion already happening because set point is later
	- Oropharyngeal + upper GI receptors respond short-term to relief of thirst

### Atrial natriuretic peptide 
- ↓ aldosterone, ADH 
- ECF volume + myocardial wall stress → ↑ ANP by atrial walls and ↑ BNP (brain natriuretic peptide, cleverly named because it was found in the brain and no one thought to change the name after learning about its function) by cardiac ventricles → ↓ TPR and ↑ excretion of NaCl + H2O by ↑ RBF + ↑ GFR 
- Urodilatin is another natriuretic peptide made by the kidneys → works in a paracrine fashion 
- Neprilysin breaks down ANP and BNPs, and other vasoactive compounds like enkephalins and bradykinins 
- Natriuretic peptides in general
	- Afferent arteriole vasodilation, efferent vasoconstrict
	- Renin ↓ 
	- Inhibition of aldosterone secretion 
	- Inhibition of ADH secretion

### Other stimuli
- Ca++ ↓ → parathyroid hormone → stimulate reabsorption from DCT of Ca++ and promotes phosphate secretion
- Catecholamines are going to stimulate NaCl and water reabsorption
- Dopamine → ↓ NaCl and water reabsorption
- Glomerulotubular balance = a local renal mechanism where the proximal tubule reabsorbs a constant fraction of the glomerular filtrate, despite changes in the glomerular filtration rate
	- If the GFR ↑ → oncotic pressure in the peritubular capillaries ↑ because more fluid was taken out → stronger gradient → greater reabsorption of fluid and solutes 
- Sympathetic nerve fibers innervate the afferent and efferent arterioles, and nephrons
	- Negative Na+ balance (ECF volume contraction) → baroreceptors → ↑ SNS input
	- Afferent arterioles constrict in response to $\alpha$ -adrenergic stimulation → ↓ GFR 
	- Renin secretion because of $\beta$ -adrenergic stimulation 
### Responses to K+ changes
- Plasma K+ ↑ → insulin ↑, aldosterone ↑, epinephrine ↑ 
	- High SNS activity → ↓ K+ (this is why K+ can go down rapidly after MI)
	- Insulin causes shift of K+ into cells, which can also cause hypokalemia 
- Acidosis → ↑ H+ → means that K+ has to move out of cells → ↑ plasma K+
	- Inhibits Na+/K+ ATPase 
	- Reduces apical permeability to K+

# Calcium and phosphate
![500](00%20media/{B9BEA401-5DA1-4EC4-B2A2-E858FC811635}.png)
- Ca++ and P involved in normal bone remodeling
- Growth and pregnancy → ↓ excretion of Ca++ and Pi
- Chronic renal failure → Pi accumulation
- Acidemia → ↑ %Ca++ ionized vs bound to proteins
- Alkalemia → ↓ %Ca++ ionized → tetany
- Hypoalbuminemia → ↑ %Ca++ ionized vs bound to proteins
- GI absorbs Ca++ through transport stimulated by calcitriol, which is an active metabolite of vit D3 (made in the PCT)
	- Skin (UV light) and diet → vit D3 → converted to calcifediol in liver → converted to calcitriol in the kidneys 
- Ca++ plasma ↑ → calcium-sensing receptor (CSR) in parathyroid gland → ↓ PTH release
- PTH ↑ → bone resorption, Ca++ reabsorption in DCT, ↑ calcitriol → ↑ Ca++
- Hypoparathyroidism → ↓ PTH → ↓ Ca++ leading to hypocalcemic tetany 
- Parathyroid hormone-related peptide (made by cancers) → hypercalcemia
- Thiazide diuretics → ↓ Na++ reabsorption by DCT and ↑ Ca++ reabsorption
	- This is why these can be given for kidney stones with Ca++
- TRPV5 is upregulated with calcitriol
