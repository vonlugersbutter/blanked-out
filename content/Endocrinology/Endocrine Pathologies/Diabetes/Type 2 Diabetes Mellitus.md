# Etiology

![](00%20media/{915E0FE6-4E8B-4D8F-93DC-C01942DCCE6C}.png)

The pathogenesis of T2D is a complex interaction between genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors there are four cardinal abnormalities consistently demonstrated in persons with T2D

## Insulin resistance and other mechanisms
- Insulin resistance = impaired insulin action in peripheral tissues, specifically muscle, fat, and the liver
	- FFA → activated serine tyrosine kinases → can't be phosphorylated
	- TNF alpha also activates this, again can't phosphorylate this 
	- Prevent GLUT4 from going into the membrane through DAG translocation 
	- Ectopic lipid accumulation = when fat storage capacity in adipose tissue is exceeded, lipids accumulate in the liver and muscle this creates toxic metabolites like diacylglycerols (DAG) and ceramides that disrupt insulin signaling
	- Inflammation = obesity triggers the infiltration of proinflammatory macrophages (m1 type) into adipose tissue these cells release cytokines like TNF $\alpha$ and IL6, which activate kinases that inhibit the insulin receptor and IRS proteins
	- Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress = overnutrition causes the er to accumulate misfolded proteins, triggering the unfolded protein response (UPR), which further contributes to inflammation and insulin resistance
	- Mitochondrial dysfunction = a reduction in mitochondrial oxidative capacity and altered enzyme activity (due to acetylation) is often seen in insulin-resistant states
	- Gut microbiome = alterations in gut bacteria can lead to the release of endotoxins and changes in metabolic products (like short-chain fatty acids), which integrate with host genetics to drive resistance
- Defective insulin secretion = failure of pancreatic $\beta$ -cells to secrete enough insulin, particularly in response to a glucose stimulus
	- Because of inflammatory markers and apoptosis of the cells 
- Decreased glucose uptake = reduced ability of muscle and fat to take up glucose, leading to postprandial (after-meal) hyperglycemia
- Increased hepatic glucose production = the liver produces excessive glucose, causing fasting hyperglycemia
- Histology shows amyloid deposits (amylin) in the islets → it is a byproduct of insulin production
## Genetic issues 
- Polygenic nature = common T2D involves over 700 linked genes, though individual variants typically have a modest effect (odds ratios of 110 to 145)
- Key identified genes
	- TCF7l2 = has the greatest impact of common variants; associated with reduced glucose-stimulated insulin secretion
	- KCNJ11 and ABCC8 = encode subunits of the $\beta$ -cell $K_{ATP}$ channel; mutations are a common cause of neonatal diabetes
	- PPARG = encodes PPAR $\gamma$, a master regulator of lipid and glucose homeostasis; the Pro12Ala mutation is associated with a decreased risk of T2D
- Epigenetic risk = perinatal nutrition, both overnutrition and undernutrition, can create epigenetic marks in a fetus that increase the risk of obesity and T2D for generations
## Special conditions
- Visceral adiposity = central (abdominal) fat is more lipolytically active and more strongly linked to insulin resistance than subcutaneous fat
	- Subcutaneous fat has more insulin receptors 
- Circadian disruption = experimental sleep disruption and late-night feeding patterns directly impair insulin action and alter appetite-regulating hormones
- Gestational diabetes = driven by placental factors (eg, growth hormone, lactogen, and miRNAs) that induce insulin resistance in the mother
- Drug-induced resistance
	- Glucocorticoids (eg, prednisone) = increase hepatic glucose production and muscle atrophy
	- HIV medications = protease inhibitors can inhibit glucose transport and interfere with adipocyte differentiation
	- Other drugs = statins, antipsychotics (causing weight gain), and mTOR inhibitors used in transplants
- Postoperative stress = surgery triggers a release of epinephrine and cortisol, which causes rapid insulin resistance and can lead to severe postoperative hyperglycemia

# Epidemiology
- Predominance = T2D is the most common form of diabetes globally, accounting for 90% to 95% of all cases
- Global burden = in 2021, an estimated 537 million adults had diabetes; this is projected to rise to 738 million by 2045
- Growth trends = while currently most prevalent in high-income countries, the fastest growth in new cases is expected in low- to middle-income countries
- Costs = global diabetes-related health expenditures reached $966 billion USD in 2021 in the us, individuals with diabetes have average health expenditures 23-fold higher than those without the disease
- Prediabetes = in the US, approximately 38% of adults (96 million people) have prediabetes, placing them at high risk for progression to T2D
- Global prevalence = type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a massive public health challenge, with the international diabetes federation (IDF) estimating 5356 million cases globally in 2021
- Dominance of T2D = t2d accounts for approximately 90% of all diabetes cases worldwide
- Morbidity and mortality = t2d is a leading cause of blindness and accounts for at least 40% of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) in the us
- Life expectancy = having diabetes reduces life expectancy by approximately 10 years, with a significantly higher hazard ratio for death in those diagnosed before age 55
# Clinical features
- Polydipsia
- Polyuria
- Nocturia
- Numbness of the extremities
- Visual deficits 
# Investigations
- Hyperglycemia thresholds = diagnosis is based on thresholds of fasting plasma glucose (FPG), 2-hour plasma glucose after an oral load (2-hr PG), or glycated hemoglobin (hba1c) that predict retinopathy
- Diabetes criteria
	- Fasting plasma glucose $\ge$ 126 mg/dl (70 mmol/l)
	- 2-hr plasma glucose $\ge$ 200 mg/dl (111 mmol/l)
	- HbA1C $\ge$ 65% (48 mmol/mol)
- Prediabetes criteria
	- Fasting plasma glucose 100–125 mg/dl
	- 2-hr plasma glucose 140–199 mg/dl
	- HbA1C 57%–64%
- Screening recommendations = testing should be considered for all adults at age 35–45, or earlier for overweight/obese individuals with risk factors like high-risk ethnicity, family history, or hypertension
- Follow up HbA1C → once every 3 months, half life of RBC
- Optometrist once per year, lipid panel and uACR once per year
# Prognosis
- Hyperkalemia here is not good → indicates that the potassium is not being filtered out 
## Acute complications
- Hypoglycemia = symptoms include tachycardia, sweating, and tremors (at ~54 mg/dl), progressing to confusion and headache (~50 mg/dl) and eventually seizures or coma
- Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) = driven by low insulin and high glucagon, catecholamines, and cortisol this leads to high levels of ketone bodies (3- $\beta$ -hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate, and acetone)
	- More common in [Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus](Endocrinology/Endocrine%20Pathologies/Diabetes/Type%201%20Diabetes%20Mellitus.md)
	- Treatment involves IV fluids, insulin, monitor potassium levels 
	- Cerebral edema → too much fluid going into the cells, because of excessive fluid correction 
- Hyperglycemic-hyperosmolar nonketotic coma = occurs if osmolarity exceeds 330 mOsm/l due to extreme hyperglycemia
	- More common in Type 2
	- Extreme hyperglycemia ($> 1000\text{ mg/dl}$), severe dehydration
	- High serum osmolarity without significant ketoacidosis
	- Cerebral encephalopathy → too much water coming out because of higher glucose
- Both DKA and HHS are caused by things like infection, illness → increase cortisol, growth hormone, glucagon secretion, epinephrine

## Chronic complications
- Involve damage to the vascular system, peripheral nerves (neuropathy), skin, and eyes (blindness)
- Macrovascular disease (infarction/stroke) is more likely in type 2
- Non-enzymatic glycation = high glucose leads to the formation of advanced glycosylation end products (ages), which cause atherosclerosis (macroangiopathy) and damage to renal arterioles (microangiopathy)
- Diabetic kidney disease = features efferent arteriole hyaline arteriosclerosis (leading to hyperfiltration) and Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules (nodular glomerulosclerosis)
- Sorbitol accumulation = through the polyol pathway, chronic hyperglycemia leads to sorbitol buildup, causing osmotic damage this is a major factor in the development of cataracts, retinopathy, and neuropathy
	- Sorbitol accumulates in the Schwann cells, causing osmotic damage → affects the longest axons first
# Treatment 
![](00%20media/{4AAC29DC-FB7C-4250-9F2F-E885CBE313B9}.png)
## Glucose-lowering treatments
- Lifestyle modifications = this is the first-line treatment for newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes weight loss and exercise improve glucose levels, and a trial of 3-6 months is usually recommended if initial A1C is not markedly elevated
- Metformin (biguanides) is generally the initial drug of choice due to its effectiveness, safety, lack of hypoglycemia risk, and potential cardiovascular benefits
	- Exact mechanism is unknown, but its primary effect is decreasing hepatic glucose production by inhibiting gluconeogenesis
	- Also reduces glucose absorption from the GI tract, stimulates glycolysis for increased glucose uptake, and reduces glucagon levels, leading to increased insulin sensitivity
	- It is associated with weight loss and rarely causes hypoglycemia because it does not depend on beta cells
	- Lipid effects = lowers serum FFA, triglycerides, and LDL, while slightly increasing HDL
	- Adverse effects = GI upset (nausea, pain) and a metallic taste are most common lactic acidosis is a rare but life-threatening complication, almost always associated with renal insufficiency, liver disease, heart failure, hypoxia, or serious illness → should be held during acute illness or iv contrast tests and avoided in patients with low GFR
	- Major contraindication = cannot use it in impaired kidney function, because it can cause a major lactic acidosis 
		- It does this because it impairs the electron transport chain, turns it into AMP, turns into a more catabolic state → weight loss effect 
- Second-line options = if metformin is insufficient, seven classes are typically considered
	- Sulfonylureas, GLP-1 receptor agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, TZDs, basal insulin, and GIP/GLP-1 dual agonists
### Key drug classes
- SGLT2 inhibitors = promote glucose excretion via kidneys; known for significant nonglycemic benefits, including reduced heart failure hospitalizations and delayed kidney disease progression
	- Act on the Na+/glucose symports in the PCT (see [Normal Renal Anatomy and Physiology](Nephrology/Normal%20Renal%20Anatomy%20and%20Physiology.md)), decrease glucose reabsorption 
	- Mechanism = they inhibit sglt2 in the proximal tubule, causing glucose loss in urine and mild osmotic diuresis
	- Benefits = mild weight loss and may improve heart failure outcomes
	- Adverse effects = UTIs and vulvovaginal candidiasis
- GLP-1 receptor agonists = improve satiety and weight loss; several have demonstrated cardiovascular protection in high-risk populations
- Sulfonylureas = effectively lower glucose but carry a higher risk of hypoglycemia and modest weight gain
	- Include 1st generation (tolbutamide, chlorpropamide, tolazamide), 2nd generation (glyburide, glipizide), and 3rd generation (glimepiride)
	- "Insulin secretagogues" that close ATP-dependent K+ channels in pancreatic beta cells, causing depolarization and increased insulin release
	- [Hypoglycemia](Endocrinology/Endocrine%20Pathologies/Hypoglycemia.md) = most common side effect they also cause weight gain chlorpropamide specifically can cause flushing with alcohol (disulfiram-like reaction) and hyponatremia due to increased ADH activity
- Meglitinides (repaglinide, nateglinide) = similar mechanism to sulfonylureas (closing k+ channels) but short-acting and given before meals they contain no sulfur and can be used in patients with sulfa allergies
- Insulin = essential when other agents fail; basal insulin is typically initiated first, titrated based on fasting glucose levels → see [Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus](Endocrinology/Endocrine%20Pathologies/Diabetes/Type%201%20Diabetes%20Mellitus.md) for more information about details 
- Incretin-based therapies 
	- GLP-1 analogs (exenatide, liraglutide) =  stimulate insulin release and slow gastric emptying; common side effects are nausea and vomiting
	- DPP-4 inhibitors (sitagliptin, linagliptin) = oral drugs that inhibit the enzyme that degrades glp-1; side effects include urinary and respiratory infections
	- GIP/GLP-1 dual agonists (tirzepatide) = a single molecule showing superior HbA1C reduction and weight loss compared to earlier GLP-1 agonists
- TZDs (thiazolidinediones, pioglitazone, rosiglitazone) = act as potent PPAR-gamma agonists, act on adiponectin improving insulin sensitivity in muscle, fat, and liver tissue
	- GLUT4 and adiponectin ↑
	- Adverse effects = weight gain, fluid retention/edema, and a risk of hepatotoxicity because of fluid retention, they must be avoided in advanced heart failure
- Alpha-glucosidase inhibitors (AGIs) = oral antidiabetic medications (e.g., Acarbose, Miglitol) that treat Type 2 diabetes by slowing carbohydrate digestion, thereby reducing post-meal blood sugar spikes
	- Competitive inhibitors of intestinal $\alpha$ -glucosidases that slow glucose absorption
	- Main side effects are GI-related, such as flatulence and diarrhea
- Amylin analogs (pramlintide) = a protein co-secreted with insulin that suppresses glucagon release, delays gastric emptying, and reduces appetite it is given sq with meals for both type 1 and type 2 diabetes
## Treatment targets
- Long-term benefits = early intensive glycemic control provides a "legacy effect," reducing the risk of myocardial infarction and death even 20 years later
- Glycemic targets = a general target of hba1c < 70% is recommended for most nonpregnant adults, though targets should be personalized
- Personalization = more stringent targets (< 65%) may be appropriate for those with short disease duration, while less stringent targets (7%–8%) are better for those with severe hypoglycemia history or limited life expectancy
## Special cases
- Pathophysiologic subgroups = it is vital to identify atypical forms of diabetes, such as LADA (autoimmune), type 3C (pancreatic disease), and [Monogenic Diabetes](Endocrinology/Endocrine%20Pathologies/Diabetes/Monogenic%20Diabetes.md), as these require specific treatment strategies
	- Latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA) = a slow-progressing form of autoimmune diabetes, often called "type 1.5"
	- Type 3c diabetes = form of diabetes caused by structural damage to the pancreas, such as chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, cancer, or surgery
- Pregnancy = women with T2D require intensive management and lower glucose targets during pregnancy to reduce fetal risks; insulin is generally the preferred therapy
	- Major fetal risk is cardiac defects
