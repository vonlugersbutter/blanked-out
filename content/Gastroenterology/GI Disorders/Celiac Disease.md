# Definition
Autoimmune destruction of small intestinal villi (primarily in the duodenum) triggered by gluten exposure (wheat, barely, rye). 
# Etiology
- Gliadin is a protein component of gluten → gluten gets digested into smaller peptides
- Gliadin enters lamina propria, tTG modifies it through deamidation → enhanced ability to bind to HLA-DQ2 and HLA-DQ8 on APCs, presented to CD4+ T cells
- CD4+ T cells release pro-inflammatory cytokines, driving inflammation, crypt hyperplasia, and the destruction of intestinal villi, collectively resulting in enteropathy
- Stimulated B cells produce autoantibodies, such as tTG-IgA that can be detected
- Cytotoxic CD8+ T cells are recruited to attack intestinal epithelial cells, exacerbating villous atrophy and disrupting the intestinal lining
- Increased intestinal permeability, more inflammation, impaired nutrient reabsorption 
# Epidemiology
- More common in females
- Bimodal incidence, either 8-12 months following first exposure to diet, or third to fourth decade of life
- Most common in those with north European descent 
# Clinical features
- Diarrhea, unexplained weight loss, distension, bloating, nausea, vomiting
- Steatorrhea, constipation
- Children will present with similar symptoms, including irritability 
- Fatigue, pallor, dyspnea, anemia (IDA or B12/folate)
- Later on, myalgias, bone pains, osteoporosis
- Infertility, recurrent pregnancy loss, delayed puberty 
# Investigations
- Testing needs to be done while patient follows a diet with gluten for at least 6-8 weeks 
- Can be done in those that are symptomatic, or those that have autoimmune disorders or immediate family if desired 
- Screening involves testing for IgA tTG or anti-endomysial antibodies; could also use deamidated gliadin antibodies 
- Go ahead with an esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) with multiple duodenal biopsies to confirm 
- Histology features include blunting of villi, crypt hyperplasia, and lymphocytes in the lamina propria
- Use Marsh criteria to examine villi to diagnose

# Prognosis
- Includes a small risk of EATL (T-cell lymphoma) and dermatitis herpetiformis, a herpes-like skin condition that resolves with a gluten-free diet
	- For the rash, IgA antibodies cross-react with skin antigens and deposit in the papillary dermis, causing neutrophil inflammation + blisters 
# Treatment 
- Strict adherence to gluten-free diet 
- Nutritional support, avoiding deficiencies
- Monitoring for calcium and vitamin D intake to avoid osteoporosis
