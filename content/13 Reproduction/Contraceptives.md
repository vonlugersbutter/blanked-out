- Adolescents = Providers are encouraged to recommend LARC methods (implants and IUDs) to adolescents due to their high efficacy and safety
- Special considerations apply for contraceptive use in women with HIV, potential interactions with antiretroviral therapies → they ↑ CYP activity 
	- Same thing with antiseizures, antifungals, rifampin
- Fertility Awareness = methods identify fertile days of the cycle (eg, using CycleBeads) to avoid intercourse or use barriers → proper instruction and complex charting are critical for efficacy

- Oral contraceptives (OCPs) = analogs of estrogens and progesterone
- Progestin-only = suppresses ovulation through negative feedback and thins the endometrium to prevent implantation
	- E.g. "mini pill" and Depo-Provera injections
- Combination OCPs = contain both progestin and estrogen for better suppression of follicular growth and stabilization of the endometrium
	- Risks include breakthrough bleeding, hypertension, and thrombosis (DVT, PE)
	- Contraindicated in smokers over 35 due to cardiovascular risks
# Effectiveness tiers
Four primary tiers based on the number of pregnancies per 100 woman-years:
- Most Effective (<2 pregnancies)
	- Implants = Long-acting hormonal rods inserted under the skin
	- Intrauterine Devices (IUDs) = Both copper-bearing (Cu-IUD) and levonorgestrel-releasing (LNG-IUS) systems
	- Female Sterilization = Various surgical methods including salpingectomy and tubal occlusion
	- Vasectomy = Male sterilization involving the removal of a segment of the vas deferens
- Very Effective (3–9 pregnancies)
	- Injectables = Hormonal injections like DMPA (depot medroxyprogesterone acetate)
	- Lactational Amenorrhea Method (LAM) = Contraception through breastfeeding
	- Combined Hormonal Contraceptives (CHCs) = Including oral contraceptive pills, transdermal patches, and vaginal rings
- Effective (10–20 pregnancies)
	- Male and female condoms, diaphragms, and fertility awareness-based methods
- Least Effective (21–30 pregnancies)
	- Spermicides = Chemical barriers used alone or with other methods

# Long-acting reversible contraception (LARC)
- Intrauterine Devices (IUDs)
	- Identification = Devices are distinguished by size, string color, and the presence or absence of a silver band at the junction of the stem and arms
	- Hormonal (LNG-IUS) = Devices like Mirena and Liletta contain 52 mg of levonorgestrel and are FDA-approved for 5 years
	- Copper (Cu-IUD) = ParaGard is a copper-bearing device used for long-term (up to 10 years) contraception
	- Contraindications = Suspension of pregnancy or uterine abnormalities that distort the cavity
- Implants = These involve a permeable membrane regulating continuous daily hormone release Complications can include intravascular migration or difficult removals requiring peripheral nerve experts

# Permanent sterilization
 - Female Methods = Techniques include bipolar coagulation, silicone bands, spring clips, and partial salpingectomy (puerperal or interval)
- Over a 12-year interval, unipolar coagulation has the lowest cumulative probability of failure, while bipolar coagulation shows a significant increase in failure probability over time
- Increasing trend of using salpingectomy (complete removal of the fallopian tubes) as a form of permanent sterilization, partly for its potential role in reducing ovarian cancer risk
# Combined hormonal contraceptives (CHCs)
- Oral Contraceptives (COCs) = most common format
	- Certain formulations, such as the drospirenone-containing pill Yaz, are FDA-approved for treating PMDD (Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder) and moderate acne vulgaris in addition to providing contraception
- Non-Oral Formats = transdermal patch and intravaginal ring are generally considered to have efficacy and safety profiles similar to COCs
- CHCs may interact with other medications
	- Typically lower levels of the anticonvulsant lamotrigine 
	- Interactions with antidepressants (imipramine) and tranquilizers (diazepam) may require dose adjustments
# Emergency contraception
- Copper-Containing IUDs = most effective EC method, inserted up to 5 days after intercourse It provides the added benefit of ongoing contraception for 10 years
- Yuzpe Method = specific doses of COCs taken within 72 to 120 hours of intercourse, more effective the sooner it is taken
- Hormonal Regimens = Common side effects include nausea, which can be mitigated by taking an antiemetic before the EC dose
