# Definition
- Peptic ulcer = defect in the gastric or duodenal mucosa that penetrates through the muscularis mucosa
	- Gastric ulcer = a peptic ulcer of the stomach
	- Duodenal ulcer = a peptic ulcer of the duodenum, usually duodenal bulb
# Etiology
- Gastric ulcers = usually occur on the lesser curvature and cause pain that is worse with meals, leading to weight loss bleeding
	- Typically involves the left gastric artery
- Duodenal ulcers = comprise ~90% of PUD and are nearly always related to *H. pylori*
	- Pain typically improves with meals because food stimulates bicarbonate secretion
	- Brunner’s glands in the duodenum hypertrophy to provide additional alkaline fluid 
	- Complications include bleeding from the gastroduodenal artery or perforation
- Causes: *H. pylori* infection, prolonged NSAID use, hypersecretory states, stress
	- Hypersecretory state could be something like [Zollinger-Ellison Syndrome](Gastroenterology/GI%20Pathologies/Zollinger-Ellison%20Syndrome.md)
- Smoking, heavy alcohol use, glucocorticoids, caffeine, genetic factors → all risk factors for [GERD](Gastroenterology/GI%20Pathologies/GERD.md) and also [Chronic Gastritis](Gastroenterology/GI%20Pathologies/Chronic%20Gastritis.md)
- Other drugs, infections can also cause it 
- In gastric cases, *H. pylori* secretes urease →  producing ammonium to protect itself from acid this increases gastric pH, triggering gastrin release and further acid production
	- Bacterial colonization, attachment to epithelial cells, cagA toxin production that disrupts mucosal barrier and causes damage to underlying cells 
- Duodenal ulcers → *H. pylori* inhibits somatostatin secretion → ↑ gastrin secretion → ↑ H+ coming into the duodenum
- NSAIDs inhibit COX-1 and COX-2 → ↓ prostaglandin production → ↓ erosion of gastric mucosa
# Epidemiology
- Duodenal ulcers happen earlier
- Usually middle aged people, more males
# Clinical features
- Epigastrium, gnawing, burning pain
- Indigestion, [GERD](Gastroenterology/GI%20Pathologies/GERD.md), nausea, vomiting, bloating 
- Gastric ulcers of the lesser curvature may cause bleeding from the left gastric artery
	- Here, the pain increases after eating, so weight **loss**
- Duodenal ulcers of the posterior wall may cause bleeding from the gastroduodenal artery
	- Here, the pain decreases immediately after eating due to bicarbonate release, leading to weight **gain** 
# Investigations
- Initial evaluation
	- Screen for common etiologies, consider CBC, fecal occult blood test
	- For *H. pylori* → urea breath test, stool antigen test
- Further, for red-flags, especially those unresponsive to empiric therapy 
	- EGD, LFTs, abdominal ultrasound if there is jaundice
- Alarm features if there is worsening dysphagia, rapid weight loss, GI bleeding, family history 
- Use esophagoduodenoscopy (EGD) and biopsies to rule out gastric cancer, especially in older patients/those with family history 
	- Malignancy screening to differentiate from gastric cancer
	- Invasive *H. pylori* testing, biopsy 
- An atypical location raises suspicion for adenocarcinoma 
# Prognosis
- Untreated *H. pylori* , can lead to [Chronic Gastritis](Gastroenterology/GI%20Pathologies/Chronic%20Gastritis.md), also associated with [MALT lymphoma](Gastroenterology/GI%20Pathologies/MALT%20lymphoma.md), gastric adenocarcinoma 
## Bleeding ulcer
- Much more likely with posterior duodenal ulcers!
- Hematemesis (coffee-ground)
- Melena
- Anemia
- Hematochezia in massive bleeding
- Forrest classification of bleeding peptic ulcers, where I is higher risk than III for some reason 

| Stage                                      |     | Stigmata                                   |
| ------------------------------------------ | --- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Active hemorrhage (Stage I)                | Ia  | Active spurting                            |
|                                            | Ib  | Active oozing                              |
| Evidence of a recent hemorrhage (Stage II) | IIa | Nonbleeding ulcer with a visible vessel    |
|                                            | IIb | Ulcer with an adherent clot                |
|                                            | IIc | Flat pigmented spot (covered with hematin) |
| Clean-based ulcer (Stage III)              | III | Clean ulcer base (no active hemorrhage)    |
## Perforation
- Much more likely with anterior duodenal ulcers than posterior ones!
- Leakage of intestinal contents
- Sudden, diffuse pain, rigidity, fever, tachypnea, pneumoperitoneum
- Shoulder pain through irritation of phrenic nerve 
## Penetration and fistula formation
- Adjacent organs become affected, especially in duodenal ulcers
- Conservative management as they close by themselves, surgical resection if needed 
# Treatment 
- Symptom control
	- Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) = (eg, omeprazole) block the H+/K+ ATPase
	- H2 blockers = (eg, cimetidine, famotidine) block histamine receptors
	- Misoprostol = a PGE1 analog that blunts acid secretion
- *H. pylori* eradication therapy → quadruple therapy of PPI, bismuth, clarithromycin, amoxicillin/metronidazole  
- Gastric ulcers are more risky for malignancies, take multiple biopsies from different areas
- For duodenal, only need to take from those that suggest malignancies 
- Could treat [Zollinger-Ellison Syndrome](Gastroenterology/GI%20Pathologies/Zollinger-Ellison%20Syndrome.md) if it is underlying based on gastrin levels 
- Elective surgeries
	- Vagotomy, but associated with complications
	- Billroth I = distal gastrectomy with end-to-end or side-to-end gastroduodenostomy
	- Billroth II = resection of the distal two-thirds of the stomach with a blind-ending duodenal stump and end-to-side gastrojejunostomy
	- Total gastrectomy and reconstruction = roux-en-Y
- Those with gastric ulcers should be monitored and *H. pylori* eradication should be confirmed → endoscopic follow-up
- Managing bleed
	- NPO
	- Volume resuscitation and transfusion
	- Could need some kind of clipping 
- Managing perforation
	- NPO, volume resusciation
	- Graham patch = surgical repair of small perforation
