# Growth phases
## Intrauterine growth
- Placenta acts as an endocrine organ, releasing hormones and growth factors while supplying oxygen and nutrition
	- Placental weight is typically directly related to birth weight
- GH is high in the fetus, but GH receptors are limited, suggesting GH plays a smaller role prenatally than postnatally
	- GH-deficient infants often have birth weights 1 SD below the mean
- TH = deficiency does not directly affect birth weight, though it may lead to prolonged gestation
- IGF-I and IGF-II are detectable from the first trimester
	- IGF-II is more critical in early gestation, while IGF-I becomes more important in later gestation 
	- Fetal IGF-I is regulated by metabolic factors rather than GH
- Major growth factor in fetal life; fetal hyperinsulinism, often seen in infants of diabetic mothers, leads to macrosomia (excessive birth size), see [Diabetes](Endocrinology/Endocrine%20Pathologies/Diabetes/Diabetes.md)
## Postnatal growth
- Growth is not constant but occurs in spurts
- Adiposity rebound = period of accelerating weight gain and rising BMI in early childhood; an early rebound is a risk factor for later obesity

- Phases seen postnatally
1. Fetal period = HPG axis is active mid-gestation but becomes quiet toward term
2. Minipuberty = brief reactivation within the first few months of life (peaking at 1–3 months)
	- Boys = elevated LH and Testosterone lead to penile growth and proliferation of Sertoli cells
	- Girls = elevated FSH leads to ovarian follicular development and transiently palpable breast tissue
3. Juvenile pause = decade-long period of HPG suppression during childhood, maintained by both gonadal steroid negative feedback and intrinsic CNS inhibitory mechanisms
4. Puberty onset = marked by nocturnal surges of LH pulses during sleep, which gradually increase in amplitude and frequency
## Adrenarche
- Adrenarche is the increase in adrenal androgen secretion (primarily DHEA and DHEAS) beginning in early to mid-childhood, usually around age 3
- Progressive rise in plasma DHEAS is the most useful marker
- Adrenal androgens contribute to the development of sebaceous/apocrine glands and the onset of pubic hair (pubarche)
- Independent of the HPG axis and occurs when the axis is still at a low activity level
- Associated with the growth of the zona reticularis (ZR) in the adrenal cortex
- Higher adrenal androgen levels at age 7–9 are associated with an earlier pubertal onset
## Neuroendocrine control
- GnRH neurons are unique; they originate in the nasal placode and migrate into the hypothalamus during the mid-gestational period
- Key regulators
	- Kisspeptin/KISS1R = Kisspeptin neurons in the arcuate nucleus are critical for the resurgence of GnRH pulses
	- MKRN3 = Acts as a "brake" or inhibitory factor; mutations leading to a loss of MKRN3 function cause central precocious puberty (CPP)
	- GABA = primary inhibitory neurotransmitter during the "juvenile pause" (childhood)
	- Leptin = permissive factor secreted by adipocytes → signals that a critical level of energy stores has been reached but does not act as a trigger for puberty in humans

## Puberty
- Females 
	- Thelarche (breast buds) = first clinical sign, usually between ages 8 and 13
	- Menarche = early cycles are frequently anovulatory
- Males
	- Testicular enlargement
	- Spermarche 
- Age of menarche has decreased over the last century in developed countries, largely due to improved nutrition and rising BMI
- Peak height velocity (PHV) = occurs about 2 years earlier in girls than in boys
	- Girls = Tanner stage 2 to 3
	- Boys = Tanner stage 3 to 4
	- Average of your parents, and then plus 2 inches for males, minus 2 inches for females 
- Estrogen is the critical hormone for epiphyseal fusion (closing the growth plates) and bone mass accrual in both sexes
- A lot of adult calcium is laid down during the adolescent years 
	- Bone growth goes length → width → mineralization 
	- Clavicle (collarbone) = first bone to begin the process of ossification in the human embryo, starting around the 5th to 6th week of gestation → one of the last bones to fully complete epiphyseal union, not finishing until the early twenties
## Puberty disorders
- Constitutional delay of growth and puberty (CDGP) = the most common cause ("late bloomers") it is a transient state of GnRH deficiency, often with a positive family history and delayed bone age
- Congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (CHH) = a rare genetic deficiency in GnRH if associated with anosmia (loss of smell), it is called [Kallmann Syndrome](Endocrinology/Endocrine%20Pathologies/Kallmann%20Syndrome.md)
- Hypergonadotropic hypogonadism = primary gonadal failure
	- Klinefelter syndrome (47, XXY) = most common chromosomal cause in males; features include small testes, tall stature, and infertility
	- Turner syndrome (45, X) = most common cause in females; features include short stature and primary ovarian failure
## Precocious puberty
- Precocious puberty = premature onset of physical puberty signs, generally occurring before age 8 in girls and age 9 in boys → 2 to 2.5 SD (less than 5%)
- Central precocious puberty (CPP) = GnRH-dependent caused by premature HPG axis activation
	- Can be idiopathic (more common in females) or caused by CNS lesions like hypothalamic hamartomas
	- Could be infectious, cyst, radiation to the brain 
	- Sequence of puberty is going to be the same, but quicker → also always isosexual
- Peripheral precocious puberty (PPP) = GnRH-independent caused by autonomous secretion of sex steroids from the gonads or adrenals (eg, McCune-Albright syndrome, which includes café au lait spots and fibrous dysplasia of bone)
	- May have variations to sequence of puberty → could be contrasexual 
- Variations
	- Premature thelarche = isolated breast development in girls <2 years old; usually benign and self-limiting
	- Premature adrenarche = isolated pubic hair; while often benign, it may be a forerunner for polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) and metabolic syndrome
# Short stature
## Non-endocrine causes
- Constitutional short stature = variation of normal development characterized by delayed puberty and a thin habitus, often with a family history of late growth
- Genetic short stature
- IUGR and SGA = approximately 20% of infants born small-for-gestational-age follow a lifelong pattern of short stature
- Conditions affecting the heart (congestive failure), lungs (cystic fibrosis, asthma), or GI tract (malabsorption, celiac disease) can impair growth
- Malnutrition = serum IGF-I concentrations are low, similar to GH deficiency
## Endocrine Disorders
- Growth Hormone Deficiency (GHD) = can be idiopathic, due to hypothalamic/pituitary tumors, or genetic mutations (eg, PIT1, PROP1)
- GH Resistance (Laron Syndrome) = characterized by high serum GH but low IGF-I due to abnormal or absent GH receptors
- [Hypothyroidism](Endocrinology/Endocrine%20Pathologies/Hypothyroidism.md) = often detected via TSH and free T4 measurements
## Syndromes
- Turner Syndrome = often undiagnosed until careful evaluation for short stature occurs
- Noonan Syndrome = associated with an increased risk of neoplasia
- Prader-Willi Syndrome = features infantile hypotonia, later insatiable hunger/obesity, small hands/feet, and developmental delay
- Bardet-Biedl Syndrome = obesity, mental retardation, polydactyly, and kidney dysfunction
# Tall stature
- Causes include constitutional or genetic tall stature, as well as syndromes like Marfan syndrome, Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome (fetal gigantism), and endocrine disorders like pituitary gigantism or thyrotoxicosis
# Investigations
- Medical history = must include birth weight, gestational age, family heights, pubertal timing of parents/siblings, and dietary intake
- Physical Exam = focuses on charting growth, looking for midline defects, unusual body proportions, or signs of chronic disease
- Initial tests include CBC (for anemia/malignancy), chemistry panel (for renal/hepatic issues), and TSH
- GH Testing = Performed last if no other diagnosis is found
	- Because GH is pulsatile, a single basal measurement is useless; instead, stimulated tests (using L-Dopa, Clonidine, or Glucagon) are required to assess secretory ability
# Treatment
- GH replacement = successful therapy leads to a rise in growth rate, IGF-I, and IGFBP-3
	- Monitoring involves tracking height velocity and annual bone age assessments
- Fetal programming = low birth weight or malnutrition early in gestation can "program" fetal metabolism for survival, which may become maladaptive in an environment of plenty, increasing the risk of metabolic syndrome (hypertension, impaired glucose tolerance, [Obesity](Gastroenterology/GI%20Pathologies/Obesity.md)) in adulthood
- Catch-up growth risks = excessive or supramaximal catch-up growth in premature or SGA infants may also contribute to later obesity and cardiovascular mortality
