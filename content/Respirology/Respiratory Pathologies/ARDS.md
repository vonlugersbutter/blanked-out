# Definition
End result of lots of injuries that are either intrinsic to the lung, like pneumonia or aspiration, or extrinsic to the lung, like trauma, burns, non-pulmonary sepsis, or acute pancreatitis.
# Etiology
- Interstitial and alveolar edema
- Hemorrhage, cellular debris, proteinaceous (took me lots of tries to spell that) fluid in the alveoli
- Hyaline membranes can be seen, shown below

![300](00%20media/Hyaline_membranes_-_very_high_mag.jpg)

- Damaged alveolar epithelium becomes lined with type 2 alveolar pneumocytes (because that's what happens when the type 1 cells get damaged) → cellular infiltration of the alveolar walls
- Complete healing can occur, but interstitial fibrosis may develop 
- Probably all happening because of initial injury, proinflammatory cytokines, neutrophil recruitment and activation 
	- Neutrophils release those ROS, proteases, cytokines 
	- Impaired surfactant function and increased permeability and more flooding
- Lung becomes stiff, high distending pressures are required to inflate them
- Marked ↓ FRC, because of ↓ compliance 
	- ↑ recoil is probably because of alveolar edema and exudate that exaggerate surface tension forces
- A lot of the blood is going to unventilated alveoli, even 50% (this is shunting), profound hypoxemia
- Arterial pCO2 can vary significantly, some have a low or normal 
	- Some patients can have a low or normal pCO2 despite severe V/Q inequality and shunt, while others develop hypercarbia due to ↑ physiologic dead space

# Prognosis
- Increased risk of interstitial lung disease, pulmonary fibrosis

# Clinical features
- ARDS can happen several hours to 7 days after the initial injury 
- Worsening hypoxemia, increasing oxygen requirements 
- Bilateral alveolar opacities on CXR 
- Increased work of breathing due to severe hypoxemia and ↓ compliance 
- Measure the severity of hypoxemia using the A-a difference, and this is usually done by taking the ratio of arterial pO2 and inspired oxygen fraction → PaO2/F1O2 or P/F ratio
	- ARDS diagnosis requires P/F ratio <300
	- The lower the value, the worse the V/Q inequality
# Treatment
- Invasive mechanical ventilation 
- High inspired oxygen concentrations, increased positive end-expiratory pressure
- Inhaled pulmonary vasodilators
- Ventilation in the prone position
- All ventilation needs to have adequate hydration and humidification 
- Neuromuscular blockade
- Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation
- Bronchodilators, albuterol and ipratropium
- Diuretics to alleviate interstitial edema and peribronchial cuffing (a radiological sign on a chest X-ray or CT scan that appears as a hazy or increased density around a bronchus → signifies wall thickening or fluid buildup)
- Diuresis or thoracentesis (in those with large pleural effusions and edema); drainage of significant ascites or bowel decompression → all can help with compliance problems
