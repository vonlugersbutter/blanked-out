# Gallbladder
- Sac that stores and concentrates bile produced by the liver until needed in the duodenum
- Ampulla of Vater = the point where biliary and pancreatic ducts merge to empty into the major duodenal papilla it marks the anatomical transition from foregut to midgut and the transition from the celiac trunk to the SMA
- Sphincter of Oddi = a circular smooth muscle layer surrounding the major duodenal papilla that controls the flow of bile and enzymes
	- Dysfunction = narrowing of the sphincter can cause RUQ pain, abnormal LFTs, or recurrent pancreatitis opioids (like morphine) can cause a sphincter of Oddi spasm via smooth muscle contraction
# Bile
- Bile is produced in the liver, stored in the gall bladder, and secreted into the duodenum after a meal to aid in lipid absorption and bilirubin excretion
- Biliary tree = anatomical components include the liver, right and left hepatic ducts (RHD/LHD), common hepatic duct, gallbladder, cystic duct, common bile duct, and the pancreatic duct
	- These structures drain into the duodenum via the ampulla of Vater and the sphincter of Oddi
- Bile salts = these act as surfactants and emulsifiers for fats they are synthesized in the liver from cholesterol cholesterol 7-α hydroxylase is the rate-limiting enzyme in the classic synthesis pathway
- Conjugation = bile acids are conjugated to taurine or glycine to become more hydrophilic at one end, making them better surfactants
- Enterohepatic circulation = while most lipids are absorbed in the jejunum, conjugated bile acids remain behind and are eventually absorbed by active transporters in the terminal ileum approximately 95% are recycled, and 5% are excreted in stool
- Cholestasis = this is the disruption of bile flow, leading to jaundice, pruritus (itching), dark urine, and clay-colored stools
- Laboratory patterns
	- Cholestatic pattern = alkaline phosphatase is significantly more elevated than ast/alt this indicates the primary site of dysfunction is the bile ducts, often caused by gallstones, pancreatic masses, or primary biliary cirrhosis
	- Hepatocellular pattern = AST and ALT are significantly more elevated than alkaline phosphatase, indicating primary damage to hepatocytes

# Bilirubin
- Bilirubin is the yellow/brown pigment produced during heme metabolism and serves as a mode of excretion from the body
- Production = macrophages convert heme from old red blood cells into biliverdin (green) and then into unconjugated bilirubin
- Transport and liver processing = unconjugated bilirubin is water-insoluble and carried by albumin to the liver in the liver, the enzyme udp-glucuronyltransferase (ugt) adds glucuronic acid to make it water-soluble (conjugated bilirubin) for excretion
- Intestinal processing = bacteria in the distal small intestine and colon convert conjugated bilirubin back to unconjugated form and then into urobilinogen
- Fates of urobilinogen 
	- 80-90% is converted to stercobilin, which makes stool dark
	- 10-20% is reabsorbed; most returns to the liver, while a small amount is excreted in urine as urobilin (which makes urine yellow)
- Hyperbilirubinemia and jaundice is the yellowing of the skin and eyes, usually occurring when total bilirubin is >30 mg/dl
- Hemolysis = results in elevated unconjugated bilirubin because the liver's capacity is overwhelmed no bilirubin is found in the urine because unconjugated bilirubin cannot cross the glomerulus
- Biliary obstruction = causes elevated conjugated bilirubin this leads to dark urine (as water-soluble bilirubin enters the urine) and clay-colored stools (due to a lack of stercobilin)
- Primary liver disease = often shows a mixed increase in both types of bilirubin
## Special syndromes
- Gilbert’s syndrome = a mild decrease in UGT enzyme levels leading to mild unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia, often triggered by fasting, stress, or illness
- Crigler-Najjar syndrome = severely reduced or absent UGT enzyme type i is often fatal in infancy due to kernicterus (bilirubin acting as a neurotoxin in the brain) type ii is less severe and may be treated with phenobarbital
- Dubin-Johnson syndrome = a defect in excreted conjugated bilirubin, classically resulting in a black liver
	- Impaired biliary excretion of bilirubin glucuronides is due to a mutation in the canalicular MRP2
	- Black liver happens due to polymerized epinephrine metabolites
- Rotor’s syndrome = similar to Dubin-Johnson but milder and without the black liver
- Neonatal jaundice = caused by increased bilirubin production and "immature" UGT activity it is treated with phototherapy, which isomerizes bilirubin into lumirubin, a more water-soluble form that can be excreted without conjugation
