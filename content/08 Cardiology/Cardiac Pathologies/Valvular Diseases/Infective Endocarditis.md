# Definition
Infection of the endocardium, especially the valves. Extensive tissue damage, often fatal. 
# Etiology
## Classification methods
### Clinical course
#### Acute bacterial endocarditis (ABE)
Rapidly progressive, explosively fast, something really virulent like S. aureus. Can damage even health valve
#### Subacute bacterial endocarditis (SBE)
More insidious, usually something less virulent like viridans streptococci. Usually happens in people with previous valve damage. 
### Host substrate
#### Native valve endocarditis
Most cases.
#### Prosthetic valve endocarditis
Staphylococcus epidermis, common skin contaminant. 
#### IVD use 
Usually S. aureus endocarditis of the tricuspid valve, coming in straight from the venous supply. 
### Specific microorganism
Usually gram-positive cocci. Viridans group streptococci from the oropharynx, Streptococcus gallolyticus from the GI tract (raises suspicion for colonic polyps or colorectal carcinoma). 
## Pathophysiology
Requires:
- Endocardial surface injury
	- Turbulent blood flow due to existing abnormalities, like MR or AS
	- Congenital heart disease
	- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
- Platelet-fibrin-thrombus formation at injury site
	- Sterile thrombus, called vegetation → nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis (NBTE)
	- Provides fibrin deposits for bacteria to sit on
	- Fibrin covers the organisms, protects them from defenses → chemotaxis and phagocyte migration
- Bacterial entry
	- Dental procedures
	- Intravenous drug use 
	- Gram-positives are good at this, because they are resistant to destruction, like to adhere to proteins
	- MRSA and VRE are particularly associated with ↑ mortality
- Bacterial adherence to injured area 
	- Protected by phagocytic activity

## Complications
- Mechanical cardiac injury
- Thrombotic or septic emboli
	- CNS (40% of patients), kidneys, spleen → infection or infarction! 
- Immune injury because of antigen-antibody deposition
	- Glomerulonephritis, arthritis, vasculitis 
- Local extension of infection
	- Valvular damage
	- Abcess formation
	- Erosion into cardiac conduction 
- Embolic infarction, especially in vasa vasorum → localized mycotic aneurysm (a weakening and bulging of an artery wall caused by an infection, often bacterial, which can lead to rupture and severe consequences like sepsis or hemorrhage)
	- Aorta, viscera, peripheral organs 

# Epidemiology
# Prognosis
6 month overall mortality of 20-25% even with treatment, and 100% mortality if not treated properly. 
# Investigations
## Laboratory findings
- Elevated WBCs → higher neutrophils, immature granulocytes
- Elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (a non-specific blood test measuring how quickly red blood cells settle in a test tube over one hour, indicating inflammation in the body)
	- How fast red blood cells (erythrocytes) settle at the bottom of a standardized tube in one hour
	- Higher sedimentation rates mean more inflammation is present, as certain proteins cause red blood cells to clump and fall faster
- C-reactive proteins = a substance produced by the liver that indicates inflammation or infection in the body, with levels rising rapidly in response to injury or disease
	- Activates the complement pathway
	- Binds to damaged or foreign substances, such as apoptotic cells, pathogens, and debris
	- Acts as an opsonin
- 50% of cases also ↑ serum rheumatoid factor = due to a chronic and intense immune response to the ongoing intravascular bacterial infection, leading to the immune system producing autoantibodies → this immune system overstimulation can cause the production of RF, which is an antibody that mistakenly targets the body's own tissues
	- Autoantibody, starts binding to IgG
## Electrocardiogram
- Visualize vegetations, valvular dysfunction, abcess formation
## Blood culture
- Need to figure out organism, antibiotic resistance 
# Clinical features
## Acute IE
- Rapid, progressive illness
- High fever
- Chills
## Subacute IE
- Low-grade fever
- Nonspecific constitutional symptoms: fatigue, anorexia, weakness, myalgia, night sweats 
- Could be confused with another infection! So a history of a valve lesion or other predisposing issue is important → ask about IV drug use, prior valve disease, recent dental procedures, etc
## Cardiac examination
- Reveal murmur that predisposed the patient
- Tricuspid valve lesion, tricuspid regurgitation
- Severe valve destruction would cause signs of heart failure → leading cause of death! 
## Other physical findings
- Neurologic findings
- Flank pain, hematuria, renal failure
- Lung infarction or infection → especially right sided
- Skin findings! Septic embolism or immune complex vasculitis → peripheral stigmata of endocarditis 
	- Petechiae = tiny, circular red-brown discolorations
	- Splinter hemorrhages = subungual microemboli, found beneath fingernails
	- Janeway lesions (rare) = flat, irregular discolorations on the palms and soles
	- Osler nodes = tender, pea-sized, erythematous nodules on the pulp space of fingers and toes
	- Roth spots = microinfarctions, emboli to the retina that look like white dots surrounded by hemorrhage

## Diagnostic criteria

Definitive diagnosis requires two major, one major plus three minor, or five minor. Possible diagnosis requires one major plus one minor, or three minor. 
![500](00%20media/{38461E7C-6A09-44EA-96AD-6E65810F9691}.png)
# Treatments
- 4-6 weeks of high-dose IV antibiotics
- Empiric broad-spectrum initially, before blood culture
- Directed once the causative organism known
- Surgical intervention → valve replacement for persistent bacteremia or fever
