# Acid-base and solute regulation 
- Phosphate buffer system is really important in the cytosol, bicarbonate system is more important in the plasma
- The only way to get rid of acids other than carbonic acid is through the urine
- Intercalated cells have proton pumps (H+ ATPases) 
- One type of intercalated cells are Cl-/HCO3- antiporters
- Another type has proton pumps in its basolateral surface and the antiporters on its apical side, so it allows the cells to secrete HCO3- and reabsorb H+, if the blood is getting too alkaline 

![300](00%20media/{383720A2-B079-4576-98CE-E3FD1FF9812E}.png)
The bicarbonate buffer system is important for maintain pH. 
$$
CO_2 + H_2O \leftrightarrow H_2CO_3  \leftrightarrow H^++HCO_3^-
$$
The second step is fast, the first step is catalysed by carbonic anhydrase. We can calculate the pH based on the bicarbonate level.
$$
pH = pK_a + log\frac{[HCO_3^-]}{\alpha P_{CO_2}}
$$
Where $\alpha$ is the solubility. We can substitute the appropriate values.
$$
pH = 6.1 + log\frac{[HCO_3^-]}{0.03 P_{CO_2}}
$$
$$
H^+ = \frac{24\times P_{CO_2}}{[HCO_3^-]}
$$
- Volatile acid = can be rapidly eliminated from the body as a gas via respiration, as opposed to a non-volatile acid, where this doesn't happen
- Aspartate and glutamate can get metabolized → HCO3-
- HCO3- also plays an important role in the neutralization of other acids
$$
H_2SO_4 + 2NaHCO_3 \leftrightarrow Na_2SO_4 + 2CO_2+2H_2O
$$
$$
HCl + NaHCO_3 \leftrightarrow NaCl + CO_2 + H_2O
$$
- Net endogenous acid production = NEAP = total amount of non-volatile acid the body must excrete daily to maintain acid-base balance, calculated by subtracting dietary alkali (from sources like fruits and vegetables) from dietary and metabolic acid (primarily from protein)
	- Needs to be replaced by an equivalent loss in bicarbonate
- Renal net acid excretion = RNEA = net amount of acid the kidneys excrete in the urine over a specific time period

Koeppen gives this as,
$$
RNAE = [(U_{NH_4}\times \dot{V}) + (U_{TA} \times \dot{V})] - (U_{HCO_3^-}\times \dot{V})
$$
Which is literally baffling to me because it can just be written as,
$$
RNAE = \dot{V}(U_{NH_4} + U_{TA} - U_{HCO_3^-})
$$

- To maintain acid base homeostasis, NEAP = RNEA 
- Kidneys regulate RNAE to match NEAP → replenish HCO3- lost by neutralization of non-volatile acids 
	- H+ secretion → reabsorption of filtered HCO3- and excretion of acid 
	- H+ is secreted with buffers like phosphate and other buffers to a lesser extent → titratable acid 
	- This is not enough, and synthesis and excretion of NH4+ also happens (this is the main contribution towards responding to acidemia)
- Normally, there is very little bicarbonate secreted in the urine, so RNAE mostly just reflects the TA and NH4+ 
## Mechanisms of bicarbonate reabsorption
This is in the PCT, and has been covered in [Normal Renal Anatomy and Physiology](Nephrology/Normal%20Renal%20Anatomy%20and%20Physiology.md). 
![400](00%20media/{0D0FE57E-5569-47A0-BEA7-4F1BB515A5D1}.png)

- Na+/H+ antiporter (predominant) and H+/ATP (V-type H+-ATPase)
- Movement of H2O and CO2 occurs through AQP1 
- DCT and collecting duct reabsorb a small amount of HCO3-
- As discussed elsewhere, alpha intercalated cells secrete H+, while beta intercalated cells secrete HCO3- into the tubular fluid 
- Regulation of all this secretion → assume that H+ ↑ 
	- Makes H+ across apical membrane more favorable
	- Changes to transport proteins 
	- Transporters shuttled to the plasma membrane during acidosis/increased gene expression 
		- Acid → endothelin-1 → phosphorylation and insertion of Na+/H+ antiporter into apical membrane and Na+/3HCO3- symporter into the basolateral membrane 
		- Acid → cortisol → more expression of those genes in the PCT 
- Acid → PTH → ↓ phosphate reabsorption in the PCT 
- Volume contraction → ↑ H+ secretion
	- Na+ reabsorption ↑ → H+ secretion ↑ because of the antiporter
	- RAAS → angiotensin II → stimulate Na+/H+ antiporter into apical membrane and Na+/3HCO3- symporter into the basolateral membrane 
	- Volume contraction → peritubular capillary changes → ↑ PCT reabsorption → ↑ HCO3- reabsorption 
## Making new HCO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup>
- Happens through excretion of TA and synthesis and excretion of NH4+
- TA
	- When H+ gets made and secreted out, HCO3- also gets made and is put into the ECF
- Glutamine metabolism in the kidneys → NH4+ synthesis or ammoniagenesis
	- Glutamine makes 2 NH4+ and 2-oxoglutarate → provides 2HCO3-, which exits into the blood
	- WHY did Koeppen reverse the orientation of the PCT cell 
	- NH4+ gets secreted out by the PCT, and then goes right back to getting reabsorbed by the thick ascending limb of the LoH → NH4+ substitutes for the K+ on the Na+/K+/2Cl- symporter 
	- Ends up in the medullary interstitium, and then gets secreted back out in the collecting duct 
		- Rhesus glycoproteins RhBG (on basolateral) and RhCG (apical and basolateral) act as transporters of the NH3 → non-ionic diffusion
		- But the apical membrane has low permeability to NH4+, so that can't get through (diffusion trapping)
![](00%20media/{8FC99F5C-5DE8-487C-9B77-A91CDB2D20EE}.png)
- Okay, so it's important to see how much NH4+ the kidneys are excreting, but there aren't any routine assays available for NH4+ in urine
	- If there's low HCO3- in the urine, urine is acidic, NH4+ excretion must be increased
	- Figure it out using the urinary net charge or the urine anion gap 
$$
urine \; anion \; gap = ([Na^+] +[K^+])-[Cl^-]
$$
- The urine anion gap will be negative when there's enough NH4+ being secreted
- The absence of a urine anion gap or a positive value → defect in NH4+ production/excretion 
## Acid-base disorders
- Acidosis → ↑ cortisol, ↑ angiotensin II, ↑ RhBG and RhCG → ↑ ammoniagenesis → ↑ HCO3-
- Hyperkalemia → ↓ NH4+ production
- A change in bicarbonate indicates a metabolic disorder, while a change in pCO2 indicates a respiratory disorder
- Methods of compensation
	- ECF + ICF buffering: non-volatile acid → ↓ HCO3-
	- pCO2 adjustment: ↑ ventilation → ↓ pCO2
		- Kussmaul breathing → muscle fatigue → ↓ resp compensation
	- RNAE adjustments
		- Takes hours-days
		- H+ ↑ → H+ secretion, HCO3- reabsorption, TA excretion, NH4+ excretion → all ↑ RNAE 
![400](00%20media/{BB9F89D3-9603-4F8B-8AE3-6C67E8BC1DF7}.png)
- Vomiting/nasogastric suctioning → ↓ HCl → ECF volume contraction
	- Kidneys cannot secrete enough HCO3-, because Na+ excretion ↓ to correct for volume loss
	- GFR ↓ 
		- Filtered HCO3- ↓, ↑ Na+ reabsorption → Na+/H+ antiporter → ↑ H+ secretion
		- Aldosterone ↑ → ↑ Na+ reabsorption, ↑ H+ excretion
	- Alkalosis and paradoxically acidic urine present, corrected by euvolemia

### Plasma anion gap 
- Non-volatile acid, like in DKA → H+ ↑, pH ↓, HCO3- ↓ 
- Anion associated with non-volatile acid ↑ → use this to calculate plasma anion gap
$$
PAG = [Na^+]-([Cl^-]+[HCO_3^-])
$$
In reality,
$$
[Na^+] + [unmeasured \; cations] = [Cl^-] +[HCO_3^-] + [unmeasured \; anions]
$$ 
- If the acidosis is accounted for by Cl-, like diarrhea, renal tubular acidosis, then the PAG will be normal
- If the acidosis is not accounted for by Cl- (like lactate, or beta-hydroxyglutarate) → anion gap will increase 
# Physical principles of fluid homeostasis
- The body has a steady state balance = some semblance of equilibrium
- Molarity = molar concentration per L
- Equivalence = one equivalent is equal to one mole of positive or negative charge, so an ion's equivalents are determined by its charge. For example, the equivalence of a Ca++ solution would be twice that of a Na+ solution, at the same molarity

van't Hoff's law:
$$
\Pi = nCRT
$$ Where $\Pi$ is the osmolarity, $n$ is the number of dissociable particles per molecule of the solute, $C$ is the concentration, $R$ is the gas constant, and $T$ is the temperature. It can be modified to accommodate for when the membrane is slightly permeable, which apparently it usually is.
$$
\Pi = \sigma (nCRT)
$$
Where $\sigma$ is the reflection/osmotic coefficient, reflecting the substance's relative ability to cross the membrane. It is equal to 1 when it is impermeable. 

- In order to maintain osmotic pressure, the substance should not be able to permeate through the membrane
- An effective osmole = does not permeate and is able to cause osmotic pressure, while an ineffective osmole is the opposite
- Oncotic pressure = osmotic pressure generated by larger molecules
- Specific gravity = total solute concentration in a solution
- Total body water = ICF + ECF (where ECF consists of plasma and interstitial fluid)
- Third space = other place where fluid accumulates, like ascites
- Gibbs-Donnan effect = unequal distribution of diffusible ions across a semi-permeable membrane due to the presence of impermeable substances on one side

$$
plasma \; osmolarity = 2 \times (plasma \; [Na+])
$$
Or, more precisely,
$$
plasma \; osmolarity = 2 \times (plasma \; [Na+]) + \frac{[glucose]}{18} + \frac{[urea]}{2.8}
$$
Filtration rate, using the Starling forces,
$$
filtration \; rate = K_f[(P_c-P_i)-\sigma(\pi_c-\pi_i)]
$$
Where $K_f$ is the capillary filtration coefficient, tells us about the permeability of the capillary wall and how much surface area there is for exchange. 

Also see [Blood Vessels and General Vasculature](Cardiology/Blood%20Vessels%20and%20General%20Vasculature.md), under the capillary exchange section for a discussion on the more vasculature-related aspects of this.
