# Definition
Loss of pumping efficiency by the heart. The heart fails to pump blood at a sufficient rate to meet the demands of the body, or is only able to do so if the cardiac filling pressures are really high. 
# Etiology

![400](00%20media/{2F3544C9-55CB-4ED8-984D-91B9FEDF8712}.png) 

We can have either diastolic dysfunction or systolic dysfunction, which seems to be largely determined by whether the ejection fraction is okay. Recall that the ejection fraction is:
$$
EF = \frac{SV}{EDV}\times 100
$$

Where a normal fraction would be 50-70%. 

Let's talk about systolic dysfunction.
![200](00%20media/{25FF138D-40BC-4019-9740-9CB72252F11D}.png)

In this case, contractility is impaired. The ESPVR slope is less steep, so ESV and ESP is lower, leading to a lower SV. There is an issue of positive feedback here. If the ventricle isn't able to push as much blood out, it's going to have to deal with that blood, along with the normal pulmonary venous return. That is going to lead to ↑ EDV, ↑ preload, which is going to try and increase SV. But, ESV will remain elevated. EDV and preload starts to increase, which then increases the preload. Heart overstretches in this positive feedback loop: less effective pumping → ↑ EDV and preload → ↑ contractility → overstretching of heart → less effective pumping. Note that the curve is shifted down and to the right. 

Either way, we can also talk about diastolic dysfunction.
![300](00%20media/{D470BB33-672E-491C-9B3C-3EDD40EF8691}.png)

LV pressure remains high, and that is transmitted to the LA, pulmonary veins and capillaries. Transudation of fluid into the pulmonary interstitium and pulmonary congestion results. Note that the curve is shifted up and to the left. EDV ↓. 

But, a lot of patients have both systolic and diastolic abnormalities, so they are divided based on how good their LVEF is.
## Heart failure with reduced EF (HFrEF)
- The ventricle can't get blood out
- Loss of contractility
	- Destruction of myocytes, abnormal myocyte function, fibrosis
- Pressure overload
	- Impaired ejection due to really high afterload or resistance 
## Heart failure with preserved EF (HFpEF)
- Ventricular diastolic dysfunction
	- Impaired early diastolic relaxation
	- Increased stiffness of the wall
	- Acute myocardial ischemia leading to ↓ energy delivery and diastolic relaxation
	- LV hypertrophy, fibrosis, restrictive cardiomyopathy → stiff walls
	- Pericardial diseases → ↓ filling 
- Usually vascular congestion because all that backup pressure and volume has to go somewhere
## Right-sided heart failure
- RV has a high compliance. It takes in blood at a low pressure, ejects it against a low pressure system. Very chill like that.
- But, this makes it not that great at adapting to afterload, in the case of things like acute pulmonary embolism

## Compensatory mechanisms
Three of them: Frank-Starling, neurohormonal alterations, ventricular hypertrophy and remodeling. Also note how these compensatory mechanisms can be bad in severe heart failure.
### Frank-Starling
↓ SV → ↑ EDV → Frank-Starling → ↑ force of contraction 
- But this can be bad, because ↑ EDV and preload → ↑ contractility → overstretching of heart → less effective pumping
### Neurohormonal alterations, all ↑ TPR
Recall that 
$$
BP = CO \times TPR
$$
- Adrenergic response
	- Carotid sinus and aortic arch baroreceptors detect low BP → ↓ firing → medulla → ↑ SNS, ↓ PNS
	- This leads to ↑ HR, ↑ contractility, vasoconstriction through $\alpha$ receptors to ↑ TPR
	- But this can be bad because ↑ afterload for an already failing heart 
- Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system
	- Things that lead to renin production from juxtaglomerular cells
		- Renal artery perfusion ↓ 
		- Salt to kidney macula densa ↓ 
		- $\beta$ -2 stimulation of juxtaglomerular receptors from adrenergic activation ↑ 
	- Renin → angiotensinogen to angiotensin I
	- ACE → angiotensin I to angiotensin II
	- Angiotensin II → ↑ thirst in hypothalamus + ↑ aldosterone in adrenal cortex
	- Aldosterone → ↑ Na+ reabsorption in distal convoluted tubule → ↑ water retention → ↑ TPR
- ADH
	- Arterial baroreceptors signaling to posterior pituitary + ↑ angiotensin II → ↑ ADH (vasopressin) → ↑ water retention in distal nephron + ↑ vasoconstriction 

- All of this could ↑ afterload. HR ↑ increases metabolic demand. SNS ↑ leads to down regulation of cardiac $\beta$ adrenergic receptors, ↓ sensitivity, ↓ contractility. 
- Chronic ↑ angiotensin II and aldosterone → cytokine production + macrophage activity → fibrosis and remodeling of the heart 
- This is why a lot of pharmaceutical therapies focus on blocking these compensatory mechanisms
### Remodeling
- LV dilatation ↑ + ↑ ESP → ↑ wall stress → myocardial hypertrophy + ECM deposition → ↑ stiffness → ↑ EDP → ↑ LA + pulmonary pressures
- Chronic volume overload  → myocytes in series → eccentric hypertrophy
- Chronic pressure overload → myocytes in parallel → concentric hypertrophy 
- Lots of things that can cause myocyte loss
	- Myocyte necrosis from infarct
	- Cardiotoxic drugs like doxorubicin (chemotherapeutic)
	- Apoptosis from inflammatory response due to ↑ angiotensin II and aldosterone 
	- Mechanical stress, lack of Ca++ control

## Chronic HFrEF
- Identify underlying cause
- Eliminate acute precipitating cause
- Management of symptoms
	- Treat pulmonary and systemic congestion
	- Increase forward cardiac output
- Modulate neurohormonal response
- Prolong long-term survival
- Ivabradine = selectively and specifically blocks the "funny" current, a mixed sodium-potassium inward current that controls the heart's pacemaker activity → do not use if blood pressure or heart rate is super low
- ICD = implantable cardioverter defibrillator

## HFpEF
- ↓ pulmonary congestion
- Addressing impaired diastolic function causes
- No mortality benefit from beta blockers, ace inhibitor, or ARBs
- No use of inotropic stuff 
## Acute HF
- Classify 
- Normalize ventricular filling pressures
- Restore tissue perfusion
