## Gonococcal infections

![](00%20media/Pasted%20image%2020260312034035.png)

 -  Cervicitis, urethritis, proctitis, and conjunctivitis
 - Lead to [Pelvic Inflammatory Disease](13%20Reproduction/Reproductive%20Pathologies/Pelvic%20Inflammatory%20Disease.md), disseminated is uncommon but → skin lesions, tenosynovitis, septic arthritis, [Infective Endocarditis](08%20Cardiology/Cardiac%20Pathologies/Valvular%20Diseases/Infective%20Endocarditis.md), meningitis
 - Exclusively a human pathogen 
 - Pilus expression switched off → piliated strains adhere better to mucosal cells, more virulent → also allow for horizontal gene transfer
 - Ability to do that allows for antigenic variation of gonococci, this along with polyploid genome 
 - Opacity associated protein = intergonococcal adhesion, and to eukaryotic cells → suppress activation and proliferation of CD4+ T cells 
 - Porin = most abundant gonococcal surface protein = basis for serotyping 
	 - PorB.1A more likely to form disseminated infection
	 - PorB.1B is usually local infections
 - Activation of phospholipase C and DAG in host cells → apoptosis, disrupts barrier integrity and allows for entry into subepithelial tissues 
 - Lipooligosaccharide (LOS) = Possessing potent endotoxic activity, LOS undergoes significant phase variation; sialylation of LOS can inhibit both the classical and alternative complement pathways, facilitating immune evasion
- Iron/Zinc Acquisition = expresses specialized proteins (Tbp1/2, LbpA/B, HpuA/B) to scavenge essential metals from host proteins like transferrin and lactoferrin
 - In those who don't have terminal complement components, gonococcal and meningococcal infections are common 
 - Gonorrhea is an independent risk factor for HIV transmission and acquisition, increasing viral shedding in the genital tract and recruiting susceptible CD4+ T cells and dendritic cells to mucosal sites
 - In Men = Primarily manifests as acute urethritis with purulent discharge and dysuria 
	 - While most are symptomatic, an asymptomatic reservoir exists
- In Women = Cervicitis is the most common presentation, involving the columnar epithelium of the cervical os 
	- Symptoms include mucopurulent discharge and dysuria
	- Complications include pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), which carries a risk of fetal loss in pregnancy
- Extragenital Sites
	- Anorectal = Often asymptomatic but can cause proctitis; common in men who have sex with men (MSM) and women with cervicitis
	- Pharyngeal = Usually mild or asymptomatic; acquisition is via oral-genital contact
	- Ocular = Can result in severe conjunctivitis with potential for corneal perforation in adults (via autoinoculation) and neonates (ophthalmia neonatorum)
### DGI
- Disseminated Gonococcal Infection (DGI) = occurs in 0.5–3% of untreated cases, often involving strains that resist serum bactericidal activity
- Bacteremic Stage = Characterized by fever, chills, polyarthralgias, and characteristic skin lesions (papules, pustules, or hemorrhagic lesions) on the extremities
- Joint-Localized Stage = Presents as suppurative arthritis, most frequently in the knees, wrists, and ankles
- Rare Complications = Include endocarditis and meningitis
### AMR
- Sulfonamides, penicillins, and tetracyclines are no longer reliable due to chromosomal mutations and plasmid-acquired resistance (eg, PPNG, TRNG)
- Fluoroquinolones = Quinolone-resistant N gonorrhoeae (QRNG) is now widespread globally due to alterations in DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV
- Cephalosporins = Rising MICs for cefixime led to its removal as a first-line agent
	- Ceftriaxone resistance is emerging through mutations in the penA allele, the mtrR efflux regulator, and penB porin
## Chlamydiae
![](00%20media/Pasted%20image%2020260312042300.png)

- Chlamydial life cycle involves two specialized morphologic forms
	- Elementary Body (EB) = The infectious, metabolically inactive form adapted for extracellular survival It enters the host cell via receptor-mediated endocytosis
	- Reticulate Body (RB) = The non-infectious, metabolically active, replicating form adapted for the intracellular environment
- Within the host cell, chlamydiae reside in a membrane-bound inclusion and prevent phagosome-lysosome fusion 
- EBs reorganize into RBs, which divide by binary fission for approximately 18–24 hours before condensing back into EBs for release and subsequent infection of adjacent cells 
- Exposure to interferon-gamma (IFN-$\gamma$) can induce a state of persistence characterized by large, aberrant RBs
- Pathology mainly driven by immunopathologic mechanisms or nonspecific host responses rather than direct toxic effects of the organism
- Chlamydial heat-shock protein may sensitize the host; repeated infections often lead to late-stage complications such as [Pelvic Inflammatory Disease](13%20Reproduction/Reproductive%20Pathologies/Pelvic%20Inflammatory%20Disease.md), [Ectopic Pregnancy](13%20Reproduction/Reproductive%20Pathologies/Ectopic%20Pregnancy.md), and blinding trachoma
	- Blinding trachoma = chronic infection leads to conjunctival scarring, which causes the eyelids to turn inward (trichiasis), resulting in corneal abrasion, ulceration, and eventual opacification
- Men = C trachomatis is the primary cause of nongonococcal urethritis (NGU) and postgonococcal urethritis (PGU), implicated in 30–50% of NGU cases
	- Complications include acute epididymitis (most common in men <35 years) and reactive arthritis
- Women = Infection is frequently asymptomatic but may present as mucopurulent cervicitis, characterized by endocervical discharge and friability
	- Ascending infection leads to PID (salpingitis, endometritis), which can cause tubal factor infertility and chronic pelvic pain
	- Perihepatitis (Fitz-Hugh–Curtis syndrome)
	- Reactive Arthritis = sterile inflammatory arthritis (formerly Reiter's syndrome) that may follow NGU, often associated with the HLA-B27 phenotype
		- Can't see, can't pee, can't bend the knee 
- Lymphogranuloma venereum (LGV) = LGV is a more invasive systemic STI caused by serovars L1–L3 It typically presents in two forms
	- Inguinal Syndrome = Painful, matted inguinal lymphadenopathy (buboes) with the "groove sign" appearing 2–6 weeks after a transient primary lesion
	- Proctocolitis = Increasingly common among men who have sex with men (MSM), presenting with anorectal pain, bloody discharge, and tenesmus, often mimicking [Inflammatory Bowel Disease](10%20Gastroenterology/GI%20Pathologies/Inflammatory%20Bowel%20Disease.md), Crohn's disease histologically

## HPV
- Low-risk types = Such as HPV 6 and 11, which primarily cause genital warts and approximately 10% of low-grade cervical lesions
- High-risk types = Such as HPV 16 and 18, which are associated with high-grade dysplastic lesions and a significant percentage of invasive cervical cancers
- HPV exhibits strict tissue tropism, targeting basal keratinocytes of specific anatomic sites 
- Viral entry typically occurs through microtrauma that exposes these basal cells
- Intrinsically linked to keratinocyte differentiation; virions assemble in the nuclei of differentiated cells and are transmitted via contact with these desquamated cells or free virus 
- Lacks a viremic phase (period during a viral infection when viruses are present and actively circulating in the bloodstream), which often results in incomplete Ab response
- While neutralizing antibodies provide type-specific protection, the cell-mediated immune response, particularly CD4+ T cell regulation, is the primary mechanism for controlling infection progression 
- In advanced malignancies, the virus may suppress antigen presentation and antitumor activity
- Persistent infection with oncogenic types is the most significant risk factor for malignancy; the transition from initial infection to invasive cancer may exceed 20 years
- Genital Warts (Condyloma Acuminata) = Primarily caused by types 6 and 11; appear as soft, sessile, or papillary growths
- Cervical Disease = Often subclinical; advanced carcinomas may present as eroded, bleeding, or exophytic masses
- Anal Cancer = Often presents with rectal bleeding, pain, or a mass sensation, though 20% are asymptomatic at diagnosis
### HPV-associated malignancies
- HPV is responsible for nearly 100% of cervical cancers, the second most common cancer in women globally
- Cervical, anal, oropharyngeal cancers → HPV 16 and 18 → also vulvar, vaginal, penile cancers
- Other Sites = HPV is implicated in 50–70% of vulvar and vaginal cancers and 50% of penile cancers
### HIV co-infection
- Higher rates of persistent infection, recalcitrant genital warts, and precancerous lesions 
- Low CD4+ counts and increased cervical cancer risk
- ART may reduce the likelihood of new infections, it has not significantly decreased the incidence of cervical or anal cancer in this population, likely due to the persistence of oncogenic HPV acquired prior to ART initiation 
### Treatment
- Bivalent (Cervarix) = Targets types 16 and 18; no longer marketed in the US
- Quadrivalent (Gardasil) = Targets types 6, 11, 16, and 18; no longer available in the US but used globally
- Nine-valent (Gardasil-9) = Targets types 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58, estimated to protect against 90% of HPV-associated squamous cell cancers
- Routine vaccination at age 11 or 12, with "catch-up" vaccination through age 26 
- For adults aged 27–45, shared clinical decision-making is recommended, as the population-level benefit is lower due to prior exposure
- In patients with HIV, a three-dose series is recommended for those aged 11–26
- Topical = Imiquimod cream (immunomodulator); effective for external genital warts but not mucosal lesions
- Physical Destruction = Cryotherapy (liquid nitrogen) is the safest and least expensive first-line option for genital warts
	- Laser therapy and surgical excision are highly effective for exophytic lesions and cervical dysplasia
- Intralesional = Interferon-α may be used for recalcitrant cases but is costly and associated with systemic side effects
- Future Directions = Therapeutic vaccines targeting E6 and E7 oncoproteins are in development to enhance cell-mediated responses against established cancers, though none are currently available for clinical use

# Specific conditions
## Syphilis
- Caused by the bacterium _Treponema pallidum_, transmitted through direct contact with sores during oral, anal, or vaginal sex
- Reverse sequence syphilis screening protocol → interpretation of results
	- Nonreactive Treponemal & Non-treponemal Tests = Indicates either an absence of syphilis or very early infection before seroconversion has occurred
	- Reactive Treponemal Test + Reactive VDRL/RPR = Confirms untreated or prior treated syphilis
	- Discordant Results = If the initial treponemal test is reactive but the VDRL/RPR is nonreactive, a TP-PA (Treponema pallidum particle agglutination) test is utilized for confirmation
## Surgical site infections (SSI)
- Superficial Incisional = Involving only the skin and subcutaneous tissue
- Deep Incisional = Extending to the deep soft tissue, including fascia and muscle
- Organ/Space = Infections involving any part of the anatomy opened or manipulated during surgery, such as an infected pelvic hematoma
- Vulvar Abscesses = Microbiological cultures often reveal Staphylococcus, group B Streptococcus, Enterococcus species, E coli, and P mirabilis
- Clinical Alert = Notably, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) has been identified in 40 to 60 percent of cultured vulvar abscesses
- Risk Factors = Hysterectomy increases SSI risk due to greater bacterial exposure during the procedure
## Bacterial vaginosis
- Clinical Presentation = Patients often report an "off" odour, which characteristically increases following intercourse or during menses due to the alkalinity of seminal fluid and blood
- Pathophysiology & Diagnostics = The diagnosis is often supported by the Whiff Test, where 10-percent potassium hydroxide (KOH) is added to vaginal secretions, releasing volatile amines that produce a fishy odour
- Pathognomonic Finding = The simultaneous presence of clue cells (squamous epithelial cells heavily studded with bacteria, blurring cell borders) on a saline wet preparation and a positive Whiff test is considered pathognomonic for BV, even in asymptomatic individuals
- Key Metrics = Vaginal pH > 45; discharge is thin, gray or white, and adherent
- Check Ansel criteria
## Vulvovaginal infections 
- Cervical infection with N. gonorrhoeae or C. trachomatis does not often cause an increased amount or abnormal odor of discharge
	- When these cause cervicitis, ↑ neutrophils in vaginal fluid, yellow color
- Vulvovaginal Candidiasis
	- Symptoms = Intense itching and burning with a white, "curdy" discharge
	- Diagnostics = Vaginal pH remains < 45 Microscopic examination of a 10-percent KOH wet prep reveals hyphae and buds
- Trichomoniasis 
	- Clinical Features = Notable for a frothy discharge, foul odour, dysuria, pruritus, and occasional spotting

## Urethritis in men
- Discharge, dysuria, without frequency of urination
- N. gonorrhea, C. trachomatis, Mycoplasma genitalium, Ureaplasma urealyticum, Trichomonas vaginalis, HSV, and (rarely) adenovirus
- Differentials include disseminated gonococcal infection and reactive arthritis 
- IM ceftriaxone
## Epididymitis
- Unilateral, pain, swelling, tenderness of epididymis → usually C. trachomatis 
- Differentials include torsion, tumor, trauma 
- IM ceftriaxone, followed by oral doxycycline 
# Investigations
## Risk assessment
- Framing statement, screening questions, STD history, sexual preferences and practices, injection drug use, characteristics of partners, symptom checklist
- Males = discharge of pus, genital sores or rash
- Females = abnormal vaginal discharge, genital sores, rash, itching 
- Ask if they would like to get tested for HIV and any other infection 
## Diagnostic testing
- pH of vaginal fluids
- Gram's strain of urethral discharge
- Culture = remains the gold standard for antimicrobial susceptibility testing; requires selective media (eg, Thayer-Martin) and immediate incubation in CO₂-enriched environments
- Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests (NAATs) = preferred for screening due to high sensitivity and the ability to use non-invasive samples like urine or self-collected vaginal swabs

# Treatment 
- Pre-exposure prophylaxis, early diagnosis, safe sex practices 
- DGI = Requires initial parenteral therapy with Ceftriaxone (1 g IV/IM every 24 hours), with a transition to oral agents only after clinical improvement and documented susceptibility
- Special populations
	- Neonates = Ophthalmia neonatorum is prevented with erythromycin ointment but treated with systemic ceftriaxone
		- Acute conjunctivitis with significant eye discharge, swelling, and potential vision loss (corneal scarring/blindness) occurring within the first 4 weeks of life → untreated maternal infection transmitted at birth 
	- Contacts = Prophylaxis for meningococcal contacts (often discussed alongside gonococcal management) utilizes rifampin, ceftriaxone, or ciprofloxacin to eradicate carriage
		- In documented meningococcal outbreaks, immunization with MenACWY or MenB vaccines is recommended
