- Germinal Stage (Week 1-2): Starts with fertilization, followed by cleavage and the formation of a blastocyst. The blastocyst implants into the uterine lining around days 5-6
- Gastrulation (Week 3): This crucial step establishes the three primary germ layers—the ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm—which are responsible for creating different tissues
- Organogenesis (Weeks 3-8): The embryo develops specific structures. By the end of this period, the heart begins to beat, and limbs start forming
- Embryonic Period End: By the end of week 8, the major organs are in place, and the embryo becomes a **fetus**
	- First 8 weeks, the fetus is most vulnerable to [[Teratogens]]
- Fetal period = most adult structures establishes, organs and structures grow
- See [Pregnancy](Reproduction/Pregnancy.md)

## Key steps
- Fertilization from haploid spermatozoon (1N, 1C) and ovum (1N, 1C) (ampullary region of fallopian tube) → prezygote (2N, 2C) → 2 cell zygote (underwent cleavage) → 8 cell zygote → morula (16+ cells, moving to uterus) → preimplantation blastocyst → implanted blastocyst (uterine wall, usually near the fundus)
- Maternal and paternal DNA start separate in the pronucleus
- So upon fertilization, the zygote will be 2N, 2C, but it immediately undergoes DNA synthesis and makes chromatic copies so it turns into 2N, 4C
- Once the zygote divides into two cells, it once again becomes 2N, 2C

For issues with fertility, see [Infertility](Reproduction/Reproductive%20Pathologies/Infertility.md). 

- Contact between spermatozoa and oocyte membrane 
- Path that it takes = corona radiata → zona pellucida → plasma membrane of secondary oocyte → cytoplasm of secondary oocyte 
- Germ cell membranes fuse and stop moving, sperm nucleus enters
- Three events that occur from ↑ Ca++ in oocyte
	- Membrane depolarization = primary block to polyspermy = prevents additional spermatozoa from fusing
	- Cortical reaction = cortical granules beneath membrane causes zona pellucida to harden = secondary block to polyspermy
	- Resumption of the second meiotic division of the egg, second polar body extruded, female pronucleus ends up haploid 
- Mitochondrial DNA (maternal) is absolutely important here, sperm mitochondria is instantly tagged with ubiquitin → high mtDNA is a signal for  
- Another mechanism to prevent polyspermy = zinc released, inhibits acrosome of other sperm → can also be used for quality control screening in IVF 
## Blastulation
- Blastula has a fluid-filled cavity called blastocoel
- Blastocyst contains trophectoderm (forms the placenta) and inner cell mass (fetus)

![400](00%20media/Pasted%20image%2020260311173957.png)

- Trophectodermal cells differentiate to trophoblast, make contact with uterine luminal epithelial cells 
- Surface epithelium becomes eroded, nearby cells lyse
- Endometrium undergoes many changes → decidualization starts at attachment, continues in a concentric wave from point of implantation → entire implant will eventually be buried inside the endometrium 
- Trophoblast turns to inner cytotrophoblast and outer syncytiotrophoblast
	- Syncytiotrophoblast = continuous, multinucleated, active in placental hormone secretion and nutrient transport 
	- Cytotrophoblast cells = invasive properties, invades endometrial stroma to reach maternal blood vessels → invasion and remodeling of spiral arteries by extravillous cytotrophoblast cells is key 

See [[Placenta]]. 
## Implantation
- Implants around day 6-10
- Successful implantation requires  = leukemia inhibitory factor, integrins, and TGF B
- Ovarian corpus luteum needs to continue to secrete progesterone to allow for survival longer than 14 days after ovulation 
- Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) = produced by trophoblast, into maternal bloodstream, acts like LH and inhibits luteal regression 
## Gastrulation
- Blastula turned to gastrula → ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm
- Inner cell mass turns into bilaminar disc, which is two cell layers separated by a basement membrane → called epiblast (becomes embryo) and hypoblast (yolk sac)

![400](00%20media/Pasted%20image%2020260311174222.png)
- Primitive streak = formed from invagination of epiblast cells → indicates start of gastrulation
# Germ layers
## Ectoderm
- Epidermis (nail beds, hair), nervous system 
![200](00%20media/Pasted%20image%2020260311174552.png)

- Notochord comes from the mesoderm, and it influences the ectoderm → make the ectoderm form neural crest and neural plate, which folds to become tube
- Becomes the neural tube and crest, which becomes the nervous system 
- Adult remnant = nucleus pulposus of the spine, which is made of mesoderm 
- Neural tube: CNS
	- CNS neurons, oligodendrocytes, astrocytes
	- Retina
	- Spinal cord
- Neural crest: PNS
	- Cranial nerves
	- Dorsal root ganglia
	- Autonomic ganglia
	- Schwann cells
	- Meninges
- Microglia (phagocytes): Mesoderm
## Mesoderm
- Muscle, bone, connective tissue
- Cardiovascular structures
	- Week 4 is when the heart starts to beat, can be seen through transvaginal ultrasound 
- Kidneys
- Lymphatics
- Blood
#### Mesenchyme
- Embryonic connective tissue, not in adults except for the mesenchymal stem cells
- Mostly from mesoderm 
- Gives rise to most connective tissue 
- Mesenchymal tumors = sarcomas 
## Endoderm 
- GI epithelium and derivatives
- Liver, gallbladder, pancreas
- Alveoli, epithelium of trachea/bronchi
	- Airway cartilage from mesoderm
# Development of body pattern
- Limbs develop along three planes, all based on anatomic position
- Proximal to distal is your limbs growing from body wall to tip
- Anterior-posterior is head to tail, defined by primitive streak
- Dorsal-ventral = back to belly axis
- There is also medial-lateral = midline or side 
## Proximal to distal
- Apical Ectodermal Ridge = critical for proximal to distal development
- Ectoderm overlying mesoderm, area of limb bud formation
- Removal here = limb stops growing
- Continuous signaling occurring between mesoderm and growing cells of the ectoderm
- “Progress zone” forms in mesoderm with growing cells
- Mesoderm also influences ectodermal ridge
- Key transcription factor for communication = Fibroblast Growth Factor
- Ridge removed, replaced with FGF: Normal growth
## Dorsal-ventral axis
- Depends on multiple genes
	- Radial fringe (dorsal)
	- Engrailed1 (ventral)
	- SER2 (border)
	- Wnt-7a = activates LMX-1 to dorsalize mesoderm, deletion here will lead to two ventral sides to the limb 
		- On the ventral side = engrailes1 represses Wnt-7
## Anterior-posterior axis
- Depends on zone of polarizing activity, influences AER → found on the posterior area of the limb 
- Major signaling molecule here is SHH 
# Key embryonic genes 
## Sonic Hedgehog
- Coded for by SHH Gene = embryonic signaling protein, many embryonic roles: limbs, brain, eyes
- Key roles = CNS development, limb development and patterning
- Formation of forebrain, where signaling separates right and left brain, establishes the midline
- Mutations in this gene lead to holoprosencephaly = no left and right halves of the brain, failure of cleavage of prosencephalon → facial abnormalities like cleft lip/palate and cyclopia
## Fibroblast growth factor
- Important for normal limb growth, along proximal-distal axis 
## Wnt-7a
- Originally found in Drosophila, Winged integration gene
- Early embryogenesis, acts as regulator of dorsal-ventral axis; later embryogenesis, causes anteroposterior axis development 
## Homeobox genes
- HOX genes
- Code for transcription factors
- Regulators of AP axis development
- Homeotic genes
	- Homeosis = transformation of one structure into another
	- Homeotic genes = lead to formation of body segments
	- Mutation → abnormal body part formation
- All homeotic genes have same sequences ~180 bases
	- Called the Homeobox (part of gene)
- Rare mutations of some HOX genes described
	- Most result in abnormal limb formations
	- Fruit flies: legs grow from head instead of antenna!
		- Polydactyly (extra fingers/toes)
		- Syndactyly (fused fingers/toes)

# Uhh heme and other stuff
- Erythropoiesis = This process is primarily controlled by fetal erythropoietin because maternal erythropoietin does not cross the placenta
- Fetal Hemoglobin (HbF) = HbF is the main type of hemoglobin during fetal life It has a higher oxygen affinity than adult hemoglobin (HbA1) because it binds 2,3-diphosphoglycerate (2,3-DPG) less avidly, allowing the fetus to effectively extract oxygen from maternal blood
- Coagulation Factors = The fetus begins producing adult-type coagulation factors by 12 weeks, though their concentrations at birth are typically only about 50% of adult levels
- Respiratory System = Surfactant, composed mainly of glycerophospholipids like dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine, is essential for lung maturity and preventing respiratory distress syndrome
- Gastrointestinal System = Fetal swallowing of amnionic fluid begins early; by term, a fetus swallows between 200 and 760 mL per day, helping to regulate amnionic fluid volume
- Hepatic System = The liver is a key site for hemopoiesis early in gestation and slowly develops the capacity to conjugate bilirubin
- Urinary System = The permanent kidney, or metanephros, forms between weeks 9 and 12, and glomerular filtration begins by week 9
- - Pituitary Gland = By 17 weeks, the fetal pituitary synthesizes and stores all major pituitary hormones
- Thyroid Gland = Maternal thyroid hormones do not cross the placenta in significant amounts because the placenta rapidly deiodinates them Testing newborns for TSH levels at birth is standard to prevent cretinism
- Adrenal Glands = These are 10 to 20 times larger than adult glands relative to body size, primarily due to a large fetal zone that involutes rapidly after birth
- - Glucose = This is the major nutrient for fetal growth and is transported via facilitated diffusion across the placenta using GLUT carrier proteins
- Leptin = Produced by the mother, fetus, and placenta, this hormone regulates energy homeostasis and contributes to functions like angiogenesis and pulmonary maturation
- Passive Immunity = IgG is the only major immunoglobulin class that crosses the placenta in large amounts, providing the fetus with maternal antibodies

## Pharyngeal arches
![](00%20media/Pasted%20image%2020260311201923.png)
- Core of mesenchyme (connective tissue, mesoderm)
	- Gives rise to cartilage/bone and muscles
- Neural crest cells migrate to center, those are what make the nerve
	- Gives rise to cranial nerves
- Artery → forms aortic arches
- Go down from the chest to above the heart
### First pharyngeal arch
- Bones of the face and jaw
	- “Maxillary process”
		- Maxilla
		- Zygomatic bone
	- “Mandibular process”
		- Mandible
		- Meckel’s cartilage (does not persist in the adult) → incus and malleus
- Muscles
	- Muscles of mastication = temporalis, masseter, pterygoids
	- Anterior digastric
	- Mylohyoid
	- Tensor tympani (ear)
- Trigeminal Nerve
	- Trigeminal mandibular and maxillary divisions
	- Sensory to face
	- Motor = muscles of mastication
- Aortic arch = portion of maxillary artery
### Second pharyngeal arch
- Bones
	- “Reichert’s cartilage”
	- Stapes (ear)
	- Styloid process of temporal bone
	- Lesser horn of hyoid
- Muscles
	- Stapedius (ear) → smallest muscle of human body 
	- Auricular muscles (ear)
	- Stylohyoid
	- Posterior digastric
	- Muscles of facial expression 
- Facial nerve
- Arteries
	- Stapedial artery = embryonic vessel, usually involutes in development
	- Hyoid artery = embryonic vessel, develops into small branch of internal carotid
### Third pharyngeal arch
- Cartilage/Bones
	- Hyoid bone = body and greater horn
- Stylopharyngeus
- Glossopharyngeal nerve (IX)
- Arteries = common carotid, proximal internal carotid
### Fourth and sixth arches
- Fifth arch does not persist in humans
- 4th/6th: both innervated by vagus nerve branches
	- 4th: superior laryngeal
	- 6th: recurrent laryngeal
- Both arches fuse to form larynx cartilage (thyroid, cricoid, arytenoid, corniculate, cuneiform)
- Laryngeal muscles
	- 4th Arch
		- Cricothyroid
		- Levator palatini
		- Pharyngeal constrictors
	- 6th Arch
		- Intrinsic muscles of larynx
		- (except cricothyroid)
- Arteries
	- 4th Arch
		- Left: aortic arch
		- Right: proximal right subclavian artery
	- 6th Arch (“pulmonary arch”)
		- Left: proximal pulmonary artery
		- Left: ductus arteriosus
		- Right: proximal pulmonary artery
### Abnormal development
- Treacher Collins Syndrome = first and second arch syndrome, failure of neural crest cell migration
	- Underdeveloped facial bones
		- Small mandible (mandibular hypoplasia)
		- Small jaw (micrognathia)
		- Absent/small ears
		- Glossoptosis (retraction of tongue)
	- May lead to difficulty breathing due to underdeveloped lower jaw and obstruction of airway by tongue
### Tongue
- Anterior two thirds: 1st and 2nd arches
	- Lingual swellings and tuberculum impar (an embryonic swelling appearing in the midline floor of the pharynx during the fourth week of development, initial swelling of tongue development)
	- Sensation: CN V (1st arch)
	- Taste: CN VII (2nd arch)
- Posterior third: 3rd and 4th arches
	- Sensory: GP Nerve (IX) of 3rd arch
	- Some posterior taste via CN X (4th arch)
- Motor:
	- Hypoglossal (XII)
	- One exception: palatoglossus (CN X)

# Errors in morphogenesis
- Intrinsic
	- Failure of embryo to develop
	- Abnormal genes or other internal processes
	- Agenesis = missing organ because no embryonic tissue
	- Aplasia = growth failure of embryonic tissue 
	- Hypoplasia = incomplete development, like in microcephaly
	- Malformation = abnormal development → eg neural tube defects, congenital heart defects (like [Ventricular Septal Defect](Cardiology/Cardiac%20Pathologies/Congenital%20Diseases/Ventricular%20Septal%20Defect.md))
- Extrinsic
	- External force impacts normal development
	- Disruption = arrested by external force
		- Example is amniotic band syndrome = fetal structures entrapped by fibrous bands in utero, affecting limbs or digits
	- Deformation = abnormal growth, not arrest
		- Like [[Medical Genetics/Potter's Syndrome]]
