- Point Mutations: A small, single nucleotide change can alter the amino acid sequence of a protein, making it hyperactive or unable to turn off
	- Example: The RAS gene family, where mutations (e.g., codons 12, 13, 61) impair GTPase activity, keeping the protein constantly active
- Gene Amplification: Multiple copies of a gene are created, leading to excessive protein production
	- Example: HER2 (breast cancer) or MYC (neuroblastoma) amplification
- Chromosomal Translocation: A gene is moved to a new chromosomal location, often placing it under the control of a highly active promoter
	- Example: The Philadelphia chromosome in Chronic Myelogenous Leukemia (CML)
- Chromosomal Rearrangements/Fusion: Translocations or inversions can create new fusion proteins that are constitutively active
	- Insertion Mutation (Retroviral Insertion): a virus inserts its own genetic material near a proto-oncogene, activating it
- Epigenetic Alterations: Changes in DNA methylation or histone modification that turn up expression
